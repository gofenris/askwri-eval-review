# Executive summary

## Introduction

**The state of Rio Grande do Sul faced, between the end of April and the beginning of May 2024, the largest extreme hydrometeorological event ever recorded in Brazil, both for its territorial extension and number of people affected.** Intense and prolonged rainfall triggered a series of floods and landslides that deeply impacted the population, infrastructure, and the state economy. Of the 497 municipalities in Rio Grande do Sul, 418 declared a State of Emergency or State of Public Calamity, and 2.4 million people have been affected (State of Rio Grande do Sul, 2024a; State of Rio Grande do Sul, 2024b). Rainfall volumes exceeded 500 millimeters in the first 13 days of May 2024 in some of the state's municipalities (Inmet, 2025).

**The event was an unprecedented climate, socio-environmental and multi-regional disaster, record-breaking at both the state and national levels, that tested the limits of institutional capacity at the municipal and state scale.** It also exposed weaknesses in integrated planning and governance, especially at the intermunicipal and regional levels, reflected in the chain reaction and interdependence of impacts across the state's various regions. The creation of the Brazilian government's Extraordinary Secretariat in Support of the Reconstruction of Rio Grande do Sul (Brasil, 2024) highlighted the magnitude of the disaster and mobilized all levels of Brazilian governance.

## About this study

**Given the systemic complexity of the disaster—which cannot be explained solely by the occurrence of extreme events, but rather by their interaction with pre-existing conditions of exposure and vulnerability—a multisectoral analysis was conducted to identify its root causes.** These comprise the social, economic, political, institutional and environmental conditions that have historically perpetuated vulnerabilities and shaped risk (Oliver-Smith et al., 2016). To this end, the Forensic Investigation of Disasters (FORIN) approach was used (Oliver-Smith et al., 2016; IRDR, 2011; UNDRR, 2021; Ferreira, 2023; Ferreira et al., 2023; Ferreira; Marchezini; Mendes, 2025), associated with the Retrospective Longitudinal Analysis (RLA) method. The FORIN approach suggests that disasters are the result of social and historical processes and that the magnitude of loss and damage is largely influenced by human decisions and actions in the presence of

potential disaster risks, including the choice to ignore them or minimize their importance (Wisner; Gaillard; Kelman, 2012; Wisner et al., 2025). The RLA method starts from the identification of loss and damage and retrospectively progresses toward the factors that influence these choices and drive risk (Oliver-Smith et al., 2016).

**To build a multilevel causal chain of the disaster risk, analyses were conducted with data generated by two workshops with 30 local experts from different fields of knowledge, questionnaires applied to another 15 people, and interviews with three representatives of the public sector and civil society.** The analysis started with a descriptive stage of the immediate impacts of the disaster and advanced to the three levels of risk production proposed by the FORIN methodology: the unsafe conditions observed, the dynamic pressures that produce and reinforce these unsafe conditions over time, and the root causes.

**This publication aims to present and discuss the results of the analysis, offering a qualitative understanding of the structural and intermediate elements that shape and reinforce vulnerability over time within the causal chain of disaster risk.** The pathway back to the root causes allows the identification of factors that cumulatively and systemically influence the construction of risk, contributing to intersectoral decisions and public policies that strengthen security and promote disaster risk reduction over time.

## Main results and conclusions

**The results demonstrate that the disaster, although triggered by an extreme hydrometeorological event, was the product of a historical process of risk construction.** Social, economic and governance factors were central drivers of the outbreak and amplification of the impacts of heavy rainfall. These factors, common to other climate disaster scenarios, highlight that the role of human and institutional decisions in the production and social dissemination of risk over time is as significant as the occurrence of the physical climate event itself.

**The contrast between the scale of the disaster and the intergovernmental coordination structure (involving federal, state, and municipal governments) was evident.** Although all the affected municipalities had local Civil Defenses and contingency plans, the magnitude of the event revealed that such administrative structures, when isolated, are insufficient in face of multi-regional disasters, pointing to the need for supra-municipal planning and governance arrangements, with a special focus on river basin scale.

Low social memory, underestimation of alerts, and climate change denial reveal sociocultural dimensions that hinder preventive action and response capacity, aggravating the impacts. On the other hand, transparent public communication that rapidly reaches the population show potential to strengthen community mobilization and confidence in the alerts.

**Understanding the neglect of climate justice and adaptation with a focus on protecting vulnerable groups is key to understanding the disaster and preventing new occurrences.** Low-income communities, informal workers, smallholders and traditional groups were disproportionately affected, highlighting the direct consequence of overlapping vulnerabilities. The lack of visibility of climate justice, expressed in the root cause of socioeconomic inequality and wealth concentration, perpetuates the social production of risk in uncontrolled urban sprawl processes, in the unmet demand for safe housing, and in the absence of integration between adaptation policies, social protection and territorial planning.

**More important than breaking down and analyzing a past disaster, this research supports decisions that shape risk in the present.** By adopting a multisectoral approach, it reinforces that adaptation and climate justice are development agendas and should inform land use policies, public investment and social protection efforts. The challenge therefore arises to transform the identified gaps into coordinated action, strengthening regional governance and integrating risk reduction, equity and long-term planning.

## Introduction

Between the end of April and the beginning of May 2024 (Figure 1), the state of Rio Grande do Sul (RS) faced the largest extreme hydrometeorological event ever recorded in Brazil, both for its territorial extension and number of people affected. Intense and prolonged rainfall triggered a series of floods and landslides that deeply impacted the population, infrastructure, and the state economy. Of the 497 municipalities in Rio Grande do Sul, 418 declared a State of Emergency or State of Public Calamity (State of Rio Grande do Sul, 2024a). Rainfall volumes exceeded 500 millimeters in the first 13 days of May 2024 in some of the state’s municipalities (Inmet, 2025).

According to the State Civil Defense, as of August 20, 2024, approximately 2.4 million people have been affected, including 183 deaths, 27 people reported missing, 806 injured, and more than 660,000 displaced or homeless (State of Rio Grande do Sul, 2024b). A total of 980 temporary shelters were established across 117 municipalities, managed by government agencies, organized civil society, or volunteers, accommodating 81,170 people unable to return to their homes (Suarez, Bello, and Campbell, 2024).

The floods reached urban and rural areas, causing damage to 11,572 kilometers of roads (Suarez; Bello; Campbell, 2024), 1,247 public facilities and 9,837 agricultural establishments (Ipea, 2024). Approximately 14,240 km² of productive areas are estimated to have suffered direct losses (Ipea, 2024). A survey jointly conducted by the Inter-American Development Bank (IDB), the World Bank and the Economic Commission for Latin America and the Caribbean (ECLAC) estimated total losses of R$ 88.9 billion (Suarez; Bello; Campbell, 2024).

These numbers represent the magnitude of an unprecedented climate, socio-environmental and multi-regional disaster in Brazil, with few parallels in the world. The event tested the limits of municipal and state institutional capacity and led to the creation of the federal government’s Extraordinary Secretariat in Support of the Reconstruction of Rio Grande do Sul, on May 15, 2024 (Brasil, 2024), highlighting the magnitude of the disaster and mobilizing all levels of Brazilian governance.

## Methodology

The methodological choice was based on an exploratory literature review on different approaches to investigating the root causes of disasters. Three potential approaches related to the objectives of the study were identified: (1) Forensic Investigation of Disasters (FORIN) (Oliver-Smith et al., 2016), (2) the analysis frameworks of the German Committee for Disaster Reduction (DKKV, 2012) and (3) the frameworks of the Preparing for Extreme and Rare Events in Coastal Regions (Pearl) project (Fraser; Paterson; Pelling, 2016). While the DKKV and Pearl approaches are focused on risk management at the regulatory and institutional levels and preparation for specific extreme events, FORIN offers a broader perspective, integrating physical, social, institutional and historical aspects in disaster analysis. This approach proposes the critical reconstruction of the causal chain and structural processes of risk production, better supporting the objectives of this study.

FORIN suggests that disasters are the result of social and historical processes and, as such, the magnitude of loss and damage is largely influenced by human decisions and actions in the presence of potential disaster risks (Oliver-Smith et al.,

2016; IRDR, 2011; UNDRR, 2021; Ferreira, 2023; Ferreira et al., 2023; Ferreira; Marchezini; Mendes, 2025), including the choice to ignore them or minimize their importance (Wisner; Gaillard; Kelman, 2012; Wisner et al., 2025). The approach is developed around a multilevel causal chain based on the progression of vulnerability model (Blaikie et al., 1994; Wisner et al., 2004; Wisner; Gaillard; Kelman, 2012; Wisner et al., 2025).

Three main levels are proposed for the construction of risk (Figure 2): (1) unsafe conditions, understood as immediate situations that increase the vulnerability of people and infrastructure; (2) dynamic pressures or risk drivers \( ^{1} \) , corresponding to the forces that, in the short and medium term, create or aggravate these conditions, such as uncontrolled urban sprawl and poor governance; and (3) root causes, i.e., deep structural factors, such as socioeconomic inequalities and development policies that have historically perpetuated vulnerabilities and shaped the production of risk (Oliver-Smith et al., 2016). Threats, which can trigger disasters according to their magnitude and duration, represent conjunctural inducing events that intensify the conditions for the progression of vulnerability.

The study adopted the Retrospective Longitudinal Analysis (RLA) method (Oliver-Smith et al., 2016) to develop the causal progression of vulnerability model. Focused on the reconstruction of the risk chain, the RLA allows the analysis of structural (root causes) and intermediary elements (dynamic pressures and unsafe conditions), enabling the identification of target areas for public policies to improve security and reduce disaster risk over time.

The RLA starts with the identification of the immediate impacts caused by the disaster and moves backwards, examining the processes and social, environmental, normative, and institutional conditions that drive the risks (Oliver-Smith et al., 2016). Thus, in this study, the process of tracing back to root causes originated from a descriptive analysis of the immediate impacts of the disaster, advancing to the unsafe conditions and dynamic pressures, and finally arriving at the structural (root) causes.

## Definition of the study's delimitations

While the 2024 disaster impacted the entire state of Rio Grande do Sul, a more in-depth analysis required a focus on the areas influenced by the Guaíba Basin – the main hydrographic region affected by heavy rainfall in the state. In view of this, the territorial delimitation of the research reflected the territorial arrangement of the Regional Coordinating Bodies of Civil Protection and Defense (Crepdec)², based on which the state Civil Defense actions are planned.

The four Crepdec with the largest impacts were then selected, considering human losses, material damage and recurrence of extreme events, as well as the type of hydrometeorological threats faced. ## Data collection and analysis

Data collection and analysis were carried out in two stages: the descriptive stage where a mapping exercise of the disaster's triggering events, immediate impacts and unsafe conditions was conducted, followed by the analytical stage, where participants built on the critical interpretation of the descriptive level and explored the dynamic pressures and root causes of the disaster. The first stage included (1) a

collaborative workshop, conducted as a focus group, with 20 experts and public sector representatives, (2) the application of an online questionnaire with 15 respondents who were unable to attend the workshop and (3) three semi-structured interviews with key actors. A second workshop was held in the second stage, also conducted as a focus group, with 10 experts (Figure 4). The data collection period ran from September 19, 2024, the date of the first in-person workshop, to November 12, 2024, when the second in-person workshop was held.

The online questionnaire resulted from the adaptation of the questions used in the first collaborative workshop for application in digital format, aimed at complementing the descriptive level of the research and expanding regional capillarity through the participation of local experts and civil society representatives who were unable to attend the in-person workshops in Porto Alegre. The three semi-structured interviews allowed a closer examination of aspects related to multilevel and interinstitutional governance in the prevention, mitigation, preparedness and response to disasters, as well as the perception of civil society (details in Appendix B).

### Environmental impacts

Floods and mass movements caused the loss of riparian forests and slope vegetation (Figure 11), in addition to the removal or deposition of land and sediments. River silting represents an impact that creates a feedback loop, as it increases the susceptibility to floods, flash floods and inundations by reducing the hydraulic portion of the water body, and is aggravated by the recurrence of events such as landslides and mass movements. These impacts compromise ecosystem regulatory services that are essential to disaster risk reduction, such as erosion control, soil and slope stabilization, and sediment regulation. Importantly, the environmental impact of changing landscapes has social and symbolic reverberations. The scars and changes in terrain constitute a geographical record of the disaster and remind the communities of the potential occurrence of similar events (Figure 12).

Soil and water contamination constituted another significant environmental impact. Contact with urban waste and untreated sewage exposed human and animal health to vectors of diseases such as leptospirosis, as an example of secondary effects. 788 cases of leptospirosis and 25 deaths directly related to the 2024 floods were confirmed (Rio Grande do Sul State Government, 2024c). Access to drinking water in rural areas was also compromised, with 4,570 records of contamination, affecting at least 34,519 rural households (Collischonn et al., 2025).

## Housing impacts

According to the National Confederation of Municipalities (CNM), 96.2 thousand houses were damaged and 9.2 thousand were destroyed throughout Rio Grande do Sul, causing losses of R$ 4.6 billion in the housing sector (CNM, 2024). As a result of landslides and floods, many homes were buried, structurally compromised (Figures 12 and 13) or devastated (Figure 14), especially in areas close to slopes and riverbanks. Due to prolonged exposure to inundation and heavy rainfall, many buildings have been made precarious (Figure 15), for example, by mold and infiltrations.

## Personal and social impacts

The disaster generated serious personal impacts, affecting the population's physical and mental health. Among the most severe consequences, there were 185 deaths, 23 missing and 806 injured (Rio Grande do Sul State Government, 2024d). According to the experts who participated in the workshops, people with disabilities, reduced mobility, the elderly, pregnant women and people with infants faced greater difficulties during rescues and handling physical trauma caused by the extreme event, while individuals with chronic illnesses were at risk of worsening their conditions due to treatment interruptions (Figure 18). The loss of income and livelihoods, particularly among informal workers and small traders, further complicated this scenario, worsening individual and family living conditions. In addition, the loss of personal documents during the disaster hindered civilians' identification and access to emergency rights and aid.

In terms of social aspects, significant impacts were observed, such as the interruption of school activities and social assistance services. During the workshop, participants pointed out that the forced displacement of affected populations weakened family and community support networks, intensifying social helplessness, particularly among the most vulnerable. Episodes of violence against vulnerable groups, especially women and children, such as cases of harassment and abuse in emergency shelters (Social Communication Secretariat, 2024), were among the severe impacts observed.

## Impacts on logistics and infrastructure

From an infrastructure and logistics standpoint, the main state and federal highways in Rio Grande do Sul were interrupted at more than 100 points in mid-May 2024 due to landslides and flooding. In total, there were 251 blockages on 99 highways in 126 municipalities (Collischonn et al., 2025). These numbers do not account for municipal roads and streets, rural roads and properties' internal roads. As for the impact on railroads, 455 kilometers dedicated to cargo were blocked (GZH, 2024). The loss of mobility and transport links was particularly critical, hampering the provision and access to essential resources and services, such as food, clean water,

### Unsafe pre-disaster conditions: understanding the relationship between exposure and vulnerability

In the context of FORIN, unsafe conditions represent the immediate factors that put communities and territories at risk of disaster. Understood during the first workshop, unsafe conditions were classified according to the dimensions of FORIN's disaster risk approach (vulnerability, exposure, and climate hazards), as illustrated in Figure 25.

Among the unsafe conditions related to exposure to hazard, the occupation of risk areas stand out, including slopes and floodplain areas which, by their nature, constitute a key premise of exposure, as evidenced in data analysis. ## Articulating unsafe conditions with governance structures and legal frameworks

The disaster of May 2024 tested and exposed the limits of the disaster preparedness and response capacity of public entities in charge, revealing structural weaknesses related to governance, especially in risk management and preparation for extreme events. In March 2025 – almost a year after the disaster – only 180 municipalities had updated their Civil Defense contingency plans during the year, another 215 had outdated plans, while 87 municipalities did not have contingency plans (TCE-RS, 2025). Lacking or insufficient information about shelters, locations with drinking water, donation centers, support for affected families and escape routes were some of the implications observed and directly related to contingency plans and Civil Defense capacity.

Participants also highlighted the delay in response actions by municipal and state authorities, including evacuations, risk communication and rescues. The lack of capacities of municipal civil protection and defense agencies had already been pointed out, in 2021, by the National Secretariat for Civil Protection and Defense (Sedec) and Cemaden, within the scope of the Diagnosis of the needs and capacities of municipal civil defense units, conducted by the Elos project (Brasil, 2021; Marchezini et al., 2025).

Risk mapping, in turn, are essential instruments for prevention in municipalities. The National Policy for Civil Protection and Defense (PNPEDEC) (Law No. 12,608/2012) assigns the competence to the federal, state and municipal levels and defines the instrument as mandatory for municipalities with areas susceptible to the occurrence of major landslides, sudden inundations, or related geological or hydrological processes (Decree No. 10,692/2021). The Brazilian Geological Survey (SGB) has been supporting municipalities with the provision of risk mappings, but prioritizes those at higher risk. As of April 2026, 26 municipalities in Rio Grande do Sul have maps indicating susceptibility to gravitational mass movements and inundations, and 132 of the 497 municipalities have Geological Risk Maps developed by SGB, although some of them were prepared after the disaster of May 2024 (SGB, n.d.).

At the state level, up to the occurrence of the disaster, two main instruments stand out for their relevance for disaster risk management (DRM) and climate adaptation: Decree No. 51,547/2014, which provides for the State System of Civil Protection and Defense (Siepdec), and ProClima 2050. While the second focuses on mitigation measures and lacks structuring propositions for municipal adaptation, Siepdec demonstrates a good level of municipal integration, although municipal governments lack sufficient structure and resources.

The survey by the Court of Auditors of the State of Rio Grande do Sul (TCE-RS, 2025), conducted in March 2025, indicates that 453 municipalities⁸ have local regulations that support the municipal civil defense system. Although a significant level of legal and administrative organization of local civil protection and defense policies is observed, only 298 municipalities (60%) reported a budget allocation for prevention and preparedness actions in 2024. In addition, 94 municipalities do not have a designated server or did not respond to the survey, and another 160 have a staff composed of one or two servers. These data reveal both the uneven implementation of these policies across municipalities and the institutional vulnerability of civil defense bodies when faced with extreme events.

From the perspective of the state Civil Defense, the Crepdec regionalization has been an important strategy that allows greater proximity to local needs and agility in responding to disasters. These regional units support the preparation and updating of municipal contingency plans. However, these plans are developed autonomously by the municipalities and only revised by the Civil Defense – which verifies elements such as the presence of formal legal requirements and whether submission deadlines are met –, without active inspection (which involves practical validations of planned actions and risk maps, among other actions). The insufficiency of regional units and limited staff have restricted the response capacity, forcing civil defense units to focus on immediate relief actions, instead of preventive and structured approaches.

Although Brazil and the state of Rio Grande do Sul have a relatively robust legal framework for DRM and climate adaptation (see Appendix E), there is a persistent gap between normative formulation and its effective multisectoral implementation. Limited budget prioritization, associated with institutional weaknesses, compromises policy application, promotion and continuous execution, resulting in a low level of operationalization of the legal instruments. Additionally, weak and often intermittent inspection reduces the ability to manage compliance with the rules and prevent the reproduction of unsafe conditions in the territory.

In total, 11 root causes were identified, which are related to 20 main dynamic pressures (Table 3). Although they encompass the entire region under analysis (1st, 3rd, 8th and 9th Crepdec), they produce different impacts due to each region's specific characteristics.

The occupation of Rio Grande do Sul, since the first settlement initiatives that followed the original indigenous presence, has been deeply linked to the water courses, as demonstrated by the Jesuit missions in the seventeenth century (State of Rio Grande do Sul, 2024e). These watercourses served as natural pathways and facilitated access to essential resources, such as water and fertile soil for subsistence agriculture and livestock development, driving the interiorization of colonization, which did not find attractive conditions on the coastal strip. Occupation intensified in the colonial period, reinforcing the importance of the Guaíba and Jacuí river basins, which were adopted as transport and communication routes with other regions, creating conditions for the constitution of the first urban centers and trade routes (Pesavento, 2014). Over time, the expansion of villages and cities in the vicinity of these watercourses promoted the occupation of environmentally sensitive areas, such as floodplains and slopes near rivers, which are naturally susceptible to floods and landslides.

This process resulted in a land occupation model that lacks resilience, identified as the first root cause, which reflects a history of inadequate occupation in PPAs, especially along water courses and on steep slopes. Urban sprawl has intensified this inadequate land use and expanded the advance into risk areas. This pattern is further aggravated by socio-spatial inequality, which forces more vulnerable populations to settle in areas with higher risks and lower

real estate value \( ^{9} \) , although occupations by high-income populations are also present. Further complicating this context, the predominance of land regularization mechanisms focused on individual plots allows the continuity of constructions in exposed locations, to the detriment of collective risk and broader planning vision. Studies on informal settlements show that individual plot regularization does not solve the precariousness of constructions and vulnerability to risks, requiring an urban policy approach that articulates regularization, infrastructure and territorial management (Fantin et al., 2022). In rural regions, the increasing homogeneity of economic activities, such as agricultural crops and intensive livestock production, degrades the soil, reduces ecological diversity and compromises environmental resilience.

Another contributing factor is the low social memory about local risks and disasters (Yulardi, 2025), which limits the collective perception of dangers associated with inappropriate occupations. This perpetuates practices such as reoccupation of susceptible areas after disasters and development of economic activities that are incompatible with proper land use practices. In September 2023, less than a year before the disaster in May 2024, the municipalities of Alto Taquari had already suffered from inundations that resulted in 54 deaths and 400 thousand people affected (Stariolo, 2024). In this scenario, environmentally sensitive areas continue to be occupied, whether for housing or the development of economic activities, contributing over time to deforestation and alteration of natural water courses, affecting the soil's infiltration capacity and increasing the risks of erosion and siltation (Westin; Araújo; Leal, 2025), whose progression is identified in the dynamic pressure characterized by changes in local geomorphological, geological and hydrological conditions.

The second root cause identified concerns the geomorphological and hydrological conditions favorable to the disaster in Rio Grande do Sul. The state's extensive hydrographic network, comprising large river basins and fed by regular rainfall throughout the year, favors the occurrence of chain floods, especially in alluvial plains and low slope areas, where water runoff is constrained. Steep slopes, alluvial plains and regions subject to siltation make the state more susceptible to floods, landslides and erosive processes, especially in periods of heavy rainfall. In terms of governance, the dynamic pressure of intensifying climate change is also connected to the fourth root cause, the **development model that prioritizes the economy over environmental and social agendas**. This prioritization compromises policies and actions aimed at environmental preservation and social protection by favoring short-term economic growth, often considered the main indicator of progress (Sachs, 2004; Ascelrad, 2002). The context of climate emergency, however, requires responses capable of generating significant environmental, social and economic returns (Brandon et al., 2025), even if the latter require longer time horizons – such as integrated territorial planning, sustainable management of natural resources, and assistance to the most vulnerable groups. As a consequence, these responses are relegated to the background, revealing the limits of a development model predominantly driven by economic growth.

The **lack of political prioritization of the socio-environmental agenda** in the context of the disaster generates dynamic pressures that compromise the capacity for risk management. At the state or municipal level, resources are restricted and often directed to areas with revenues linked to mandatory expenditures of greater political visibility or immediate return, such as health and education, which reduces funding and attention for risk management and disaster prevention. Outdated or inexistent risk mapping – basic instruments in the DRM agenda – compromise action planning and the communication of risks to the population. Additionally, the lack of hydrometeorological monitoring systems hinders the development of a consistent database for event simulations, monitoring of risk areas, and issuance of personalized alerts. In the context of Rio Grande do Sul, the weakening of regional universities is another factor that limits the ability to operate or interpret such data and systems. With adequate funding, these institutions could play a relevant role as research and innovation hubs for risk management, with particular attention to small municipalities in Rio Grande do Sul.

The root cause associated with the **neglected legal framework for risk management** triggers a series of dynamic pressures that weaken disaster prevention capacity. The PNPDEC (Law No. 12,608/2012) does not specify aspects related to

the obligations of federated entities and public agents in DRM. While the law establishes comprehensive guidelines for prevention, mitigation, preparedness, response and recovery, it assigns responsibilities in a broad and shared manner, without sufficient hierarchies or specifications, which creates overlaps, gaps, and coordination difficulties. For example, while authorizing the federal, state and municipal governments to implement preventive measures, it does not detail the operationalization of continuous local monitoring efforts and does not specify minimum standards for alerting infrastructure, which may lead to failures in the coverage and effectiveness of monitoring systems.

Moreover, although the law establishes relevant instruments, such as the National System of Civil Protection and Defense (Sinpdec) and contingency plans, there are no clear means of implementation or access to adequate resources (Damacena et al., 2022; 2023), as the National Fund for Public Calamities, Civil Protection and Defense (Funcap) (Law No. 12,340/2010) is focused on the allocation of resources to municipalities declaring a state of emergency or calamity, aimed at the reestablishment of essential services and reconstruction of affected areas.

The low budget allocated to essential risk management areas (Brasil, 2021; Marchezini et al., 2025), with underfunded human resources or operational capacities, limits municipal emergency prevention and response capacities, adding to the outdated risk mappings and the absence of hydrometeorological monitoring systems at the state and municipal levels. The neglect of the disaster management framework is also connected to the flexibility of environmental licensing in risk areas, while low investments in resilient infrastructure and the lack of inspection of practices harmful to the environment and irregular occupations increase the risk of disasters.

The **duality between public and private** reflects an imbalance in the interaction of shared responsibilities between these sectors, particularly in environmental and disaster management. The lack of human and financial resources in the public sector limits its capacity for investment, oversight and monitoring, with the resulting gaps typically occupied by private interests that prioritize financial performance at the expense of social and environmental impacts. The lack of social control over public policies aggravates this duality, allowing important decisions to be made without transparency or meaningful civil society participation, which reduces the legitimacy of government actions and creates room for practices that disregard long-term effects.

## Discussion

The root cause analysis and structuring of the causal model of vulnerability progression allowed the identification of factors that systemically contributed to the 2024 disaster in Rio Grande do Sul. These factors, common to other climate disaster scenarios, mainly highlight the role of human and institutional decisions in generating and socially disseminating risk over time.

The combined analysis of different affected regions shows that the disaster occurred in territories with specific physical, environmental, social and institutional configurations. These factors unfolded in an interconnected and systemic manner, producing relatively homogeneous impact patterns within and across regions, although with some variations according to territorial specificities. Thus, the disaster did not result from isolated causes, but from the expression of a system comprising mutually reinforcing weaknesses. By offering this structural understanding of the disaster, the study sheds light on central elements for the improvement of risk management policies, reinforcing the imperative of territorial planning at the river basin scale; the creation of regional and supra-municipal governance bodies; and integrated approaches focused on climate justice.

### Understanding the disaster beyond the physical-climate event

Although the disaster was triggered by an extreme hydrometeorological event – widely addressed by technical-scientific studies (Andrades-Filho et al., 2025; Collischonn et al., 2024; Laipelt et al., 2025; Marengo et al., 2024) –, the results of this analysis reveal that social, economic and governance factors are as structuring as hydrological and meteorological processes, thus significantly contributing to triggering and amplifying their impacts.

Governance root causes related to the traditional DRM normative framework, such as the neglected legal framework, appear in the work of Marchezini et al. (2025) and Alvalá et al. (2024), which highlight the budgetary limitations of municipal civil defenses and the absence of planning instruments such as risk mapping. The lack of political prioritization of the socio-environmental agenda is discussed by Pillar and Overbeck (2024), who point to the successive weakening of legislation to protect native vegetation in the state. Insufficient governance to address climate-related issues across management levels is partially addressed by Marengo (2025), who emphasizes the “fragmented approach” and “institutional silos” that prevent the translation of hydrometeorological data into unified and effective responses.

By going beyond the technical reading and the traditional disaster and civil defense agenda, the results point to the contrast between the regional scale of the disaster and the interfederative governance structure (Union, states and municipalities). Although all the affected municipalities had local civil defense bodies and contingency plans (TCE-RS, 2025) in place, the magnitude of the event –far exceeding administrative boundaries – revealed that such isolated structures are insufficient to address disasters on a regional scale. The dependence on state emergency responses and the creation of the federal Extraordinary Secretariat in Support of the Reconstruction of Rio Grande do Sul highlights the gaps in both disaster management and risk management.

In this context, the study highlights the lack of supra-municipal regional governance to enable integrated approaches to disaster prevention and risk management, especially at the mesoregional level encompassing the river basins. The susceptibility of Rio Grande do Sul to the recurrence of extreme hydrometeorological events demonstrates the insufficiency of local planning against the observed scale of impacts. The progressive ineffectiveness of river basin committees and councils and the absence of regional bodies with executive mandates for integrated risk management and climate adaptation illustrate the fragility of this system.

The study conducted by Tonetto et al. (2025) on the climate-related fiscal capacity of the municipalities of Rio Grande do Sul reinforces this point, underscoring the limited financial capacity of local governments: only 10% of the severely affected municipalities show some level of budgetary leeway for adaptation and mitigation actions. The state government, in turn, would need between 1% and 5% of its GDP to respond to disasters, values that exceed the approximately 5% of net current revenues in annual investments. In this context, it is important to rethink the role of coalitions (Saito et al., 2021), municipal associations and river basin committees as instruments of regional cooperation and local capacity-building.

### Culture of risk, communication and public trust

Root causes and dynamic pressures of a sociocultural nature emerge as constraints and transversal influences in both prevention and mitigation, and disaster response. The culture of climate change denial is strongly linked to the lack of prevention culture, with effects ranging from the relaxation of environmental norms to the underestimation of risk. This aspect, rarely discussed in technical diagnoses, is key to the development of resilience strategies. Low social memory, the tendency to reoccupy risk areas, the disregard for warnings,

In this context, communication plays a strategic role. The media is a key channel to inform the population, expose hidden vulnerabilities, demand accountability from authorities and communicate scientific knowledge about risks in an accessible way (Silva et al., 2025). In terms of institutional communication, the experience of the Pelotas municipal government – with direct broadcasts and centralized communication – demonstrated the importance of transparent and participatory practices to strengthen public trust and community mobilization (Vasconcelos; Casali, 2025).

## The neglect of climate justice and climate adaptation

It is important to reflect on the root cause of socioeconomic inequality and concentration of wealth. Although climate vulnerability is defined by low adaptive capacity and sensitivity to extreme events (IPCC, 2022), more than a year after the occurrence of the disaster, socio-spatial inequalities – despite determining the most severe impacts – remain invisible in the works published so far, especially with regard to the analysis of risk preconditions. Low-income populations, informal workers, smallholder farmers and traditional groups have been disproportionately affected, and adaptation with a focus on protecting the most vulnerable groups remains poorly integrated into urban and rural development strategies.

Previously described situations – such as gender-based violence in shelters (Social Communication Secretariat, 2024), loss of community networks and precarious work conditions – highlight the social and unequal face of the disaster, a direct consequence of the overlapping of vulnerabilities. This study reinforces the need for public policies and post-disaster institutional actions to incorporate climate justice, territorial equity and integrated climate adaptation. In this sense, the importance of social participation in decision-making processes is evidenced, including the development and implementation of disaster prevention and response actions, especially in historically exposed and vulnerable areas.

## FORIN’s strengths and methodological limitations

The multiregional nature of the May 2024 disaster in Rio Grande do Sul comprises physical-environmental, climate, socioeconomic and institutional dimensions that hinder a homogeneous diagnosis of root causes and imply some level of analysis generalization to the detriment of greater specificity. The systemic nature and the interdependent chain of factors that accumulated risks in the period preceding the outbreak of the disaster impose the need for a comprehensive reading that broadens the understanding of an extreme event, encompassing both the factors preceding, and the aspects beyond its occurrence.

It is worth noting the limitations of the results and analyses, as well as the application of the FORIN approach in such a particular context. Despite its multisectoral focus and consideration of social and historical factors that influence disaster risk, the approach does not originally describe methods for establishing participatory processes (Ferreira et al., 2023) and extensive consultations with affected communities. This limitation makes it difficult to apply the methodology in contexts where collaboration with local actors is essential to capture nuances in vulnerabilities and dynamic pressures. On the other hand, the application of FORIN in smaller disasters has shown more specific and detailed results (Ferreira, 2023; Ferreira et al., 2023). In the present case, isolating the analysis of the causes of a disaster of such magnitude for smaller portions of the territory would potentially lead to significant loss of understanding of the phenomenon. In light of this limitation, the application of questionnaires and interviews with representatives from the state countryside (members of civil society, public authorities and experts) aimed to broaden the analysis by incorporating different perspectives, even though the results were mostly interpreted by experts from different fields of knowledge.

To organize the responses, the data were tabulated according to the questions and analyzed using qualitative tools from Python libraries, allowing focus on the most recurrent terms and expressions mentioned by the participants. Subsequently, in the pre-processing stage, the responses were handled through automated routines, such as the preprocess_text() function, responsible for removing special characters, punctuation and irrelevant words (such as stop words), as well as tokenization (text flow breaking) and lemmatization. This stage thus aimed at ensuring that subsequent analyses focused on the most important terms and expressions. The basic data treatment process was followed by the stage of word cloud generation. This consists in the generation of a visual representation in which the most frequent words in the dataset appear in a larger size, allowing a quick identification of the most recurrent keywords in the answers. This processing used the create_wordcloud() function, responsible for creating word clouds for each question, using the processed tokens.

Finally, the research team conducted an analysis of co-occurrences, using a matrix that captures the frequency with which pairs of words appear together in the answers, allowing the identification of relevant relationships between the terms. This analysis applied a get_co_occurrence_matrix() function, which creates a co-occurrence matrix based on the words of the processed tokens. Co-occurrence is accounted for whenever two words occur within a context window. By adding the plot_co_occurrence_network() function, based on the networkx library, the co-occurrence relations were visualized in a network format.

Thus, it was possible to identify groupings of terms (shown in Tables C1, C2 and C3) that, following an assessment of cohesion, gave rise to nine categories: development, physical and environmental factors, governance, socioeconomic factors, forecast, recurrence, knowledge, communication and resilience. These describe the occurrence of the socio-environmental disaster, along with its trigger events and triggered events. Considering the methodological proposition of FORIN, a direct and retrospective relationship is established between the trigger events and the process of risk accumulation, structured in unsafe conditions, dynamic pressures and root causes (Witting, 2012). Thus, in addition to allowing the initial organization of the data, the most recurrent categories (development, physical and environmental factors, and governance) also guided the analytical structure of the subsequent research stages.

■ Development category: focused on the affected areas that were occupied or exposed, this category discusses the responsiveness of state and government structures. It is related to the vulnerability of areas occupied by exposed populations and the government's ability to mitigate the impacts.
■ Physical and environmental factors category: addresses climate factors, such as humidity accumulation, atmospheric blocking, and the impact of these phenomena in the central and southern regions of Brazil. For example, the mention of the terms river and flying suggests specific weather conditions that contributed to the disaster.
■ Governance category: reflects discussions about the lack of adequate risk management, indicating that this absence was an important factor both in triggering the disaster and in amplifying its impacts.

The keywords that were not included in any of the categories proposed by co-occurrence were then analyzed individually in order to verify the possibility of forming a new coherent category. This analysis enabled the creation of a specific category to represent socioeconomic factors.

## Glossary

**Disaster**: severe disruption of the functioning of a community or a society, at any scale, due to hazardous interacting with conditions of exposure, vulnerability and capacity, leading to one or more of the following: human, material, economic and environmental losses and impacts (UNDRR, 2017).

**Disaster risk**: potential for loss of life, occurrence of injury, destruction or damage to property that may affect a system, society or community in a specific period, probabilistically estimated as a function of the interaction between hazard, exposure and vulnerability (UNDRR, 2017). Wisner, Gaillard and Kelman (2012) approach disaster risk as a product arising not only from the combination of hazards and vulnerability, but also from the capacity for protection and public policies to mitigate large-scale risks.

**Disaster Risk Management (DRM)**: permanent and continuous social process, supported by institutional and community structures, with the aim of addressing vulnerabilities and hazards present in the territory (MIDR, 2021).

**Dynamic pressure**: an ongoing factor or process that continuously modifies vulnerability or risk over time. It represents forces in motion, which increase or transform unsafe conditions, making systems more susceptible to future disasters (Oliver-Smith et al., 2016).

**Exposure**: the presence of people, livelihoods, species or ecosystems, environmental functions, services and resources, infrastructure, or economic, social or cultural assets in places and scenarios that may be negatively affected (IPCC, 2018).

**Extreme event**: physical-climate phenomenon considered extreme due to its low incidence and its observed values, which are positively or negatively distant from the average values of a given location (Sarewitz; Pielke Jr., 2001; Seneviratne et al., 2021).

**Flash flood**: surface runoff of high speed and energy, caused by intense and concentrated rainfall, usually in small hydrographic basins of rugged relief. Characterized by the sudden increase in the flows of a given drainage and sudden overflow of the river channel. It has great destructive power (MIDR, 2022).

**Flood**: the process of raising the water level of a drainage channel due to an increase in water flow, which starts to flow at the maximum level (occupying the floodplain or larger riverbed), but without overflowing (Marchezini et al., 2024).

**Governance**: actions, processes, traditions and institutions by which authority is exercised, and collective decisions are made and implemented (IRGC, 2017).

**Hazard**: the potential occurrence of a natural or human-induced physical event or trend that may cause loss of life, injury or other health impacts, as well as damage and loss to property, infrastructure, livelihoods, service provision, ecosystems and environmental resources (IPCC, 2018).

**Hydrometeorological event**: natural process or phenomenon of atmospheric, hydrological or oceanographic origin (UNDRR, 2017).

**Impact**: consequence of perceived risks in natural and human systems, which result from the interactions of climate-related hazards (including extreme weather or climate events), exposure and vulnerability. Impacts generally refer to the effects on lives, livelihoods, health and well-being, ecosystems and species, economic, social and cultural assets, services (including ecosystem services) and infrastructure. Impacts may be referred to as consequences or outcomes, adverse or beneficial (IPCC, 2023).

**Inundation**: submergence of areas outside the normal limits of a watercourse in zones that are not normally submerged. Overflow occurs gradually, usually caused by prolonged rainfall in lowland areas (MIDR, 2022).

**Magnitude**: measures and thresholds that define the extreme character of an event, to be defined by hydrometeorological services at national and subnational levels, according to local climate characteristics and application sectors (Marchezini et al., 2023).

**Mesoregional scale**: intermediate scale of territorial organization and planning situated in the context of this study between the municipal and state levels, in which the social process acts as a determining element, the natural framework represents a conditioning factor, and the network of communication and locations constitutes the main element of spatial articulation (IBGE, 1990).

**Multi-regional**: spatial coverage that simultaneously encompasses more than one region, within the same territorial area of analysis. In the context of this study, the disaster is characterized as multiregional because it affects different regions within the state of Rio Grande do Sul, exceeding regional administrative boundaries.

**Root cause**: comprises social, economic, political, institutional and environmental conditions that have historically perpetuated vulnerabilities and shaped risk (Oliver-Smith et al., 2016).

**Trigger event**: immediate physical event that triggers a disaster by materializing the potential hazard, transforming an existing risk condition into an effective disaster (Oliver-Smith et al., 2016).
