HIGHLIGHTS
 ▪ The presence and accessibility of Public Green Areas (PGAs)  
are strongly correlated with income and wealth. 
 ▪ The wealthiest region of Belo Horizonte (Centro-Sul)  
has the highest number and best access to Public Green Areas.
 ▪ On average, in a 15-minute walk, the lowest income decile (poorest 
10 percent of the population) can access only 1 PGA, which would 
be on average 13,000 square meters (m²). In contrast , the 10 percent 
wealthiest can access 4 PGAs with an average area of 46,000 m². 
By bicycle, values for the same groups are 13 PGAs (or 140,000 m²) 
and 33 (346,000 m²).
 ▪ By public transport the results are more unequal: in 30 minutes,  
6 PGAs (0.052 square kilometers) are accessible for the lowest-
income 10 percent, and 31 (0.256 km²) for the 10 percent wealthiest. 
 ▪ The main recommendations for the city in the short term are to 
increase public transport availability in peripheral neighborhoods 
and increase their connection to PGAs. In the mid and long-term, 
we suggest prioritizing these regions for the creation of new PGAs 
and stimulating transit-oriented development (TOD).

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
Background
Public green areas provide important environmental 
benefits such as lowering temperatures and improving air 
quality. They improve residents’ quality of life by bringing 
people into contact with nature and providing space for 
recreation. These areas should be easy for all urban dwellers 
to access, but research in different countries reveals that 
these spaces are inequitably distributed and that low-income 
communities have less access to them (Kaczynski et al. 2016; 
Venter et al. 2020).
About this paper
This working paper analyzes accessibility to public green 
areas (PGAs) in the city of Belo Horizonte, Brazil. While 
most similar studies focus on access to work or study 
opportunities, we dive into access to these leisure areas and 
its relationship with income. Findings show that wealthier 
residents experience increased access in two different 
approaches: the number and the total area of accessible PGAs.
Income and access to green spaces
Findings show that public green areas are concentrated 
in the wealthier regions of the city, and the wealthiest 
residents, on average, enjoy better access to PGAs by both 
measures. Average accessibility increases steadily with income 
for the three calculated transportation modes. The difference 
in accessibility between low and high-income groups is the 
starkest for public transport (4.9 times greater in terms of 
both number and area). For walking, those figures were 3.0 
and 3.6 times greater, and for bicycling, they were 2.5 and 
2.6 times greater.
Recommendations
Policy action should be taken to make access to public green 
areas more equal. Short-term actions should be centered on 
increasing access by public transport from peripheral areas. 
Women, children, and the elderly should receive special 
attention in policy formulation due to natural mobility 
restrictions. As for the creation of new PGAs, we suggest 
changes in the legislation that can allow the creation of new 
units outside the land of new developments and also the 
creation of a fund to subsidize the development of new PGAs 
by the municipality. 
Long-term action should follow principles of transit-oriented 
development, such as improving the quality and frequency 
of public transport, encouraging more compact and mixed 
land-use development, affordable housing, and investments in 
pedestrian and bike infrastructure.

INTRODUCTION
The combination of rapid urbanization and climate change 
has created many challenges for cities. By 2020, 56 percent 
(4.4 billion) of the world’s population was living in urban 
areas. By 2050, that number will grow to an estimated 70 
percent (World Bank 2022). Between 2018 and 2030 urban 
areas are expected to expand by 80 percent (Mahtta et al. 
2019). Rapid urbanization is modifying and suppressing 
natural ecosystems, changing hydrological cycles, and 
causing soil erosion, habitat losses, and extinction of species 
(Roy et al. 2012; Schneider et al. 2012; Cilliers et al. 2013).  
This damage to natural ecosystems also harms urban 
populations by worsening air quality, raising temperatures, and 
exacerbating hazards such as heat waves and floods. 
Green spaces, urban forests, and trees are nature-based 
solutions that can relieve the environmental stress caused by 
urbanization (Alberti and Marzluff 2004; Escobedo et al. 
2011; Roy et al. 2012; du Toit et al. 2018; Bush and Doyon 
2019). Generally, they mitigate high temperatures, reduce 
pollution, sequester carbon dioxide, and host biodiversity 
(Escobedo et al. 2011; du Toit et al. 2018). They also improve 
the quality of life for residents by contributing to better health 
and well-being, social relations, recreation, and spiritual life 
(Tzoulas et al. 2007; Keniger et al. 2013; du Toit et al. 2018). 
Thus, providing access to green areas has become an important 
component of contemporary urban policy approaches aligned 
with sustainability goals (Raj et al. 2021).
In Brazil’s cities, as in cities around the world, (Kaczynski et al. 
2016; Venter et al. 2020; Zhang et al. 2021), parks and green 
areas are unevenly distributed. This limits opportunities for 
many citizens to access them, especially those from  
low-income communities, which tend to use this type of 
urban equipment more than high-income groups (Wendel et 
al. 2012). Low-income residents are often situated far from 
the city centers and are underserved by public transport (PT), 
taking longer time to access most “urban opportunities”, or 
urban services and activities, such as parks and green areas 
(Pereira et al. 2019). This type of inequality is often a legacy of 
the way green areas were created, from formerly private lands, 
often in expensive neighborhoods with good infrastructure. 
Spatial location can be another factor of exclusion, and women, 
children, and the elderly are demographic groups that, due to 
specific mobility restrictions, often experience more difficulties 
in accessing public leisure spaces (Wendel et al. 2012).
To guide inclusive urban policies able to improve access by 
marginalized groups, this study approaches accessibility by 
focusing on green areas. Most existing studies of accessibility 
in Brazilian cities focus on the ease or difficulty of reaching 
destinations for work and study (Guimarães 2011; Pereira et 
al. 2019; Bittencourt et al. 2021; Wu et al. 2021), and relatively 
few examine access to places for leisure and interaction with 
nature (Ayala Filho et al. 2018; Hino et al. 2019; Bao et al. 
2023). This study aims to fill that gap by estimating levels 
of accessibility to public green spaces in Belo Horizonte, 
Brazil, and providing evidence to policymakers, both in this 
city and others in the Global South, that can help them to 
improve accessibility to these areas and narrow inequalities 
between social groups.
It is important to point out that, as in most Brazilian cities, 
most green areas are created due to the legal obligations of 
land developers, usually in developed and wealthier regions, 
so it is expected that some degree of inequality related to 
income will be found.
Belo Horizonte was chosen primarily due to its national 
relevance, the quality of the data available on green 
areas, and the availability of GTFS1 files of the public 
transportation system.
The goal of this working paper is to identify which areas of the 
city have the highest and lowest levels of accessibility to public 
green areas and to measure correlations between accessibility 
levels and income data. The neighborhoods with the 
largest deficits can then be identified as priorities for future 
public policies such as increased park space and improved 
transportation systems.

LITERATURE REVIEW
Urban green spaces
Many terms are used to refer to urban spaces that shelter 
and preserve nature, and available literature does not follow 
a standardized pattern. “Green spaces” and “green areas” are 
used to refer to vacant (free of buildings or other waterproof 
coverage) and permeable urban spaces, usually composed of 
arboreal and shrubby vegetation, such as parks, plazas, and 
community gardens (Byrne et al. 2010; Caporusso Bargos 
and Fonseca Matias 2019). The broader term “Urban trees” 
generally refers to the presence of trees and similar vegetation 
in cities, even when they are in flowerbeds or private 
spaces. “Urban forest” relates to larger units with a focus 
on preservation.
This publication adopts the term “Public Green Areas” 
(PGAs) due to its frequent use in the literature and its 
compatibility with available data from Belo Horizonte. 
Accessibility
Accessibility has been a popular topic in urban studies 
since the 1950s. Hansen (1959) helped to popularize the 
concept, defining it as the “potential of opportunities for 
interaction”. According to Levinson and King (2020), access 
(interchangeably used with “accessibility”) is the “people’s 
ability to reach goods, services, and activities”, and this is the 
definition that will be used in this work. Another important 
concept that will be used throughout the publication is 
“opportunities.” These are goods, services, and activities that 
people need or want to access, for example, workplaces, 
schools, hospitals, and public green areas.
Accessibility is not the same as “mobility” although the 
two are often confused. Increased mobility contributes to 
increased accessibility, but accessibility is a broader concept. 
It involves transport (its ease and availability of modes), and 
land use (where people live and opportunities are distributed). 
This makes accessibility a more sophisticated indicator, but 
one that is more difficult to measure (Handy 2020). Thus 
assessing and improving it requires input from experts in both 
transport and land use.
An aspect that often is related to accessibility is equity. Equity 
is a theory of justice that states that assets, goods, or services 
should be distributed according to the needs of each person 
or group to provide the same opportunities for all (Fainstein 
2014; Pereira 2018), unlike equality, which sees justice as 
providing the same for all. More practically, equity defends 
that historically marginalized groups (due to race, ethnicity, 
income, gender, etc) should be prioritized in policies over 
groups that can already achieve their main needs (Rawls 
1992). When applied to accessibility, the “equity lens” declares

Accessibility measures
There are several approaches to measuring accessibility. 
According to Geurs and Van Wee (2004), an accessibility 
measure should ideally consider four components: transport, 
land use, time, and individual preference. Since this is not 
always possible, we focus on one or more of these four aspects. 
One approach to accessibility involves location-based 
measures, typically used in urban planning and geographical 
studies (Geurs and Van Wee 2004). This type of measure has 
both transport and land-use components: travel time/costs 
between locations and the spatial distribution of opportunities, 
respectively (Geurs and Van Wee 2004).
Location-based approaches include the cumulative 
opportunities measure, considered one of the simplest 
classes of measures, that consists of counting the number 
of opportunities reached within a given time, distance, or 
cost, thus emphasizing the number of potential destinations 
(Handy and Niemeier 1997; Geurs and Van Wee 2004).

Accessibility to green areas
When it comes to green space indicators of presence or 
accessibility, there is not a worldwide standard to be followed. 
The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends that 
cities should have at least 15 m² of green areas per inhabitant, 
but this standard does not say anything about proximity or 
accessibility (e.g. the maximum distance or travel time for 
a citizen to reach a green area) and can also be misleading 
in cities that concentrate green spaces in specific regions. 
According to Bao et al. (2023), there are a few indicators 
in Europe, the USA, China, and the UK, but they differ 
significantly. While the European Environment Agency 
recommends that residents should have a green space in a 
one-kilometer radius, this value is 800 meters (m) in the 
USA and 300m in the UK (Bao et al. 2023). However, no 
relevant standards were identified for Brazil or other South 
American countries.

Research conducted in other countries highlights that PGAs 
are not equitably distributed in cities, and that lower-income 
groups have reduced access to them (Dai 2011; Kaczynski 
et al. 2016; Venter et al. 2020; Zhang et al. 2021). In a study 
conducted in Atlanta, Dai (2011) found that lower green 
space access is strongly correlated with being Black and below 
the poverty line. Venter et al. (2020) revealed that in South 
Africa, areas dominated by wealthier white residents have 
greater tree cover and overall greenery, and their residents, 
on average, live 700m closer to a public park compared 
to areas where disadvantaged groups are more numerous. 
Similar results were found for Shanghai (Zhang et al. 2021), 
where sub-districts with higher socioeconomic status are 
more likely to have good access to four categories of benefits 
provided by urban parks. Bao et al. (2023) investigated the 
spatial associations between green space accessibility and five 
indicators of social vulnerability, and the findings highlight 
that particularly the elderly and individuals with limited 
education face restricted access.
Studies conducted in Brazilian cities show inequalities in 
access to green spaces with lower accessibility among  
low-income neighborhoods (Ayala Filho et al. 2018; 
Gonçalves and Maraschin 2021). In Florianópolis, residents 
from neighborhoods with less than 4.5 m² of PGA area per 
inhabitant are 17.65 percent less wealthy than residents from 
neighborhoods with more than 20 m²/inhab. of PGA area 
(Ayala Filho et al. 2018). A study conducted in the city of 
Pelotas by Gonçalves and Maraschin (2021) shows that, on 
average, low-income regions are further from PGAs than 
wealthy regions and the available ones are of poorer quality.
METHODOLOGY AND DATA
To gather our data, we used BHMap, the city’s open data 
platform.2 The parques e praças (parks and squares) database 
(a geographical layer containing the coordinates of all parks 
and squares of the city) was chosen, since it is the one that 
best fits the definition of PGAs used in this study. Since 
the study focuses on access, only parks and squares that are 
open to the regular public are considered – some spaces with 
limited access, like some natural reserves, were not counted. 
Only spaces with an area larger than 200 m² were counted 
as PGAs, to exclude small areas that cannot be effectively 
used for leisure purposes, such as roundabouts, extended 
median strips, and similar urban elements. This area threshold 
adopted the criteria used in Campinas’ city plan (Campinas 
2015), in which a similar-sized Brazilian city set this same 
minimum area for a similar purpose. Data sources used in 
the study include:
 ▪ BHMap, the city geographical open data website, was 
used to obtain a shapefile that reveals the locations of all 
parks (parques) and public squares (praças). Even though 
praças is a broad category that may include areas that are 
not completely surrounded by nature, they are important 
public spaces for leisure, recreation, sports practice, or other 
uses. A filtering step was taken to delete PGAs closed to 
the public and those with areas less than 200 m².
 ▪ The Applied Economics Research Institute (IPEA), 
a public Brazilian research institute. IPEA’s Access 
to Opportunities Project provides data from selected 
Brazilian cities (including Belo Horizonte) based on the 
2010 national census and other sources (Pereira et al. 
2019).3  This includes accessibility results for destinations 
such as workplaces, schools, and hospitals – but not 
green spaces – as well as population and socioeconomic 
data in high spatial resolution, using a hexagonal grid. In 
this database, municipalities’ territories are divided into 
hexagons of 0.11 km² (diagonal of 357 m) used as spatial 
units in the analysis. We decided to use this source because 
it has higher spatial resolution than the data found in 
the census (the “censitary sectors”, which differ a lot in 
terms of shape and size), and also to allow comparisons 
between both analyses.
 ▪ OpenStreetMap street networks, needed to calculate and 
analyze accessibility.4 
 ▪ Belo Horizonte GTFS (General Transit Feed 
Specification) files, available on the municipality’s website.5 
This set of data contains information regarding the 
operation of PT such as:
 ▪ Routes: list of all bus routes;
 ▪ Stop times: list with arrival and departure times of all 
routes in all stops;
 ▪ Stops: list with names and coordinates of all bus stops.
We used Conveyal6, an online software tool based on 
OpenTripPlanner7, to run the accessibility analysis. It requires 
the following inputs: 
 ▪ A transport network database containing all roads that will 
be considered in the analysis;
 ▪ A GTFS feed, a set of files containing all public transport 
itineraries and timetables;
 ▪ Origins and destinations spatial datasets that 
account for the starting and ending points of all trips 
calculated by the tool.

In our case, the origins are the centers of the city hexagons, 
and the destinations are the PGA locations. The destinations 
dataset was used in two different ways. In the first, each PGA 
accounts for a single opportunity regardless of its size, i.e., 
the smallest and the largest PGAs share the same value. The 
second considers the size of the PGA in m², as larger units are 
considered more beneficial for public use. This dual approach 
was used because, if done individually, both techniques might 
overestimate the results for regions that are served by several 
small areas or only one very large park, and none of these 
scenarios can be considered as real, good access to PGAs.
The destinations dataset consists of polygons; therefore, the 
software considers the centroid of each PGA as the actual 
destination. Figure 2 presents the division of the city surface 
in hexagons – the origins of the trips on the accessibility 
analysis – and Figure 3 presents the destinations of the trips 
for both approaches – on the first, PGAs share the same value 
regardless of their size; on the second, their area is considered.
Three sustainable transportation modes were considered: 
 ▪ Walking, with a timeframe of 15 minutes and 
speed of 3.6km/h;
 ▪ Bicycle, with a timeframe of 15 minutes and an average 
speed of 12 km/h.8 Routes with cycling infrastructure are 
not considered or prioritized by the algorithm;
 ▪ Public transport, with a timeframe of 30 minutes, 
with access to stations by walking with a maximum of 
three transfers. 
The travel time of 15 minutes was chosen following the 
principle of 15-minute cities, a concept of city where main 
opportunities are accessible in 15 minutes by walk or bicycle 
(Moreno et al. 2021; Pozoukidou and Chatziyiannaki 2021) 
and the goals of the SP 2040 (city of São Paulo’s strategic 
plan for 2040), where it is stated that every citizen should be 
able to access a green area in 15 minutes (São Paulo 2012). 
Since this is a rather short timeframe for GTFS-based public 
transport analysis, which considers walking to stations and 
waiting time along with travel time itself, we decided to 
increase the travel time of this mode to 30 minutes.
To compare access to PGAs in different areas of the city, we 
chose a specific scenario or period of time: between 7 and 9 
a.m., rush hour,on a weekday in September 2020. This was the 
first year of the COVID-19 pandemic, a time when outdoor 
activities were encouraged and public transport never stopped 
functioning in the city.
The results were obtained using the “Regional Analysis”, 
one of Conveyal’s features, which generates as outputs 
maps displaying the accessibility value of each origin, 
i.e., the number of accessible PGAs departing from each 
part of the city. 
In order to analyze the accessibility differences between 
different social groups, results were compared with the average 
income of the city’s regions. Even though inequality has 
many faces and can be expressed by different variables, we 
decided to focus on income since it is a relevant and easy-to-
address indicator. The population of the city was divided into 
10 income deciles and the average value for each decile was 
calculated, making comparisons between groups possible.
The methodology has a few limitations, primarily linked 
to the cumulative opportunities approach. It lacks the 
possibility of stating differences between each opportunity, 
i.e., it assumes they are all equally desirable despite their 
distance from the point of origin. In addition, travel time is 
the sole component of accessibility considered. Travel fares, 
safety, comfort, and personal characteristics (age, gender, or 
physical disabilities) are other aspects that can be included 
in analyses (Cui and Levinson 2018; Pereira et al. 2019). 
Moreover, using the centroid of each hexagon as the origin of 
trips can lead to inconsistencies, since ideally the accessibility 
would be calculated for each residence, but this is the most 
disaggregated geographic unit possible. Another limitation is 
the fact that this type of analysis does not consider population 
or road density, which may under or overestimate results for 
sparsely or highly populated regions. Besides that, income is 
the only socioeconomic aspect used for analyzing inequality, 
whereas other factors such as race and gender widely impact 
equity in the Brazilian context.

CONTEXT OF 
THE STUDY AREA
Belo Horizonte, the capital of Minas Gerais state, is Brazil’s 
6th most populous city with 2.35 million inhabitants in 2021. 
The city was founded in 1897 as one of the first fully planned 
Brazilian cities and its design is reflected in the grid pattern 
of its central area. Its location inside Brazil and the State of 
Minas Gerais can be found in Figure 4.
As can be seen in Figure 5, the city is divided into nine 
administrative regions. Figures 5 and 6 depict, respectively, 
population density and average household income. The city 
hexagons were divided in deciles according to income, i.e., 10 
equally large parts in terms of population, ranked according to 
household per capita income.
As the maps show, most of the 10th decile is located in the 
same region of the city, the Centro-Sul region, that shelters 
the central business district and was first developed in the late 
19th century, concentrating large shares of income. This region

In general, the further from Centro-Sul, the lower the average

Besides being one of the denser regions of the city and 
concentrating a high share of wealth, the Centro-Sul region

According to public data from Belo Horizonte, 80 parks and

Noroeste, regions with low average income, are, respectively,

that more detailed analysis are needed to certify this relation.

Historically, the creation of new PGAs in Belo Horizonte 
occurs due to diverse factors. More recently, most derive from 
legal obligations in land development by the private sector, 
according to Federal Law 6,766 (Brasil 1979), in which the 
owner must transfer at least 15 percent of the land to the 
municipality to build infrastructure, roads, and other urban 
amenities such as public spaces (including PGAs). Since most 
of these developments are deployed in regions with better 
infrastructure and higher income, it is no surprise that most 
PGAs created due to this process are in wealthier regions.

The findings demonstrate that even though Belo Horizonte 
has 10.09 m² of public green space per capita, distribution

privileged when it comes to the creation of public green

have reduced access to public green spaces (Dai 2011; 
Kaczynski et al. 2016, Zhang et al. 2021).

In the medium term, it would be interesting to develop policy 
instruments that prioritize poorer regions in the process of 
creation of new PGAs. As explained earlier, most PGAs 
are created due to land development by the private sector, 
so naturally, they are created in wealthier, better-equipped 
neighborhoods. A reform of the law that allows (or demands) 
developers to create public spaces in poorer regions, instead 
of inside the newly developed land, could be a possibility but 
would require national-level mobilization. Another option 
would be for the municipality to create a specific fund and 
allocate revenue for the creation of new PGAs in the regions 
more in need so that it does not depend on the private sector 
to develop new ones. Other criteria for consideration include 
the ecosystem and environmental protection and buffers 
against natural disasters. The results of this publication can be 
used to identify where new PGAs could be created to improve 
accessibility for those who need it most.
It is important to note that, in the last decades, habitational 
programs such as Minha Casa Minha Vida have incentivized 
the development of new homes by the private sector in 
poorer regions, thus generating new PGAs in low-income 
neighborhoods. Future studies can dive into that and, 
with data from the new Census available, identify if that 
change happened.
Over the long term, policies should be implemented that 
improve access to opportunities and strive to make cities more 
equitable. The most important actions include investing in 
affordable and reliable public transport for all and adopting 
transit-oriented development (TOD) strategies. This means 
denser development with mixed land use, especially around 
public transport corridors (Mahendra et al. 2021), and more 
affordable housing close to opportunities and transport hubs. 
Another suggestion for future studies is to identify which 
areas of the municipality are available for allocation of PGAs 
considering both accessibility and mitigation criteria. The 
Todos al Parque project, in the city of Barranquilla, Colombia, 
can be used as an inspiration. This program created or renewed 
several parks, giving priority to low-income regions and 
community participation (Maassen 2022).
Recommendations for future research are assessing 
accessibility on weekends (instead of weekdays) and analyzing 
aspects of accessibility such as travel costs, comfort, and safety. 
Qualitative aspects of the public green areas, such as their 
quality and attractiveness to users, can also be incorporated 
into the analysis. In addition to income, new research could 
examine how the racial, gender, or age compositions of 
neighborhoods influence access to PGAs, in an intersectional 
approach, as Bao et al. (2023) investigated the correlation 
with several vulnerability variables. These suggestions can 
enrich the analysis and contribute more to the formulation of 
public policies that improve the accessibility of people to these 
important urban amenities.
FINAL REMARKS  
AND CONCLUSION
This working paper examines accessibility to public green 
spaces for residents in Belo Horizonte, Brazil. Parks and 
squares undeniably improve the quality of life for city 
inhabitants, and make urban development more sustainable 
and resilient to climate change impacts, so urban planners 
should strive to provide equitable access to them.
In our study, a cumulative opportunities approach was used 
to evaluate how many PGAs can be assessed by walking, 
bicycling, and using public transport from different parts 
of the city. Ideally, all residents should be able to access 
recreational spaces using sustainable and accessible modes of 
transport. Data is not available to tell us how people actually 
travel to and from PGAs. We do not know, for instance, 
whether they travel to a PGA from their residence or their 
work location. Nor do we have data on the quality of these 
PGAs or transport modes. We relied on PGAs close to 
citizens’ residences as a proxy for general accessibility. Another 
limitation is the use of census data from 2010, more than 10 
years before the analysis, because these were the most updated 
data when the study was being conducted. A new census was 
applied in 2022, but the complete results were not released yet.
The main findings reveal that the wealthiest regions have 
the highest accessibility values and that people who live 
in the wealthiest regions can reach up to 5.4 times more 
PGAs than those in the least wealthy ones, depending on 
the type of analysis. The inequalities of accessibility values 
between income groups are more predominant for public 
transport than for bicycles, revealing that investments in 
public transport infrastructure and availability would be 
needed to improve access to PGAs for the most vulnerable 
groups. This does not exclude the fact that both pedestrian 
and cycling infrastructure should be encouraged since both 
are very cost-effective and impact cities positively. Safe bicycle 
infrastructure likely improves access, since it makes cycling 
safer, and  accessibility analyses are not always sensitive to that. 
Multiple policy implications follow from these findings and 
suggest ways to reduce the uncovered inequalities in access to 
PGAs. In the short term, this situation can be mitigated by 
investments, such as creating specific bus lines that connect 
neighborhoods with low access to green areas – especially 
Barreiro, Norte, and Nordeste. Specific policies aimed at 
improving conditions of access for women, children, and the 
elderly from those regions also need to be addressed, due to the 
natural mobility restrictions that these groups experience. Public 
transport availability on weekends is also important, as a large 
share of the population only has time to visit public green areas 
on these days. Improving streets, by redesigning them to favor 
pedestrians can have a great impact on urban life and encourage 
people to walk more to PGAs and other destinations.

ABOUT WRI BRASIL 
WRI Brasil is a trusted partner for change. Using research-
based approaches and unique coalitions, we work to meet 
people’s essential needs, to protect and restore nature, and to 
stabilize the climate. We combine technical excellence with 
political coordination and work in partnership with governments, 
companies, academia, and civil society.
