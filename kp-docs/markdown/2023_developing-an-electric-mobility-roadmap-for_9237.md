HIGHLIGHTS 
 ▪ Vietnam is beginning to implement its transition to green energy, and 
this paper evaluates lessons learned from eight case studies of e-mobil-
ity initiatives in: China, India, Indonesia, Malaysia, Mexico, Singapore, 
Taiwan, and Thailand. 
 ▪ Roadmaps can be structured around reducing emissions, identifying 
technology needs, and advancing the local e-mobility industry. They may 
include targets and goals, stakeholder analyses, consumer engagement, 
policy directives, performance monitoring, and timelines.
 ▪ Successful roadmaps are concrete, actionable, localized, and flex-
ible. Based on the good practices observed in the case studies, this 
paper proposes the following recommendations based on these four 
characteristics:
 ▪ A concrete roadmap has clear timelines, targets, related actions, and 
incentives: These elements are then assigned to specific stakeholders 
to make a roadmap actionable. 
 ▪ A localized roadmap considers the country’s economic and social 
development, political structures, and climate ambitions outside the 
e-mobility field, but it should also be flexible enough to allow trial and 
error to occur through pilots, monitoring, and scaling. 
 ▪ After implementation begins, a roadmap’s concreteness also allows 
national governments to measure progress toward achieving targets.
EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
About this paper
This paper looks at successful e-mobility case studies and summarizes good 
practices of developing and implementing an e-mobility roadmap, from 
effective subsidies to engagement. Based on these observations, this paper 
offers recommendations for Vietnam’s upcoming electric mobility roadmap, 
so that the country can smoothly transition and decarbonize its trans-
portation sector.

Key findings
From the start, including the right stakeholders is crucial 
for developing an e-mobility roadmap. Broad consulta-
tions and collaboration with research institutions and 
relevant ministries are key to effective implementation and 
desired outcomes.
By making e-mobility information accessible online, 
countries can accelerate national transportation preferences 
toward electric and low-emission modes. India’s one-stop-
shop website for e-mobility buyers and businesses illustrates 
how to effectively engage the public.
T o efficiently decarbonize transportation, national govern-
ments should prioritize the most predominant vehicle type 
for electrification, be it government fleet, transit buses, or 
motorcycles. China prioritized transit buses while Taiwan did 
so with motorcycles.
Incorporating hybrid electric vehicles in an e-mobility 
roadmap may smooth the process to eventual full electri-
fication. Most case studies have considered this transitional 
fuel technology in their roadmaps, but Malaysia is the only 
country that clearly positioned plug-in hybrid vehicles as a 
technological steppingstone.
The supply of batteries has a huge impact on the cost of and 
demand for electric vehicles (EVs); therefore countries need 
to proactively engage battery manufacturers in the roadmap 
development process. Anticipating the growing demand for 
EVs, India, for instance, is investing US$2.5 billion to ramp 
up battery manufacturing (Singh et al. 2022). 
National governments seeking to promote EV ownership 
should not neglect the need for EV charging. Singapore’s 
e-mobility strategy doubles down on installing charging 
points in multi-dwelling buildings across the country, be they 
public housing or private residences.
T o accelerate the shift toward e-mobility, national govern-
ments should offer subsidies and operational incentives to 
make e-mobility more attractive. Subsidies should lower the 
up-front costs of EVs, and operational incentives can make 
operating and riding EVs more convenient than internal 
combustion engine vehicles (ICEVs). 
Similarly, subnational governments should offer comple-
mentary e-mobility subsidies and incentives to help advance 
the national roadmap. Regional, provincial, and municipal 
governments should offer their own purchase subsidies to 
help EVs reach cost parity with ICEVs. They should also 
implement zero-emission zones in their jurisdictions to disin-
centivize fossil-fuel-based mobility.
INTRODUCTION
Background 
Responding to the urgent impacts of climate change, Vietnam 
adopted the Paris Agreement in 2016 and set out an ambi-
tious political pledge to achieve net-zero emissions in 2050 
during COP26 (CAT 2020; UNFCCC 2021; Climate Watch 
n.d.; UNFCCC n.d.).1 
Nationally determined contribution (NDC) 
planning for Vietnam 
At the heart of the Paris Agreement, NDCs embody specific 
national action on reducing greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions 
and adapting to climate change (UNFCCC 2022). In 2020, 
Vietnam updated its NDC after a three-year comprehensive 
consultation process with various stakeholders (NDC Partner-
ship 2020; UNFCCC 2020). Vietnam’s updated NDC made 
an unconditional commitment to reduce greenhouse gases by 
9 percent below business-as-usual levels by 2030, and set a 
conditional goal to reduce them by 27 percent with interna-
tional support (UNDP 2019).2 Emissions from transportation 
were included as a policy target under these NDCs (UNDP 
2019). The Ministry of Transport (MOT) is collaborating 
with domestic and international partners to prepare a strategy 
that will include the adoption of electric mobility (e-mobility) 
as a key solution (Department of Environment 2020). In 
addition to setting net-zero targets specific to the transporta-
tion sector, Vietnam is also planning a supplemental roadmap 
that aims to phase out the production, assembly, and import of 
all fossil fuel vehicles by 2050.
Vietnam’s plan to adopt e-mobility
In July 2022, Vietnam approved the Action Program on 
Green Energy Transformation, a significant step toward 
dissecting and clarifying the country’s commitment to net 
zero by 2050 (Nguyen 2022; Fox 2022).3 Leading to the 
half-century mark, the action program sets up various targets 
to decarbonize road transportation (see Appendix A, Table 
A-1).4 These targets place a notable emphasis on public trans-
portation: They call for not only 100 percent nationwide bus 
electrification by 2025, but also ensuring that nearly half of all 
trips in the country’s capital city, Hanoi, are taken by public 
transportation by 2030 (Fox 2022; VIR 2022). This shows 
that the action program encompasses two components of the 
Avoid-Shift-Improve (A-S-I) framework of environmental 
sustainability (see Glossary): the “shift” (public transporta-
tion adoption target) and the “improve” (bus electrification). 
By 2050, the full electrification goal will extend to taxis (Fox 
2022). What seems to be missing overall is clarity on the 
“avoid” component of the A-S-I framework, which involves 
promoting land use and urban development that prevents

or moped. The latter two vehicle types, known as two-
wheelers, are the dominant transportation mode serving the 
country’s travel demand (Tran et al. 2022).5
Going beyond transportation, the action program does not 
contain specific objectives or actions for renewable electricity, 
but there is great potential to integrate transportation and 
energy decarbonization. Vietnam’s investments in renewable 
energy almost doubled in 2020, and the country’s forecasted 
renewable capacity between 2020 and 2023 dwarfs other 
Southeast Asian countries (Gonçalves and Kaldjian 2021). 
Capturing its remarkable progress and momentum, Vietnam 
should not only develop goals, policy instruments, and financ-
ing mechanisms for energy decarbonization, but also link 
them to the transportation decarbonization ones in the action 
program (Fox 2022; SuM4All 2022).
Although the action program is an important milestone for 
Vietnam to decarbonize its transportation sector and fulfills 
its climate commitment, it needs to be supplemented by 
an e-mobility roadmap that will serve as a guide to realize 
the action program’s ambitious targets. The action program 
document says a roadmap is necessary “for mobilizing sources 
and ensures the practicability, effectiveness, and sustainability 
during the implementation” of the green energy transition 
(Prime Minister of Vietnam 2022). The roadmap is expected 
to be finalized in 2023 based on the planned activities of the 
NDC-Transport Initiative for Asia (NDC-TIA) program, 
which conceptualized this paper.
This paper aims to draw good practices from various e-mobil-
ity case studies to provide recommendations on Vietnam’s 
upcoming e-mobility roadmap. The paper examines ways that 
Vietnam can develop and implement effective strategies to 
build adequate charging infrastructure, promote the manufac-
turing of EVs, and phase out production of ICEVs. 
E-mobility roadmaps: definition  
and importance
Rapid innovations have allowed wide-scale adoption 
of e-mobility in recent decades (Gersdorf et al. 2021). 
E-mobility has been sparking consumer and industry inter-
est. The term refers to increasing access to EVs and charging 
infrastructure and optimizing the transportation system to 
reduce emissions (Hall et al. 2019; IEA 2021b; WRI 2021b). 
Governments have enacted policies to reduce transportation 
emissions, which account for nearly a quarter of CO2 emis-
sions. An e-mobility roadmap is the most comprehensive 
pathway for national and subnational governments (IEA 
2021b; SLOCAT Partnership 2020). 
An effective roadmap, following the framework developed by 
IEA in 2014, outlines steps and relationships to achieve out-
comes and goals and often includes metrics and milestones for 
monitoring progress (IEA 2014). This paper presents a process 
for building a well-organized e-mobility roadmap as follows: 
 ▪ Phase 1—Roadmap planning and preparation: Identify 
the scope and general approach of the e-mobility roadmap 
and map out the relevant stakeholders who will be leading 
its development.
 ▪ Phase 2—Roadmap visioning: Define targets and a 
vision for the roadmap, determining the key outcomes and 
optimal trajectory for e-mobility adoption (e.g., whether 
to favor transitional fuel technology like hybrid vehicles or 
leapfrog to battery EVs completely). 
 ▪ Phase 3—Roadmap development: Use baseline analyses 
to inform high-level electrification and emissions-
reduction scenarios and impact analyses and then develop 
the national roadmap based on the result.
 ▪ Phase 4—Roadmap implementation, monitoring, 
and revision: Engage experts to assess and reassess 
roadmap timeline and priorities, develop mechanisms to 
monitor roadmap development progress, and revise in a 
timely fashion.
There is no standard e-mobility roadmap, but it has the fol-
lowing common components: 
 ▪ Stakeholder Analysis: To make the e-mobility roadmap 
as comprehensive as possible, it is essential to identify 
and engage stakeholders who are embedded within the 
government, industry, utilities, and other sectors, so 
that they contribute their experience and expertise to 
the roadmap’s implementation. Therefore, a stakeholder 
analysis that identifies who needs to be involved and what 
role they will play is a crucial starting point. 
 ▪ Situational Analysis: A situational analysis includes a 
thorough assessment of the country, particularly its energy 
and transportation sectors. This may include general 
transportation profiles, energy system characteristics, and 
existing policies and plans related to e-mobility. It would 
also be helpful to collaborate with international and 
local technical experts when determining country-level 
situational analysis parameters and model design.6 
 ▪ Clear Targets and Timelines: A roadmap with clearly 
mapped-out goals, targets, and milestones is effective 
and comprehensive for further strategic planning and 
implementation. The goals and targets can be set based 
on a country’s roadmap perspective, such as a climate 
perspective, a technology perspective, or a vehicle and

industry perspective. Most importantly, these targets and 
timelines should demonstrate how they are aligning with 
the Paris Agreement as well as the NDC.
 ▪ Scenario Analysis: Scenario analysis assesses the potential 
impact of different vehicle electrification scenarios and 
informs tailored solutions accordingly. The generated 
projections would help decision-makers identify the best 
set of fiscal and nonfiscal policies under different scenarios 
and potential problems, such as increased demand in 
the power grid. 
 ▪ Policy Priorities and Timelines: Policy priorities and 
timelines are necessary for an effective roadmap as they 
provide guidance for overall e-mobility development. 
This section can either be general with an overarching 
direction or detailed with specific action items that can be 
implemented immediately.
Roadmaps are essential to guide e-mobility growth and adop-
tion, bringing positive impacts to the environment, health, 
and the economy. The roadmaps should align with a country’s 
NDCs and sustainable development goals. Because a unique 
set of conditions exists for each country, this study goes 
beyond common steps, analyzing the relationships among the 
lead government ministry (usually policymakers and technical 
experts), e-mobility users (including private consumers and 
public service providers), and e-mobility suppliers (of both 
vehicles and charging) (Figure 1).

exhibit some degree of similarity to Vietnam.

Summary of case studies  
and their roadmaps
Each case study has a unique e-mobility status quo (Tables 
3 and 4). Malaysia, Singapore, and Taiwan pledged to reach 
net-zero by 2060 like Vietnam. Vietnam is most like Thailand 
in expected percentage of emissions reduction. Both Viet-
nam and Singapore have the Ministry of Transport leading 
e-mobility roadmap development, emphasizing transportation 
infrastructure. Vietnam, like China, India, and Taiwan, set a 
market share for common vehicle modes but is unique in set-
ting a 100 percent electrification target for taxis.

The following descriptions detail the eight case studies, 
their similarity to Vietnam, and their e-mobility roadmaps, 
emphasizing the roadmaps’ key objectives and the government 
agencies that developed them.
China 
When it comes to e-mobility, China has two roadmaps with 
different focuses. The ministry that developed them, the 
Ministry of Industry and Information Technology (MIIT), 
dedicated one roadmap to build up the e-mobility industry 
and the other to advance e-mobility technology. The roadmaps 
complement each other.
The New Energy Vehicle Industry Development Plan 
(2021‒2035) is an industry-focused roadmap led by four min-
istries, including MIIT and the National Development and 
Reform Commission (NDRC) (ICCT 2021). The NDRC is a 
main legislative body that oversees economic and social poli-
cies (UN.ESCAP 2012). These four main ministries and other 
supporting entities are actively working together as a coalition 
to comprehensively transform the e-mobility industry (State 
Office of China 2020). 
Shifting the spotlight from the automotive industry to 
e-mobility technology, China’s other roadmap, the Energy-
Saving and New Energy Vehicles Technology Roadmap 
2.0, is a technology-focused roadmap (MIIT China 2020). 
Under the guidance of MIIT , the China Society of Automo-
tive Engineers (SAE), a nonprofit academic organization, 
consulted 1,000 experts from the automobile, energy, trans-
portation, and information communication industries for this 
roadmap (CCTP 2017; SAE-China 2020). Based on expert 
inputs, the Technology Roadmap 2.0 aims to promote the 
“lightweighting” of vehicles-material technology research, 
such as carbon fiber composites, that can reduce the weight 
and subsequently the energy consumption of NEVs (Research 
and Markets 2019).7 
China’s centralized government has been able to ensure and 
track progress toward achieving e-mobility targets defined in 
the roadmaps. Not only are they part of the country’s top-level 
policy blueprint for its national economic and social devel-
opment, but they are also supported by China’s 1+N policy 
system for carbon peaking by 2030 (Ibold 2021). The 1+N 
policy system includes policies on reducing fossil-fuel energy

consumption, which set an example for Vietnam because the 
energy mixes of China and Vietnam share a similar ratio of 
fossil fuel to renewable sources (IEA 2022a).
India 
Rather than a specific ministry, a government policy think 
tank, the National Institution for Transforming India (NITI 
Aayog), develops both the e-mobility roadmap and India’s 
flagship e-mobility subsidy program, the revised Faster 
Adoption and Manufacturing of Hybrid and EV scheme 
(FAME II). This makes India’s case unique. In March 2019, 
NITI Aayog created the country’s e-mobility roadmap, 
National Mission for Transformative Mobility and Bat-
tery Storage (NITI Aayog 2019). A day later, the National 
Mission announced the FAME II subsidy program, aiming 
to both expand EV adoption throughout the country and 
also develop India’s burgeoning EV industry and value chain 
(NAB India 2019). 
Like China, India adopted a multistakeholder approach when 
it comes to promoting e-mobility. However, unlike China, 
India assigned a government-affiliated policy commission, 
NITI Aayog, to lead. Like the process chart shown in Figure 
1, NITI Aayog is supported by a steering committee, consist-
ing of representatives from the energy, industry, technology, 
and transportation ministries. A similar interministerial 
committee oversees the subsidy program. Evident in both the 
National Mission roadmap and FAME II is India’s commit-
ment to provide nonfiscal and fiscal incentives supporting 
the EV ecosystem—namely, the vehicles, the infrastructure, 
and the industry.
Implementing the FAME II scheme will depend on state 
and territory governments offering both nonfiscal and fiscal 
incentives in addition to the national ones. These include 
waivers of road taxes, permit and toll exemptions, reduced 
registration fees, and access to preferential parking for EVs. 
Fortunately, NITI Aayog is a government agency structured to 
engage and support India’s subnational governments, and this 
makes the linkage of incentives straightforward (NITI Aayog 
2021). Vietnam could learn from India’s example by mobi-
lizing subnational governments to develop complementary 
e-mobility incentives. The two countries are similar in their 
carbon intensity of road transportation and have an identi-
cal 10 percent share of emissions coming from the overall 
transportation sector (CAIT 2021; IEA 2022a).
Indonesia
Indonesia lacks a national e-mobility roadmap but seeks to 
engage nongovernmental organizations (NGOs) and civil 
society organizations to support the EV transition. In 2017, 
Indonesia’s General Plan for National Energy set out the 
targets to adopt 2,200 electric and hybrid cars and 2.1 million 
electric two-wheelers (E2W) by 2025. In addition to fleet 
targets, Indonesia has also proposed EV production goals 
for 2025 and 2030 in its National Energy Grand Strategy 
(2019). Presidential Regulation No. 55 (2019) requires various 
ministries to each create their own implementing regulations 
to help reach these goals (Government of Indonesia 2019; 
Mahalana and Yang 2021). These ministries oversee Indo-
nesia’s economy, energy, environment, finance, industry, and 
transportation sectors. 
Despite the absence of a national e-mobility roadmap, Indo-
nesia is taking many steps to promote the production of EVs, 
such as introducing financial incentives for producers. How-
ever, because Indonesia lacks a national e-mobility roadmap, 
there is no vision to guide different government ministries 
to develop cross-cutting and comprehensive policies. In the 
near future, Vietnam should pay close attention to Indonesia’s 
policies on E2Ws as the two countries have extremely similar 
levels of two-wheeler ownership, both around 400 two-wheel-
ers per 1,000 people (GSO Vietnam 2018; MOT Indonesia 
2021; Vietnam Register 2020; The World Bank 2022).
Malaysia
In 2021, Malaysia’s Ministry of Environment and Water 
created the Low Carbon Mobility Blueprint, a national 
e-mobility roadmap for the country that includes targets for 
producing EVs by 2030, as well as for installing more charg-
ing points. However, it should be noted that this national 
e-mobility roadmap lacks a clear framework explaining 
how the country plans to achieve its goals. Furthermore, the 
Ministry of International Trade and Industry has been the 
only ministry to implement substantive policies to pro-
mote EV uptake in Malaysia. In the National Automotive 
Policy 2020, the ministry implemented policies to promote 
battery manufacturing and develop an EV Smart Grid 
Interoperability Center.
Malaysia’s ability to engage in transportation electrification 
is significantly impeded by its EV roadmap’s failure to detail 
how the country plans to achieve its targets. So far, Malaysia

Singapore 
Singapore’s E-Mobility Technology Roadmap is a technol-

(L TA) and researched by the Energy Research Institute 
of Nanyang Technology University. Commissioned by the 
National Research Foundation and National Climate Change 
Secretariat, the roadmap was part of a series of technology 
roadmaps on energy and climate change–related technologies. 
These roadmaps were researched and developed in conjunc-
tion with government agencies, the research community, and 
the private sector with the purpose of identifying e-mobility 
technology needs and guiding future policy development. 
Singapore’s roadmap is an example of how research institu-
tions and the government can partner together to identify 
challenges and explore solutions. The government-research 
institution partnership continues to collaborate on roadmap 
implementation and overseeing how policies recommended in 
the roadmap are working on the ground. Examples of imple-

EVs and helping the private sector expand charging infra-
structure, both evident in Singapore’s latest Green Plan 2030. 
The energy consumption of Singapore’s road transportation 
has a similar carbon intensity compared to that of Vietnam’s; 
both national and subnational governments in Vietnam can 
learn from the city-state’s existing practices.
Taiwan
The first phase of Taiwan’s EV National Program was 
launched in 2000 by Taiwan’s Ministry of Economic Affairs, 
as well as the Taiwan Environmental Protection Agency. It 
was solely concerned with promoting electric motorcycle 
and moped technological development, including supporting 
innovation in electric propulsion and battery systems (Shu et 
al. 2009). In 2009, Taiwan initiated a second, more industry-
focused phase of the EV National Program, which aimed to 
increase E2W sales by using financial incentives, encouraging 
business investments, and establishing an industry value chain. 
While the 2017 Air Pollution Control Action Plan (2017) 
first set a goal to electrify all government vehicles and public 
buses by 2030, it was reiterated in the most recent action plan, 
“Taiwan’s Pathway to Net-Zero Emissions in 2050,” published 
in March 2022 (NDC Taiwan 2022). 
Taiwan is unique among the cases studied in that its gov-
ernment has been subsidizing EV technology research and 
development since 1993, particularly regarding E2W tech-
nologies (Shu et al. 2009). These early investments helped 
Taiwan’s EV landscape gain an early advantage. To build 
on these technological advances, the second National EV 
Program pivoted to promote industry-led adoption. By col-
laborating with industry to develop EV business models and 
establish an industry value chain, Taiwan is well positioned to 
experience rapid transportation electrification in the coming 
years, especially now under its new 2050 net-zero pathway. 
Vietnam should absorb lessons from the evolution of E2W 
technologies and industry from Taiwan. In both cases, the 
two-wheeler ownership level is high, and the carbon inten-
sity of road transportation is almost identical, at 71 grams 
of carbon dioxide per terajoule (gCO2/TJ) (IEA 2022a; 
MOI Taiwan 2022; National Statistics, Republic of China 
[Taiwan] 2021).

setting EV production goals for 2025‒2035. The roadmap 
concentrates on EV supporting infrastructure (specifi-

Vietnam
By creating a comprehensive national e-mobility roadmap, 
Vietnam’s Ministry of Transport can help the country build on 
its progress with EVs and take the next step toward transi-
tioning to a decarbonized transportation sector. This roadmap 
should make sure to address Vietnam’s need to expand public 
charging networks, increase its electric power-generation 
capacity to support these charging networks, and ramp up EV 
production capacity in the country. If Vietnam can success-
fully address these issues, it will be well-positioned to achieve 
large-scale transportation electrification.
Differences among case study roadmaps
The eight case studies reviewed demonstrate different 
approaches to accelerating e-mobility adoption, such as build-
ing a robust national e-mobility industry (India and Thailand), 
reducing emissions and air pollution (Mexico and Malaysia), 
and propelling e-mobility technology advancements (Singa-
pore). Two case studies, China and Taiwan, adopted a hybrid 
approach that advances both the e-mobility industry and 
technologies. In each case, roadmaps serve as foundational 
documents that connect e-mobility consumers, e-mobility 
suppliers, and the national government. 
The case studies provide a diverse set of national govern-
ment types, each with unique social, climate, and economic 
characteristics that heavily influenced its e-mobility roadmaps 
or national energy-transportation policies. Other factors, 
such as each national government’s willingness to receive aid 
and engage with stakeholders, also played a role in shaping 
e-mobility roadmaps. 
Political systems and economic interests and whether they 
align or clash can have an enormous impact on whether and 
how quickly countries can reach e-mobility objectives. For 
China, e-mobility presents the golden opportunity to tackle 
the country’s strategic priorities. The explosive growth of EVs 
in China reflects the country’s clear national strategies and 
plans, including the e-mobility roadmaps ( Jin et al. 2021). 
Several factors motivated the Chinese government’s concrete 
efforts to promote e-mobility: reducing oil import dependency, 
leading the global auto industry, mitigating air pollution, and 
combating climate change (He and Jin 2021; Stauffer 2020). 
To drive the shift toward e-mobility, China benefited from its 
centralized government structure and had key stakeholders all 
pushed in the same direction ( Jin et al. 2021).
This was far less easy for Mexico. Mexico’s experience shows 
how misaligned government policies and economic interests 
can obstruct the shift toward e-mobility. In Mexico, the 
petroleum industry and ICE automotive industry both make 
significant economic contributions and wield considerable 
political influence. Earnings from the oil industry accounted 
for 58 percent of government revenues in 2021, while the 
automotive sector is also one of Mexico’s largest industries, 
employing over 1 million workers (ITA 2021). EV produc-
tion in Mexico is still in its infancy, appearing either as 
start-up test products or in planned foreign investments, and 
it is already lagging behind from planned production targets 
( Johnson 2022; Madry 2022; Wayland 2021). Worries over 
undermining the established petroleum and ICE automo-
tive industries influenced deliberations and likely tilted the 
focus toward public transportation electrification in Mexico’s 
e-mobility roadmap. 
Differences among countries mean that some of the good 
practices and recommendations detailed in this paper may 
need to be adapted and may work better in some con-
texts than others.

Roadmap authors
The e-mobility ecosystem is complex and involves myriad 
stakeholders. Governments should play a central role in 
issuing an e-mobility roadmap, but research institutions 
also have a role to play by informing roadmap creation with 
scientific evidence. 
In all case studies, a government agency, often at the min-
isterial level, issues the roadmaps and relevant e-mobility 
strategies. In addition to the anchor government agency, 
roadmap development should include relevant government 
agencies that match the roadmap’s focus. For example, if a 
roadmap’s focus is on developing a country’s e-mobility indus-
try, then it makes sense to include ministries that oversee the 
heavy industries or the economy as roadmap authors, as in the 
case of India. Depending on the focus, relevant government 
agencies can include those in charge of the economy, energy, 
environment, finance, industry, technology, and transportation.
However, national governments alone, led by the executive 
branch, cannot guarantee a sound e-mobility roadmap because 
it also depends on solid research evidence on the transition to 
e-mobility. Research institutions can conduct e-mobility cost-
benefit analysis and emission-scenario modeling, helping the 
government agencies determine pragmatic e-mobility targets, 
the extent of e-mobility’s benefits (e.g., emissions reduction 
and air quality improvement), and the negative externalities 
of e-mobility. 
Mexico’s example illustrates how to involve relevant govern-
ment agencies and research institutions. Mexico’s Secretary 
of Environment and Natural Resources (SEMARNAT) led 
the development of the country’s e-mobility roadmap in 2018, 
and it focused on emissions reduction and air quality improve-
ment as a result. The roadmap’s development also involved 
other federal government agencies, such as the Department 
of Energy and the Department of Economy (Undersecretary 
of Planning and Environmental Policy 2018). Among them, 
the participation of the Secretariat of Treasury and Public 
Credit and the Secretariat of Economy will be instrumental in 
implementing the exemption of import and export duties for 
EVs. Additionally, the SEMARNAT roadmap drew from the 
research of government-affiliated research institutions, includ-
ing the National Institute of Ecology and Climate Change, 
and listed international research institutions as co-authors, 
such as the World Resources Institute (WRI) México (Under-
secretary of Planning and Environmental Policy 2018). 
Consumer education and outreach
Governments should carry out consumer education and 
outreach as part of the roadmap development and imple-
mentation process. The goal of education and outreach is to 
promote e-mobility options as attractive (a superior driving or 
riding experience), accessible (easy to operate and affordable), 
and advantageous (great for the environment) for consum-
ers. Through education and outreach, governments can make 
e-mobility information accessible (online information portal, 
media marketing campaign) and deploy e-mobility in real-
world scenarios (test-drive events, branded environment for 
EV charging and parking). India’s experience illustrates the 
benefits of having a national e-mobility portal while Singa-
pore strategically funded an EV car-share pilot that turned 
into the second largest car-share service in world.
Public information portals for e-mobility usually offer 
information on purchase subsidies or charging stations, like 
Switch Delhi. The national government of India, however, 
took it a step further by establishing e-AMRIT , a “one-stop-
shop” portal that provides comprehensive financing options 
for EVs.9 Consumers can find financiers based on the type of 
loan (personal or commercial) and the type of vehicle (cars, 
two-wheelers, three-wheelers, buses). Because one of the main 
barriers to adoption of EVs is their high up-front costs, by 
making financing options accessible, India will empower more 
consumers to switch to e-mobility (Government of India and 
NITI Aayog n.d.).
Singapore established successful pilot EV programs. They 
are conducive to making e-mobility accessible to the public 
because participants get to experience e-mobility for everyday 
use by riding or operating EVs while traveling between home 
and work. In addition, compared to private EVs, vehicles 
from pilot EV programs are not limited to one person or 
household because they are available to the public for hire for 
an extended amount of time, such as a few weeks or months. 
Singapore’s most notable pilot EV program to date started 
in 2017. In partnership with a private car-sharing company 
BlueSG, the L TA and Singapore’s Economic Development 
Board launched a car-sharing scheme to test the viability of 
EV fleets (Land Transport Authority 2014). In 2020, BlueSG 
became the second largest electric car-sharing service in the 
world, boasting 667 shared EVs and 1,487 charging sta-
tions (Salim 2020).

During the visioning stage
After understanding consumer sentiment toward e-mobility, 
roadmap authors can start working toward an e-mobility 
vision that will guide the roadmap. Many types of vehicles can 
be electrified, so it is important to understand which ones are 
the most critical and suitable for electrification. For instance, 
roadmaps can promote transitional technologies that can be 
deployed as a first step toward full electrification, and they can 
(and should) prioritize electrification of the vehicles with the 
greatest potential to reduce emissions.

targets, and policy levers for promoting hybrid EVs along with 
the promotion of BEVs in an EV roadmap with the eventual 
promise of full electrification.

Vehicle mode to prioritize: public vehicle fleet
Compared to electrifying private vehicles, electrifying public 
vehicle fleets, including public buses and government vehicle 
fleets, is low-hanging fruit with cascading benefits. The elec-
trification of government vehicle fleets and public buses can 
catalyze wider e-mobility adoption by kick-starting market 
acceptance and developing a viable business model for local 
EV manufacturing (KASA 2021). Many countries in the case 
studies have addressed the electrification of public transporta-
tion or government fleets in their roadmaps. While China is 
aiming to achieve full electrification in the public transporta-
tion sector by 2035, Thailand set an ambitious target in which 
all vehicles procured for government agencies and public fleets 
will be zero-emission by 2025.
Vehicle mode to prioritize: electric two-
wheelers (E2Ws)
Motorcycles are popular in many Southeast Asian countries 
(including all case studies in this paper) and occupy less space 
than cars (National Statistics, Republic of China [Taiwan] 
2021; World Health Organization 2015). Targeting them 
for electrification can have a major impact. For example, a 10 
percent shift from gas to electric motorcycles in Ho Chi Minh 
City could cut emissions by an equivalent of 1 million tonnes 
of carbon dioxide (tCO2e) (Huu and Ngoc 2021). Aligning 
e-mobility with the dominant transportation mode is essential 
for an efficient transition to sustainable transportation.
Taiwan has been a leader in electric motorcycle technology 
and has taken concrete steps in its roadmap to prioritize the 
uptake of E2Ws. Not only has Taiwan invested in electric 
propulsion and battery system technology development, but 
it also offers significant purchase subsidies to consumers for 
e-scooters. Buyers of E2Ws in Taiwan can apply for national 
and local subsidies as part of the country’s scrappage program, 
encouraging the replacement of older, more polluting motor-
cycles with newer models (Chung 2021; Taiwan EPA 2022). 
These subsidies have spurred relatively rapid electrification 
of Taiwan’s two-wheeler market, with 10 percent of two-
wheelers sold in 2020 being electric (Chung 2021). Having 
a national EV roadmap focused specifically on E2Ws has 
enabled Taiwan to successfully catalyze its e-mobility.
However, the noteworthy benefits and major uptake of E2Ws 
also have unintended consequences, such as hampering 
efforts to promote public transportation and active mobility 
(e.g., walking and cycling) due to the substitution effect: The 
more people adopt E2Ws, the less they are inclined to adopt 
public transportation and active mobility. Moreover, operating 
two-wheelers has higher injury and fatality rates, compared 
to other modes of transportation (Bouaoun et al. 2015; Sood 
1988). Therefore, it is important to evaluate electrification’s 
impact on other modes of transportation and balance it with 
other modal shift strategies that encourage people to walk, 
bike, and use public transportation more often (Weiss et al. 
2015). This way, countries can holistically apply the A-S-I 
framework to maximize the advantages of zero-emission 
transportation (L. Castillo 2021).

Operational incentives 
E-mobility can be incentivized with access to roadways, 
parking, and places of interest. Preference and exemptions for 
EVs over ICE vehicles, such as “green” plates for road access 
privilege in Mexico and preferential parking policies in China, 
can encourage the transition to e-mobility (SEMARNAT 
2018; State Office of China 2020).
For access to places of interest, governments can create low 
or zero-emission zones that effectively ban more pollut-
ing vehicles and grant preferential access to non-motorized 
transportation, EVs, hybrid vehicles, and FCEVs. These zones’ 
restrictions can either apply to all vehicles or just commercial 
vehicles for freight deliveries (Steimer et al. 2022). Many cities 
around the world, like Shenzhen in China, are already imple-
menting these zones; however, when implemented nationally 
as a coordinated program, these policies resulted in freight 
companies upgrading their commercial vehicles to electric 
(Cruz and Montenon 2016). 
Vehicle registration is another opportunity for governments 
to incentivize private EV adoption. For countries that are 
seeking to boost domestic demand for EVs and that have a 
strict limit on the vehicle population, exempting EV owners 
from registration quotas can be very effective in boosting EV 
penetration. Six major cities in China have used registration 
privileges to boost EV adoption (He et al. 2018). However, 
countries pursuing sustainable transportation through public 
and shared modes should be aware that easing registration 
promotes more private vehicle ownership. Once EV adop-
tion is kick-started, countries that have already adopted this 
practice can tighten vehicle registration once again.

EV charging provision and standards 
Many countries face the chicken-and-egg dilemma concern-
ing EV ownership and charging infrastructure: whether they 
need more EVs on the road to warrant additional charging 
infrastructure or whether they need a more robust charging 
infrastructure system to encourage people to buy EVs. While 
Singapore tackles this by fostering public-private partnerships, 
India and Taiwan are opting for battery swapping as a more 
economical alternative to plug-in charging.
While Singapore’s EV and charging infrastructure markets 
are still developing, the Singapore government has decided 
on a strategy to resolve the chicken-and-egg dilemma. Its 
solution was to grow EV ownership while adopting a regula-
tory framework that encourages private-sector investment 
in charging infrastructure. In addition, Singapore is notable 
in its focus on providing charging stations for EV drivers 
living in multi-dwelling buildings. The newer climate-focused 
roadmap, SG Green Plan, sets to achieve eight EV-Ready 
Towns with charger installation at all public housing carparks 
by 2025 and 60,000 charging points nationwide by 2030 (SG 
Green Plan 2021).
To power an EV, charging stations supply electrical power 
through either direct current or alternating current while 
battery-swapping stations exchange a spent battery with a 
charged one. The latter technology can accelerate the shift 
to E2Ws because it is cost-, time-, and space-efficient in 
select Asian countries like India and Taiwan (Gode et al. 
2022; Randall 2022). The private sector, such as two-wheeler 
manufacturer Gogoro, has been driving battery-swapping ini-
tiatives seen in the case studies. However, governments should 
promote battery swapping in their e-mobility roadmaps and 
develop battery-swapping standards to better regulate this 
emerging technology.
During the implementation stage
Goal setting
Ambitious and evidence-based goal setting in an e-mobility 
roadmap can lay the groundwork for effective implementation. 
This is true, regardless of government structure or e-mobility 
objective. In all case studies examined in this research, 
countries have set goals for private EV adoption. A handful 
have incorporated charging, public fleet electrification, and/or 
emission-reduction goals. Thailand’s roadmap is laudable for 
the comprehensiveness of its targets.
Thailand’s National EV Roadmap (2021‒2035) set EV 
stock and manufacture targets for different vehicle segments, 
including public transit buses and government fleets, and the 
number and density of charging stations. The overall target for 
the country is to have 6.22 million EVs on the road by 2030 
and to produce only zero-emission vehicles by 2035 (ASEAN 
Secretariat 2020). Many other roadmaps also set overall EV 
targets, but what sets Thailand’s apart is the inclusion of both a 
manufacture and an ownership goal (Ploymee 2021). In addi-
tion, the ownership goal breaks down into specific numbers 
for cars, motorcycles, buses, and trucks. Since the roadmap 
was released in 2020, the number of registered battery EVs in 
Thailand has grown more than sixfold, from 3,682 to 24,570 
in two and a half years (Amir 2020; Transportation Statistics 
Group 2020; 2022).
Monitor roadmap progress
Successful roadmap execution requires the implementing 
government ministries to regularly monitor and track imple-
mentation progress. Continued progress monitoring and 
updates to objectives are critical for national governments to 
speed up transportation decarbonization and step up their 
responses to the climate crisis. By incorporating specific 
e-mobility targets with systematic progress monitoring (i.e., 
quarterly, yearly, etc.), national governments can track and 
measure macro trends and adjust roadmap strategies to further 
accelerate e-mobility at the national level.
The evolution of India’s e-mobility subsidy programs, FAME 
scheme, phases I and II, is a great example of how monitor-
ing can help roadmaps revise and adapt to socioeconomic 
conditions. After Phase I, an inter-ministerial committee, the 
Project Implementation and Sanctioning Committee (PISC) 
was founded, and one of its responsibilities is reviewing 
demand incentive annually based on prices and technology 
trends of EV components (Department of Heavy Indus-
try 2019).13 The PISC consists of representatives from the 
Ministry of Power and the Ministry of Road Transport. The 
PISC was instrumental in advancing the cost parity between 
electric motorcycles and ICE motorcycles half a decade earlier

RECOMMENDATIONS 
Instead of defining a prescribed way of developing an EV 
roadmap, this paper offers recommendations based on four 
common attributes of successful roadmaps: They are concrete, 
actionable, localized, and flexible.
Making a roadmap concrete
Concrete roadmaps have clear timelines and targets based on 
their vision and goals. For instance, clear and tangible targets 
can form concrete objectives for governments to work toward 
EV adoption. Depending on the intended outcomes of an 
e-mobility roadmap, targets can be from a general EV adop-
tion and climate perspective, such as having a certain number 
of electric freight vehicles and public fleet vehicles in circula-
tion by a certain year. Targets can also be from a technology 
perspective as well, such as imposing a minimum powertrain 
efficiency for all new EVs by a certain year.
An action timeline should contain clear, measurable mile-
stones like interim targets or key outcomes leading to 
e-mobility targets. Ideally, it should also detail responsibility 
for different stakeholders to provide maximum clarity and 
actionability. 
There is also an abundance of vehicle segments under 
e-mobility, including different fuel technologies, such as plug-
in hybrid vehicles (PHEVs) and conventional hybrid vehicles 
(HEVs). A country needs to specify whether and which 
transitional fuel technology (PHEVs or HEVs), is part of its 
roadmap’s vision and, afterward, align the e-mobility incen-
tives with it. While hybrid vehicles offer a smoother transition 
to e-mobility, heavily incentivizing them may interfere with a 
country’s climate emission-reduction targets in the long run.
Given the prevalence of private vehicles, every country road-
map analyzed in this paper has set electrification targets for 
them. However, countries cannot neglect the importance of 
creating specific targets for public buses and freight vehicles, 
which can guarantee demand for certain EVs and boost the 
confidence of manufacturers. 
 ▪ For example, Thailand’s roadmap set forth an aggregate 
goal of 6.22 million EVs for 2030, including individual 
targets of 160,000 buses and trucks.
Making a roadmap actionable
Actionable roadmaps translate these timelines and 
targets into policy levers and actions assigned to spe-
cific stakeholders. 
Not only is setting targets beneficial for making a roadmap 
concrete, it also helps make a roadmap actionable when 
countries set targets for public fleets, such as government 
vehicles and public buses. It is a way to guarantee demand for 
e-mobility, as well as reduce expenditures for public agencies 
and transportation authorities, as EVs are often cheaper to 
own in the long run due to their lower operating and mainte-
nance costs (Li et al. 2019).
 ▪ For example, Taiwan set a target for public fleets, requiring 
all urban public buses to be electric by 2030, thereby 
kick-starting the island’s e-bus industry (Sustainable Bus 
Editorial Staff 2022). Known for its efforts to electrify 
private two-wheelers, Taiwan’s shift toward public 
transportation electrification is notable and welcome.
To achieve specific targets, countries need to develop policy 
actions and assign them to relevant government ministries. 
In most cases, to achieve a certain number of EVs sold or 
registered, financial subsidies are necessary, and they need to 
be implemented by government ministries that oversee the 
economy or finance, rather than by those that typically lead 
an e-mobility roadmap—namely, ministries of transportation, 
energy, or environment.
One immediate step that governments can take to promote 
e-mobility is to give EVs preferential access to roadways or 
urban areas over mobility powered by fossil fuel. Vietnam 
should establish a national program of low- to zero-emission 
zones in its cities to incentivize a low-carbon and sustainable 
shift in its urban transportation. This way, residents and busi-
nesses in cities will have access to affordable, attractive, and 
convenient zero-emission transportation options to replace 
the existing, fossil-fuel-based ones. 
Besides demand-side actions, countries can include actionable 
supply-side strategies, including funding the manufacture 
and research of e-mobility components such as EV batter-
ies. An adequate supply of EV batteries can ensure that they 
are economically priced for widespread e-mobility adoption. 
Supply-side strategies also help attract private-sector invest-
ments and partnerships.

Making a roadmap localized
Localized roadmaps adapt e-mobility policy levers to a coun-
try’s unique economic, environmental, political, and social 
conditions. Localized roadmaps often tie e-mobility to other 
key policy goals, including energy or urban development.
The generation and transportation of clean energy are equally 
as important as the electrification of transportation. When 
developing an e-mobility roadmap, countries should col-
laborate with international and domestic research institutions 
to perform scientific analyses on the increasing electricity 
demand of powering EVs, to assess how such demand will 
affect the power grid and the amount of emissions it will 
generate from fossil-fuel sources.
 ▪ For example, government-affiliated and international 
research institutions helped develop Mexico’s National 
Strategy of Electric Mobility, which included an analysis 
of the lifecycle emissions generated by EVs from 
manufacturing them to operating them on fossil-fuel-
based electricity.
To achieve the maximum potential of e-mobility to decarbon-
ize transportation, countries should assess their energy mix. In 
the case of Vietnam, the Ministry of Transport needs to work 
together with the Ministry of Industry and Trade (MOIT) to 
develop strategies in the roadmap to supply renewable energy 
and meet the increasing power demand from e-mobility 
adoption (Avicenne Energy 2020; 2021; 2021). Although 
not evident from its name, MOIT oversees the power supply, 
including the power market and tariffs, as well as energy plan-
ning and policy (ITA 2022b; MOIT Vietnam n.d.). Globally, 
Vietnam made the third-largest renewable energy investments 
in 2020, and the country’s cumulative renewable capacity has 
dwarfed that of Malaysia, Indonesia, and Thailand as of 2020. 
To accelerate transportation and energy decarbonization, the 
Vietnamese government should continue to invest in renew-
able energy and simultaneously remove fossil-fuel subsidies 
and ramp down fossil-fuel financing (Gonçalves and Kaldjian 
2021; SLOCAT Partnership n.d.).
While e-mobility is sometimes seen as a silver bullet to reduce 
emissions from the transportation sector, it will not get a 
country to net-zero emissions on its own, as EVs still produce 
emissions in their lifecycles from their manufacture, and there 
is a need to shift passenger transportation to more active and 
shared modes (Thony and Tan 2021; Van Cleve 2021). Viet-
nam should analyze its urban transportation conditions and 
develop strategies to promote walking, cycling, micro-mobility, 
and public transportation simultaneously with transportation 
electrification.14 Combining electrification and modal shifts 
has significant emissions-reduction potential and can lead to 
better socioeconomic outcomes (ITDP and UC Davis 2021). 
 ▪ For example, Malaysia, through its Low Carbon Mobility 
Blueprint and National Automotive Policy, is developing 
a roadmap specific to electric bus adoption (Al-Ogaili 
et al. 2021). The country is simultaneously working on 
increasing national public transportation usage from 20 
to 40 percent by 2030, establishing national funding of 
more than $100 million for e-bus competitive leasing 
and procurement, and reducing electricity costs for bus-
charging facilities.
While a modal shift from private transportation to more 
sustainable transportation modes is an effective long-term 
strategy, transitioning to E2Ws offers an immediate oppor-
tunity to reduce carbon emissions and air pollution for 
Vietnam, a country where two-wheeler ownership is 22 times 
greater than car ownership (GSO Vietnam 2018; Vietnam 
Register 2020). However, instead of solely encouraging the 
uptake of new E2Ws, countries should consider tighten-
ing vehicle emissions standards and establishing attractive 
scrappage programs to accelerate the replacement of existing 
ICE two-wheelers.
Making a roadmap flexible
Flexible roadmaps require timely updates so that they 
can evolve from the trial and error of pilots, monitor-
ing, and scaling. 
Revision of existing policies is also a great way to make a 
roadmap flexible and adaptable to evolving socioeconomic 
conditions. When updating subsidies, countries should strive 
to reduce the up-front costs of owning an EV so that it is 
comparable or lower than the cost of its ICE counterpart. This 
makes switching to an EV more attractive sooner.
To make such revisions possible, countries should create a 
steering committee to monitor the progress of e-mobility 
adoption. This committee should consist of stakeholders from 
different ministries and key nongovernmental entities that 
represent e-mobility consumers, manufacturers, and financiers. 
It is the committee’s responsibility to prescribe updates to the 
roadmap’s vision, goals, targets, and strategies as needed.

CONCLUSION 
The success of a roadmap begins before its conception. First, 
selecting a lead government ministry will determine the 
roadmap’s focus: The ministry of environment will likely 
develop an e-mobility roadmap driven by emissions reduc-
tions. Second, assembling an appropriate steering committee 
with different government ministries, research institutions, 
and international development agencies is key to making 
a comprehensive e-mobility roadmap. Mexico serves as 
a prime example.
When setting a clear vision for a roadmap, countries should 
decide whether to prioritize hybrid vehicles as an important 
transitional fuel technology. This helps make a roadmap 
concrete. The financial subsidies for China’s NEVs have been 
partial to battery EVs compared to hybrid vehicles, support-
ing China’s focus on battery technology in its roadmaps. 
A roadmap’s vision should also explicitly state the type of 
vehicles that could bring the largest emissions reductions 
and co-benefits to that country, including improved public 
health outcomes, reduced demand for imported fossil fuels, 
and advancement of domestic auto manufacturing (Briceno-
Garmendia et al. 2022). Malaysia chose to prioritize buses and 
make its roadmap localized by supporting both e-bus manufac-
ture and procurement. 
Both during and after the roadmap creation process, engage-
ment with external stakeholders like e-mobility users and 
suppliers should be systematic. India, with an industry-focused 
roadmap, effectively engaged battery manufacturers with a 
production-linked scheme.
For a roadmap to be complete, countries should strive to 
subsidize EVs to reach cost parity with their ICE counter-
parts and create operational incentives to make e-mobility an 
attractive option for all. India’s strong subsidies for electric 
motorcycles and Mexico’s road access privileges for EVs are 
both great examples. Additionally, the roadmap should have 
strategies to tackle the chicken-and-egg dilemma of e-mobil-
ity adoption and charging provisions. Singapore’s actionable 
roadmap guided comprehensive charging deployment in pub-
lic and private spaces, ensuring a smooth e-mobility transition. 
Finally, a roadmap is only as good as its implementation. A 
good roadmap sets clear targets and timelines necessary to 
guide implementation and monitor whether it is achieving 
its objectives. Good goal setting is best reflected in Thai-
land’s comprehensive e-mobility targets for different vehicle 
segments, making detailed progress-monitoring possible. 
India’s interministerial committee effectively revised an 
important purchase subsidy and made its roadmap flexible, 
paving the way to long-term success. Vietnam will not only 
be able to learn from the steps that India and Thailand have 
taken, but also be able to create a flexible process that allows 
for new lessons.

APPENDIX B. ROADMAP PROCESS 
AND METHODOLOGY
Case study selection criteria
To overcome weaknesses and threats and to benefit from the 
country’s strengths and opportunities, countries like Vietnam 
should look to other countries that have been planning or 
implementing e-mobility adoption. From a list of 13 countries with 
known national e-mobility roadmaps, this paper narrowed down 
and conducted eight case studies. The analysis of this relationship 
then informs the barriers, best practices, and recommendations 
put forth in this report. These case studies were completed 
primarily using desk research through secondary sources, 
supported by in-house expertise from WRI’s regional offices that 
have directly participated in e-mobility planning in three out of the 
four countries. 15
Each of the eight countries selected for the case-study analysis 
brings a unique set of experiences and lessons learned that are 
relevant to Vietnam’s e-mobility planning efforts and progress 
toward achieving its NDCs. For countries that are excluded from 
this study, their relevance to Vietnam is not as apparent as the four 
selected countries, but information about their e-mobility roadmaps 
can be found in Appendix A for further investigation.
Case studies information  
collection framework
During the case-study search, this analysis included roadmaps 
mentioning EVs or the EV industry. Roadmaps that mentioned 
public transit (buses and other modes), medium- and heavy-duty 
commercial vehicles, light-duty passenger vehicles, shared cars, 
and two- and three-wheelers were all considered. EV charging 
infrastructure, utility preparation, and vehicle grid-integration topics 
were also scoped, although it is rare for country-level roadmaps 
to focus on only one aspect of e-mobility. However, roadmaps are 
often updates, so we use the most recent ones for analysis but 
refer to the historical ones for changes and evolution. 
Roadmaps were defined as assessments of the EV landscape, 
technical assessments, outlines, and draft-working-group 
information. The roadmap analysis used publicly available 
documents in English, simplified Chinese, and Spanish. General 
search terms included “EV roadmap,” “eMobility,” “electric vehicle,” 
“V2G,” “zero-emissions,” “rate structure,” and “new energy vehicles,” 
with country names. Some of the multilingual search terms include 
“electromovilidad (Spanish)” and “新能源汽车、 电动汽车 (simplified 
Chinese).” The analysis focused on the case study’s national 
government websites, energy or transportation think tanks such 
as the ICCT or IEA, translation of policy documents, news sources 
in the gray literature, and publications from research institutions.

Project limitations
This working paper provides insights, analysis, and 
recommendations on country-level e-mobility roadmap processes 
but has the following noted limitations:
This working paper relied primarily on desktop research. No direct 
interviews were conducted with government officials, potential and 
existing EV users, or the private sector. However, the sources that 
this study used include primary source documents.
Moreover, due to the nature of certain forms of government, 
specific information about the inner workings of e-mobility 
roadmaps, such as stakeholder group selection and formation, 
might not be transparent. Without accessible information, 
it is challenging to evaluate the detailed processes that the 
e-mobility roadmaps have undergone, thereby limiting the 
learning opportunities available for other governments planning to 
adopt e-mobility.
The preliminary research in this study shows that the eight 
countries are in various stages of e-mobility roadmap 
implementation and general EV adoption. Some roadmaps are 
more current, having been released one or two years before this 
study was written. However, there are gaps of up to five years. 
Therefore, information from such roadmaps might be slightly 
outdated due to electric mobility’s rapid technological progress 
and market growth.
Because the scope of this paper is to focus on the general 
framework and implementation of e-mobility roadmaps, insights on 
the technical components of e-mobility roadmaps are limited and 
covered in separate papers in the NDC-TIA e-mobility series.

GLOSSARY
2-wheeler:  Vehicle that operates with two wheels and is powered 
entirely by its motor, such as a motorcycle or moped.
3-wheeler:  Vehicle that operates with three wheels, such 
as e-rickshaws.
Avoid-Shift-Improve (A-S-I): A sustainable environmental 
planning approach that consists of three hierarchical avenues: 
avoid/reduce, shift or maintain, and improve. The “avoid” avenue 
stresses choosing to use none of or less of a resource. The “shift” 
avenue switches from a less sustainable method of consumption 
to a more sustainable one; if the more sustainable option is already 
in use, the consumer maintains its use. The “improve” avenue 
increases the resource efficiency of an existing good or service.
E-mobility:  Electric mobility, mainly referring to transportation 
powered by electricity instead of fossil fuel and the infrastructure 
needed to supply the electricity.
Electric vehicles:  Vehicles, including passenger cars, 2-wheelers, 
3-wheelers, buses, and other machines used to transport human or 
goods, powered by electricity. In this publication, the EVs referred 
to are those powered by rechargeable battery packs with no 
secondary propulsion source, such as hydrogen fuel cells.
FAME Scheme I and II: India’s Ministry of Heavy Industries 
launched the Faster Adoption and Manufacturing of Electric 
Vehicles (FAME) scheme in April 2015 to encourage electric and 
hybrid vehicle purchase by providing financial support. Its first 
phase ran for four years until 2019. Its second phase (FAME II) 
launched in 2020 and is a three-year subsidy program. FAME 
II aims to prioritize the electrification of public and shared 
transportation, but it also finances charging infrastructures under 
phase I of the scheme. 
National EV Roadmap: A published strategy for governments or 
government-backed entities to achieve wide-scale electric mobility 
adoption. The strategy does not need to be EV driven but does 
need to include EV targets or language.

ABOUT WRI 
World Resources Institute is a global research organization that 
turns big ideas into action at the nexus of environment, economic 
opportunity, and human well-being. 
Our challenge
Natural resources are at the foundation of economic opportunity 
and human well-being. But today, we are depleting Earth’s 
resources at rates that are not sustainable, endangering economies 
and people’s lives. People depend on clean water, fertile land, 
healthy forests, and a stable climate. Livable cities and clean 
energy are essential for a sustainable planet. We must address 
these urgent, global challenges this decade.
Our vision
We envision an equitable and prosperous planet driven by the wise 
management of natural resources. We aspire to create a world 
where the actions of government, business, and communities 
combine to eliminate poverty and sustain the natural environment 
for all people.
Our approach
COUNT IT
We start with data. We conduct independent research and 
draw on the latest technology to develop new insights and 
recommendations. Our rigorous analysis identifies risks, unveils 
opportunities, and informs smart strategies. We focus our efforts 
on influential and emerging economies where the future of 
sustainability will be determined.
CHANGE IT
We use our research to influence government policies, business 
strategies, and civil society action. We test projects with 
communities, companies, and government agencies to build a 
strong evidence base. Then, we work with partners to deliver 
change on the ground that alleviates poverty and strengthens 
society. We hold ourselves accountable to ensure our outcomes 
will be bold and enduring.
SCALE IT
We don’t think small. Once tested, we work with partners to adopt 
and expand our efforts regionally and globally. We engage with 
decision-makers to carry out our ideas and elevate our impact. We 
measure success through government and business actions that 
improve people’s lives and sustain a healthy environment.
