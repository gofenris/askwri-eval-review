Introduction
Urban mobility planning in India is at a pivotal stage. In the past two 
decades, considerable investments have been made in sustainable urban 
mobility options such as metro rail systems (Mukherjee et al. 2023) and, 
more recently, in augmenting public bus fleets with electric buses. Although 
these investments align with some of the broad objectives of the country’s 
National Urban Transport Policy (NUTP), such as encouraging efficient, 
equitable, and sustainable mobility (MoHUA 2006), overall trends in 
mobility (Table 1) indicate an increasing reliance on private motorization. 
This reliance is linked to India’s declining urban air quality, rising road crash 
fatalities, and increasing traffic congestion (Yadav and Chattopadhyay 2025) 
and contributes to inequity and declining urban mobility efficiency.
This unsustainable mode share change is influenced by India’s urban 
mobility planning process, which is often characterized by arbitrary or ad 
hoc decision-making approaches. This decision-making pattern has been 
observed across a variety of contexts and projects (Sreenivas 2011; Munshi 
2013; Harding 2015; Sriraman 2015; Gijre and Gupta 2020; Sridhar et al. 
2020). Urban mobility planning merits a holistic, data-based, long-term 
approach. In this context, three critical reasons explain the current ad hocism 
observed the Indian context: a reactive, often interest-driven approach to 
mobility planning (ETPI 2024; Dawda 2025); lack of clear data (especially 
data on what commuters want, i.e., mobility demand), which hinders data-
driven decision-making (IIHS 2015); and a project-based rather than a 
holistic approach to urban mobility investments (Sreenivas 2011; IIHS 2015; 
Mukherjee and Gupta 2019).
To appropriately plan and leverage significant investments in urban mobility, 
a more long-term, data-based approach is necessary. An evident first step is to 
collect better mobility demand data, and a subsequent WRI India publication 
will discuss this need and the mechanisms to address it. Equally important, 
however, is a holistic framework to structure and interpret these data for 
Indian mobility decision-making. This expert note outlines elements of such 
a framework, which can play an important role in identifying appropriate 
mobility solutions.

A key issue in Indian mobility planning is the disconnect between the demand and supply of mobility (Pucher et al. 
2004; Mukherjee and Gupta 2018; Ahmad and Chang 2020; Verma et al. 2021); thus, an overarching framework 
with supply and demand (and associated performance indicators) as the key elements appears appropriate. Indeed, the 
centrality of demand, supply, and performance data to the success of urban transport systems has been established 
in the literature for decades (Pallmann et al. 1983). Building upon several existing frameworks, such as Ballantyne 
et al. (2024) and academic frameworks for public transport selection in India (Tiwari and Jain 2021), we use an 
integrative approach to propose that a combined supply, demand, and performance framework can highlight useful 
mobility investments and interventions at the city level. We also highlight some of the key indicators for each of these 
elements—as discussed in the existing literature—and the current state of data collection for these indicators in India.
Context
Since the mid-2000s, investments in sustainable transport infrastructure in India have increased significantly. Schemes 
such as the Jawaharlal Nehru National Urban Renewal Mission (JnNURM), Faster Adoption and Manufacturing 
of (Hybrid &) Electric Vehicles (FAME), and PM e-Bus Sewa support urban bus systems (WRI India Ross Centre 
2021; MoHUA 2023); metro rail lengths in the country have more than quadrupled (Parashar 2024), with metro rail 
investments exceeding US$25 billion during the 2010 decade (Mukherjee and Desai 2021); and some cities have begun 
planning for and adopting measures to promote active mobility (ETPI 2024).
However, decision-making around urban mobility across Indian cities has remained inconsistent, frequently prioritizing 
road-widening projects (Hidalgo et al. 2013; Verma et al. 2021). Even when sustainable urban mobility projects 
are funded and sanctioned, Sreenivas (2011) points out that many cities “are increasingly seeking single, large, big-
budget solutions to their urban transport problems without exploring the many simpler, cheaper, and more effective 
options available”.

Therefore, urban India’s mobility trends are concerning: eight of India’s largest city bus operators report declining 
ridership (Gadepalli et al. 2018), and metro rail ridership remains well below projections (Mukherjee et al. 2023). 
Private motorization, especially two-wheelers, has increased exponentially, and the share of active mobility trips in total 
trips across many Indian cities (except Delhi) has declined (Table 1).
These mode-share data, primarily the increase in private vehicle mode-share, indicate that we are yet to deploy mobility 
investments in a manner that reflects what users want, encourages sustainable transport use, and is viable in the Indian 
urban context. Due to this, unsustainable and inefficient mobility trends (such as high levels of private motorization) 
persist in urban India, resulting in poorer air quality, high road crash fatalities, and time lost due to traffic delays. 
Implementing a clear planning framework can improve decision-making. Such a framework can also move India’s urban 
mobility narrative away from the current paradigm, which focuses only on “solutions” to traffic congestion without a 
holistic understanding of causative factors such as the interplay between urban mobility elements. 
The demand–supply–performance framework: A useful approach to 
assess mobility in India 
The creation of frameworks to understand and better plan for urban mobility (or transport) is not new. However, in 
the Indian context, an urgent issue is the lack of long-term planning that is responsive to the mobility demands of 
users, resulting in a mismatch between demand and supply (Pucher et al. 2004; Mukherjee and Gupta 2018; Ahmad 
and Chang 2020; Verma et al. 2021). Thus, a holistic framework focusing on urban mobility demand and supply, 
such as Ballantyne et al.’s (2024) framework, could prove useful both in the short and long terms. However, existing 
research—such as Gudmundsson (2004) and an earlier publication coauthored by one of this paper’s authors (Hidalgo 
et al. 2013)—also highlights the need for urban mobility performance measurement, which plays a major role in 
refining service design to improve the reliability, sustainability, and efficiency of urban mobility services. The fact that 
the intricate interplay between supply, demand, and performance often dictates the success or failure of urban transport 
systems in meeting user needs has been well established in the literature for decades (e.g., Pallmann et al. 1983).
Thus, the three elements of an integrative urban mobility framework that could guide Indian planning and investments 
are mobility demand, supply, and performance. The demand for mobility reflects user demand for travel across a 
variety of modes of transport. The supply of mobility refers to the provision of different types of critical mobility 
infrastructure and assets that can satisfy urban mobility demand. The interplay between supply and demand determines 
the performance  of urban mobility in the region.

These three indicators are interrelated: mobility supply should be designed based on an understanding of mobility 
demand, and supply also influences demand. At present, India lacks any sort of robust or periodic demand data 
collection. For both supply and performance, data are often collected, with several indicators (see Tables 3 and 4) 
partially or wholly collected as part of the Ministry of Housing and Urban Affairs’ Service Level Benchmarks for 
urban transport (MoHUA 2016). However, these indicators are evaluated piecemeal (rather than holistically) for urban 
mobility improvement projects, and the relationships between them are rarely explored (Sreenivas 2011; Bhasin and 
Singh 2023). The demand–supply–performance framework described in this expert note highlights data gaps and 
incorporates several of these piecemeal indicators into a more integrated framework.
From a sustainability standpoint, it is crucial that such a framework focus on public transport, and an example of 
a framework with this focus is Tiwari and Jain’s framework (2021). However, limiting the focus solely to public 
transport may not be sufficient. It is also necessary to understand road network connectivity, which is essential for 
promoting accessibility to jobs, education, health care, and other essential services, especially for vulnerable populations 
who rely on public transportation or active modes of transport (Ballantyne et al. 2024). Moreover, recent advances 
in geographical information system (GIS) technologies have significantly improved the ease and precision of road 
network connectivity analysis, allowing for faster and more accurate results, including in Indian conditions (Sahitya 
and Prasad 2021).
Demand
For urban mobility planning to be data-driven, citizen-centric, sustainable, and efficient, it must understand and 
respond to mobility demand (Schwedes and Hoor 2019). Because mobility demand data are rarely easily available, 
especially for active mobility modes, many countries use periodic travel surveys to capture and measure mobility 
demand (Stopher et al. 2014). Currently, in India, demand data are usually collected in isolation in the context of 
specific planned projects (Mukherjee and Gupta 2018) and modeled to “support” the project, rather than through a 
holistic, periodic data collection exercise. In a subsequent publication, we will elaborate on the mechanisms of how such 
an exercise could be conducted effectively.
Urban mobility demand is a function of the desire, ability, and willingness of users to travel within and around the city. 
It is also influenced by urban form and activity distribution (Wee 2002), the supply of mobility, and user demographics 
(Mwale et al. 2022). To understand a city’s mobility flows and volumes, demand can be expressed as a travel origin–
destination matrix (Bhouri et al. 2021), as shown in Figure 2.

Demand data, however, have many indicators (as highlighted in Table 2), and disaggregating demand data is important 
for mobility planning. Also, for new mobility projects, it is important to capture latent (potential) demand (Clifton and 
Moura 2017). Many data collection methods are available to capture or estimate latent demand; however, it is crucial to 
develop a system to capture these data periodically and use them. 
Supply
Supply is easier to measure than demand, and data on most of the urban mobility supply components mentioned in 
Table 3 are available in the public domain. With GIS analytics, aspects such as road network structure and connectivity 
are now easier to measure. Table 3 presents the key indicators of urban mobility supply in India, which were drawn from 
extant research. 
Supply is critical to urban mobility. However, merely providing or increasing supply without understanding the nuances 
of mobility demand at the city and local levels can often result in utilization and viability issues (Macário 2011; Small et 
al. 2024). The resulting mobility infrastructure or assets could remain highly underused (see Albalate et al. 2015 for an 
example from Europe) or, conversely, overutilized. Although supply does create some demand, assuming that any supply 
will be adequately utilized by demand is a fallacy that needs to be avoided when making transport investment decisions. 
Unfortunately, as mentioned earlier, although supply data are collected and used in existing approaches to 
benchmarking urban transport—nonmotorized infrastructure and public transport supply are already used as transport 
Service Level Benchmarks (MoHUA 2016)—these indicators are currently evaluated in isolation for “improvement” 
projects and are not assessed in the context of either or a holistic approach to planning urban mobility.

Performance
As mentioned in the previous section, the intersection of mobility demand and supply determines the city’s urban 
mobility performance. Table 4 presents the key performance indicators. 
Several of these performance indicators are discussed frequently in the media (congestion, emissions, and crashes). 
Many are also collected as part of transport Service Level Benchmarks (pedestrian infrastructure facilities, travel 
speeds, road safety, and pollution) by the Ministry of Housing and Urban Affairs (MoHUA). However, as with supply 
indicators, these indicators are also considered in isolation without a holistic understanding of causative factors such 
as the interplay between supply and demand. Equally importantly, there is a need to leverage and embed newer data 
sources (such as vehicle and phone telematics) to increase the accuracy, breadth, and dynamism of these indicators. 
Finally, focusing on performance indicators without understanding their determinants usually results in inaccurate 
narratives and short-term solutions, such as addressing traffic congestion solely through flyover construction.

Discussion
Implementing a more data-informed framework, as is the practice in several countries, would be a useful first step 
toward improved mobility planning across urban India. In this expert note, we have highlighted a three-element 
framework  and several important mobility indicators corresponding to each of these elements, some of which are not 
currently considered (or for which data are not collected) as part of the mobility planning process. Equally importantly, 
a holistic planning approach that considers the relationships between these different elements of mobility planning 
(supply, demand, and performance) has not been developed. On the positive side, data and indicators for most 
performance and supply-side aspects of these indicators are already being collected, although in an ad hoc, disjointed 
manner and without holistic investigation. For example, poor-quality road crash data often hamper the creation of 
effective road safety measures (Chatterjee et al. 2025).
Multiple approaches are possible to begin institutionalizing this type of a data-driven, framework-driven mobility 
planning process. A ministry of the central government could initially issue guidelines on appropriate data collection 
and their incorporation into these mobility indicators. In addition, to spur initial adoption of this process, when 
rolling out a scheme dealing with urban mobility, the ministry could mandate data collection and framework-driven 
analysis as preconditions for obtaining union funding. Alternatively, a few cities could be designated proof-of-concept 
cities and provided funding to collect data for these indicators. Once sufficient data have been collected and a more 
precise quantitative understanding of the relationship between these indicators across different cities is established, the 
indicators (and guiding ideas) of this framework could begin to be incorporated into the transport models that guide the 
urban masterplans and CMPs of cities. These CMPs should ideally be developed periodically in a consistent manner. 
To ensure clarity, multiple implementation and regulatory aspects would need to be streamlined as well because a 
framework alone cannot leverage the full potential of urban mobility investments informed by demand–supply–
performance indicators in the absence of appropriate monitoring and implementation. Given the explosion of data on 
mobility (from GIS and telematics sources), there is a need to understand, collect, and embed such (anonymized) data 
in data collection processes and mobility frameworks to improve their dynamism and accuracy, Apart from the data 
collection itself, the capacity of civic and transit agencies to analyze such data needs to be improved. It is also important 
to account for the impact of land-use and spatial activity patterns across cities because these considerably influence 
travel behavior and in turn, mobility demand, which can be catered to with appropriate supply shifts. Regulatory 
frameworks for mobility also play an important role: mode shares for paratransit across all the cities mentioned in Table 
1 showed an increase, and there is a need to evaluate how they can be regulated in a manner that meets user demand 
while also minimizing congestion and mode shifts away from public transport, aspects that we will address in more 
detail in an upcoming work. However, if decision-making can be made more data-based and less intuitive or interest 
driven, India will have greater potential to improve its urban mobility.
