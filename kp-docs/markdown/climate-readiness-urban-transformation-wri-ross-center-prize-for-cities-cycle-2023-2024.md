## About this paper

**This paper examines how climate readiness can act as a driver of urban transformation by analyzing five finalists from the 2023–2024 WRI Ross Center Prize for Cities (see Figure ES-1).**

Each finalist represents a concrete, on-the-ground initiative that sought to improve climate outcomes, and in so doing also delivered wider socio-ecological benefits. We carried out a comparative case study of the five finalists, drawing on evidence from interviews, documentation, and application materials. We investigated the types of impacts these initiatives generated, the processes through which they grew, and the underlying conditions that supported their development. Our analysis offers lessons for urban practitioners, in particular project implementers and local authorities, who aim to design climate actions that also prepare cities for broader, long-term change. The paper does not claim transferability or generalizability beyond the Prize finalists; rather, it is designed to build contextual understanding and enable readers to assess the relevance of its findings for their own contexts.

**Limitations:** This analysis draws on a purposive sample of Prize finalists and qualitative documentation generated through the competition process. As such, it is not representative of urban initiatives globally, does not capture average or unsuccessful efforts, and does not include counterfactual or negative-case analysis. In addition, because members of the research team were closely involved in the Prize process, the study adopted a reflexive approach—triangulating sources, interrogating interpretations, and incorporating internal and external review—to mitigate potential bias and ensure conclusions remained grounded in the available evidence.

## The research questions

**Urban climate action is often treated as a discrete technical field, but in practice cities need strategies that strengthen long-term integrated development.** Individual climate gains, such as improvements to public space, ecosystems, or cooling infrastructure, matter, but even more valuable is understanding how such actions grow beyond single sites to influence governance, community dynamics, or institutional routines (Revi et al. 2014; Revi et al. 2020). This paper focuses on this topic by examining how climate actions can act as entry points into broader urban transformation.

The paper addresses three questions:

1. What kinds of transformative change did Prize-recognized initiatives generate across urban systems, including and beyond climate benefits?
2. Through what scaling processes did individual interventions result in impact beyond their initial scope?
3. What underlying enabling conditions allowed these initiatives to achieve the outsized impact they had?

These questions speak directly to the broader challenge of urban transformation, and the role climate readiness can play within it. In this paper, we define climate readiness as the set of governance, financial, organizational, and social infrastructures that enable climate initiatives to take root, persist, and expand beyond individual projects, shaping how urban systems operate over time. Key findings

1. Climate-readiness initiatives generated wide-ranging impacts that extended beyond climate outcomes: Initiatives reported improved safety, mobility, public and ecological health, and access to public spaces while strengthening community cohesion and governance coordination and changing public narratives about climate risk and equitable urban development. This layering of impacts helped shift what was possible in each city and created openings for broader institutional, spatial, and social change.
2. Climate actions grew through recognizable scaling processes that helped them gain momentum and become established: Across the five cities, early climate actions tended to grow through a combination of “scaling out,” “scaling deep,” and “scaling up.” These scaling processes, present in the urban transformation literature (e.g., Lam et al. 2020; Ghosh et al. 2021), and observed at work in practice in the finalist initiatives, did not follow a fixed sequence. Together they increased the influence of early interventions; some initiatives became more visible and attracted new partners, while others established clearer roles and procedures. Still others settled into routine practice after repeated cycles of learning and adjustment. These processes were central to how climate actions contributed to wider urban transformation.

3. Seven types of “infrastructures” shaped how initiatives influenced broader systems: Based on a comparative thematic analysis, we propose that the finalists illustrate how climate readiness may be constituted by seven interconnected infrastructures: governance, human and organizational capacity, knowledge and learning, societal relationships, physical setting, technology and data, and financial continuity. These infrastructures supported climate actions by giving them authorized spaces, staff and skills, community legitimacy, physical pathways, information, and operational continuity. They also evolved as initiatives matured. Strengthening these infrastructures made adaptation more effective and helped cities move toward longer-term, system-shaping change.

## Priority actions for cities

The findings point to a set of practical actions that can help urban practitioners strengthen the infrastructures underlying climate action and thereby allow broader forms of urban transformation to take root, expand, and endure.

1. **Recognizing the phase your initiative is in:** We found that climate actions tend to move through phases of gaining meaning, stabilizing routines, growing outward, and settling into routine practice. Knowing the phase helps practitioners strengthen the right work at the right time.
2. **Diagnosing the context:** Each city has different physical, social, technological, and governance infrastructures. Understanding the starting point using multiple dimensions of infrastructure helps identify which early steps are achievable and could be catalytic.
3. **Designing for spillover:** Initiatives have the greatest effect when they open the door to new partners, uses, or sectors. Creating opportunities for lateral uptake makes climate actions more influential across urban systems.
4. **Learning from early interventions:** First steps reveal constraints, build legitimacy, and generate feedback that improves future work. Making early actions visible, transparent, and adaptable strengthens momentum.
5. **Putting simple governance support in place early:** Light but reliable anchors such as agreements, routines, or mandates help steady initiatives through uncertainty.
6. **Investing in the elements that help new practices become routine:** Staffing, learning routines, physical upkeep, community stewardship, and predictable resources help climate actions endure and expand.

## Conclusion

**Climate readiness is not only preparation for climate shocks; it may also become a pathway to deeper urban transformation.**

The Prize finalists show that when climate actions strengthen the conditions that help cities act, adapt, and sustain progress, they do far more than reduce risk—they can help reorient the wider urban system toward more resilient, inclusive, and sustainable futures.

## Introduction

This paper is a first step in addressing a key evidence gap for action—the broader transformative potential of climate interventions to address environmental and social objectives in urban settings (Revi et al. 2014). Cities are recognized as important arenas for tackling climate change, where social, economic, and environmental vulnerabilities intersect and where many of the most immediate adaptation decisions are made and implemented (IPCC 2023; Satterthwaite et al. 2020). Over the past two decades, adaptation and resilience have become the dominant paradigms guiding responses to the effects of climate change in cities (Pelling and Blackburn 2014; Carmin et al. 2012). Adaptation is typically understood as a suite of targeted interventions to reduce exposure or vulnerability to the impacts of climate change, whereas resilience refers to the capacity of systems and societies to absorb disturbance, reorganize, and continue functioning (Folke 2016; Meerow and Stults 2016). In urban contexts, adaptation and resilience have evolved along increasingly convergent paths (Carmin et al. 2013).

An underexplored dimension concerns how adaptation and resilience efforts relate to broader processes of urban transformation. Just as both agendas have moved closer in scholarship and practice, both remain bounded in scope around managing risks and less about reshaping the systems that produce these risks (Ziervogel et al. 2016; Bahadur and Tanner 2014). The resulting initiatives have sometimes been criticized for producing incremental adaptation rather than transformative change (Pelling et al. 2015). The growing recognition that climate impacts are entwined with social inequities and vulnerabilities has prompted calls for shifts in how cities plan, govern, and invest (Revi et al. 2020; Frantzeskaki et al. 2017). This prompts the following question: Under what conditions do adaptation and resilience interventions lead to more fundamental changes in urban systems?

The 2023–2024 cycle of the WRI Ross Center Prize for Cities (herein referred to as “the Prize”) provides a unique vantage point from which to explore this question as it solicited applications through an open call under the theme of “Accelerating Momentum for Climate-Ready Communities.” The Prize is a global impact award that identifies and disseminates exemplary practices in urban transformation that have had an outsized impact on their cities. This framing offers a look beyond project-level outcomes toward wider transformations that these efforts can catalyze. Since its launch in 2018, the Prize has spotlighted 20 finalist initiatives that tackled urban transformation in various thematic domains and geographies, from over 1,200 submissions across nearly 600 cities and disbursing US$1.4 million in prize-money awards.

## Climate readiness: the missing link between climate action and urban transformation

To explain how climate initiatives may contribute to wider systemic urban change, we integrate insights from three complementary strands of existing research. First, academic scholarship on urban transformation clarifies what constitutes systemic, cross-sectoral change and how it differs from more incremental improvements. Second, within sustainability transitions research, research on scaling shows how individual initiatives gain wider influence through processes of expansion, replication, deepening, or institutionalization. Third, approaches at the adaptation-resilience nexus highlight the capabilities cities need to manage risk, while enabling durable forms of change. These ideas help us interpret when and how climate actions may become entry points to broader transformation.

## Urban transformation and the scaling of individual interventions

The term “urban transformation” has only recently become associated with sustainability in urban scholarship. Historically, it described a range of major urban shifts, including demographic transitions, post-socialist restructuring in the former Soviet Union, urban renewal, and shifts in urban culture or morphology—each with distinct disciplinary meanings and without a shared normative orientation. Only since roughly 2010 has sustainable urban transformation emerged as a recognizable field, shaped by the convergence of urban studies, sustainability science, and global environmental change debates. This more recent strand frames transformation as deliberate, cross-sectoral, systemic change directed toward more sustainable and equitable urban futures (McCormick et al. 2013; Wolfram and Frantzeskaki 2016; Mahendra et al. 2021).

Despite this conceptual maturation, definitions remain diverse. Most converge on relatively abstract descriptors—as the “persistent” and “far-reaching” reconfiguration of urban systems; changes that are “multi-actor,” “multilevel,” and “non-linear” (Wolfram et al. 2016; Grandin et al. 2018). What distinguishes transformation from more incremental forms of improvement is not dramatic rupture, but the accumulation of changes that begins redirecting how systems function and what they prioritize. Much of the literature describes urban transformation either in these abstract terms, or uses more conventional sustainability indicators from the climate mitigation, adaptation and resilience, and social equity fields, including tangible, empirical improvements to environmental, social, or physical conditions (e.g., reductions in hazard exposure, ecological gains, or enhanced access and inclusion) (Maassen and Galvin 2019).

To understand how urban transformation unfolds, a body of work at the intersection of urban studies and sustainability transitions offers insight into how small, situated interventions can extend their influence beyond their initial scope. Earlier urban scholarship described “catalyst” projects—specific interventions that triggered wider physical or social renewal (Attoe and Logan 1989; Shen 2013). More recent work reframes this catalytic role for climate action through the lens of “urban experimentation,” emphasizing experiments as situated interventions in governance, infrastructure, or social practice through which actors test new ways of organizing and learning for sustainability (Evans et al. 2016; Bulkeley et al. 2019; Bulkeley and Castán Broto 2013). Experiments are essentially projects that are structured for learning and problem-solving under conditions of uncertainty (Sengers et al. 2016; Torrens et al. 2018).

Individual interventions may grow to have wider influence through “scaling”: processes that allow lessons generated in one site to shape outcomes elsewhere (Lam et al. 2020; Ghosh et al. 2021). The main principle behind scaling is that localized initiatives increase their reach and relevance through observable “stepping-stone” outcomes that signal increasing influence and realignment (e.g., broadening of actor networks around a new technology, policy embedding and institutionalization, consolidating new routines or revising policy standards). Scaling frameworks—like Ghosh et al.’s (2021) “transformative outcomes” and Lam et al.’s (2020) “amplification processes”—emphasize that systemic change emerges through the interaction of multiple scaling dynamics rather than through discrete, linear stages. Across these two main scaling frameworks (Lam et al. 2020; Ghosh et al. 2021), five distinct scaling mechanisms can be synthesized (see Table 1):

- Scaling deep: shifts in meaning, norms, relationships, or administrative routines that strengthen internal coherence or legitimacy
- Scaling out: spatial or operational extension into new sites, corridors, districts, or organizational units within one geography
- Scaling up: embedding practices in mandates, budgeting cycles, regulatory frameworks, or other institutional structures that provide durability
- Scaling across: horizontal diffusion to other geographies
- Scaling down: deliberate removal or weakening of carbon-intensive or dysfunctional practices, norms, or infrastructures

These categories provide a practical vocabulary for analyzing how individual projects and initiatives move beyond isolated interventions. However, by themselves they cannot fully explain

why similar interventions scale differently depending on their context, or why certain trajectories become possible in some cities and not others. This points to an underlying set of conditions that shape whether interventions gain traction, accumulate influence, or begin to redirect systems. Understanding these underlying conditions requires paying closer attention to how cities build their capacities for transformative action, which is the focus of the next section.

## Climate readiness: connecting risk management to systemic change

In this section, we examine how adaptation and resilience approaches already identify many of the enabling factors that shape cities’ capacities for climate action—such as governance arrangements, resources, social cohesion, and learning processes. At the same time, we show that these approaches often remain oriented toward managing hazards and coping with impacts, rather than reshaping the systems that produce vulnerability. In the context of our research on the relationship between climate action and urban transformation, this opens space for a novel concept—climate readiness—that links risk-oriented capabilities with the wider, system-facing capabilities required for transformative scaling.

In practice, adaptation and resilience approaches provide a concrete entry point for understanding how cities undertake climate action (Chu et al. 2019). As global attention expanded from climate mitigation toward managing climate impacts, adaptation entered the urban policy landscape through city networks and planning frameworks promoted by actors such as ICLEI – Local Governments for Sustainability and C40 Cities (Bulkeley and Betsill 2013). These efforts emphasized diagnostics of exposure and vulnerability, leading to sector-specific strategies and interventions across water, housing, energy, mobility, and health

Resilience, in turn, gained prominence through initiatives such as the former 100 Resilient Cities program of the Rockefeller Foundation, which broadened the focus beyond climate hazards to include social, economic, and institutional stresses, and foregrounded governance coordination, learning, and cross-sector collaboration (Leitner et al. 2018). While adaptation and resilience approaches have significantly expanded cities' abilities to assess risk, coordinate action, and implement climate-relevant interventions, cities tend to frame these attributes primarily in relation to preparedness and coping. Much of the focus remains on reducing exposure to hazards and strengthening the robustness of existing systems, rather than on how climate actions might accumulate to reshape development pathways, governance arrangements, or social and spatial relations over time. As a result, these approaches offer limited insight into why some climate initiatives gain momentum beyond their initial scope—in particular, beyond climate outcomes—while others remain bounded, even in cities with comparable levels of risk and institutional capacity. In other words, they are closely focused on climate and resilience outcomes, but stop short of linking interventions to deeper shifts in urban development pathways, governance patterns, or social and spatial arrangements (Chu et al. 2019).

To address this limitation, we use “climate readiness” to focus on the underlying conditions that shape whether climate action can be sustained and expanded over time. We conceptualize these conditions as infrastructural in nature: They form the often invisible but foundational arrangements of institutions, resources, routines, and relationships through which action becomes possible, replicable, and durable. In this paper, therefore, climate readiness refers to the set of governance, financial, organizational, and social infrastructures that enable climate initiatives to take root and expand beyond individual projects, shaping how urban systems operate over time. This understanding aligns closely with how readiness is already treated in practice. Tools such as the Global Covenant of Mayors’ Climate Innovation Readiness Navigator (GCoM 2024), the United Nations Office for Disaster Risk Reduction’s Making Cities Resilient 2030 framework (UNDRR 2021), and capacity diagnostics used by development banks (e.g., World Bank 2020; ADB 2014) and city networks (ICLEI 2024) emphasize that effective climate action depends not only on plans or projects, but on the quality of public support—the institutional, financial,

and learning systems that support implementation. Read in this way, climate readiness captures the infrastructural foundations that allow climate initiatives to move from isolated interventions toward more durable, system-level change.

In this view, readiness can be both a precondition and a product of transformation. ## Case study and methodology

### The WRI Ross Center Prize for Cities as an analytical setting

The Prize provides a well-documented empirical setting for examining how climate readiness can act as a driver of urban transformation. Each cycle generates a structured body of material that offers comparable evidence across diverse urban initiatives. These materials provide insight into the contexts, challenges, focuses, and impacts of different initiatives. The Prize follows a standardized, multistage review process. While this process is designed for award evaluation rather than research, it produces a rich, internally coherent record of practice that lends itself well to qualitative comparative analysis. A full description of the Prize’s purpose, evaluation stages and criteria, and documentation workflow is provided in Appendix A.

### Case study: the 2023–2024 cycle

Following Yin’s (2018) typology of case study design, the 2023–2024 cycle serves as a bounded case study comprising five urban initiatives. Within the Prize cycle 2023–2024 case, the unit of analysis is the project, defined by the Prize as “a specific activity or programmatically linked set of activities in the city that have contributed to inclusive and sustainable urban transformation” (WRI Ross Center 2025). In line with Stake (1995), a case study approach allowed us to explore how climate readiness is interpreted in practice and how individual interventions can act as entry points for broader urban transformation. The 2023–2024 cycle invited initiatives that demonstrated increased scale, speed, or scope of action toward inclusive, climate-ready urban development. Although contextual variables cannot be perfectly isolated, Flyvbjerg (2006) notes that detailed real-world cases can still generate valuable, context-specific insights about complex urban change. Through transparency on case boundaries, data sources, and analytical procedures, the paper enables readers to assess the relevance of its findings for their own contexts. We do not claim that findings have transferability or generalizability beyond the Prize finalists.

The 2023–2024 cycle received 200 eligible submissions from 148 cities in 62 countries, spanning sectors such as river management, housing, mobility, waste, and public space. From this pool, five finalist projects were selected through the Prize’s evaluation process as exemplars of urban transformation through the lens of climate readiness (see Figure 2). The five finalists—Rodrigo Bueno (Buenos Aires, Argentina), Kham River (Aurangabad, now Chhatrapati Sambhajnagar, India), Re-Ciclo (Fortaleza, Brazil), Green Community Schoolyards (New York City [NYC], United States), and the Climate Budget (Oslo, Norway)—anchor the analysis. They represent diverse entry points and contexts across housing, ecology, circular waste systems, green public infrastructure, and fiscal governance. This diversity is both asset and limitation, as it enables the paper to explore climate readiness in a range of contexts, including different cultural, institutional, and high- and low-resource contexts, yet it also underscores limits to generalization that arise from a purposefully selected diverse sample (see “Limitations” below). A detailed description of the call for entries, evaluation steps, and finalist selection process is provided in Appendix A, and additional background information on the finalists is given in Appendix B.

### Evidence base and data sources

The five finalists underwent a comprehensive evaluation and verification, which produced an evidence base consisting of application materials, evaluator notes, due diligence reports, jury deliberation briefings, and practitioner convening proceedings (see Appendix A for the Prize evaluation process and Table C-1 in Appendix C for a full list of data sources used in the analysis). A workshop, co-hosted with the Yale Hixon Center for Urban Sustainability, helped us co-create, alongside finalists and experts, refined case narratives—clarifying system boundaries, interrogating causal claims, and reflecting on conditions that enabled or constrained change. To mitigate variation in detail and narrative emphasis across cases, we triangulated evidence across multiple document types and data sources. Documentary claims were cross-checked against interview data, site-visit observations, and independent materials where available.

### Analytical approach

We used a qualitative interpretive design and thematic analysis. In an interpretive design, the goal is to understand how people and organizations make sense of their actions and contexts rather than attribute causal effects or measure impact (Schwartz-Shea and Yanow 2012). Thematic analysis provides a systematic way to identify and interpret recurring patterns of meaning within qualitative material such as project applications, evaluations, and workshop discussions (Braun and Clarke 2006).

## Limitations and reflexivity

Scope, representativeness, and transferability: The analysis does not examine other World Resources Institute (WRI) programs or external urban initiatives where similar dynamics might also emerge. It is designed to build contextual understanding, not make causal or statistical claims. Projects self-select into the competition through an open global call through which they are incentivized to emphasize innovation and positive impact. The dataset is not representative of urban initiatives globally in any way, and does not claim to provide insights into “average,” stalled, or unsuccessful efforts. We do not include a counterfactual analysis and a negative-case analysis approach is not within the scope of the paper, but such an approach could be a possible direction for future research.

Data and documentation limitations: The study relied primarily on qualitative documentation generated through the Prize process. These sources are rich but uneven. Because applications are self-reported, the depth and clarity of information vary across submissions. The Prize team conducted extensive due diligence on the information submitted by the finalists during the evaluation phase. Language translations (Portuguese, Spanish, Hindi, or Norwegian) contributed to unevenness, potentially flattening nuance, especially around accounts of governance or social processes.

## Findings

This section presents how climate readiness first emerged and what forms of transformative change the finalists generated (see “Transformative impacts across Prize finalists”), what scaling processes were driving impact (see “Scaling processes that expand and deepen change”), and the underlying infrastructures that enabled it to deepen over time (see “Infrastructures of climate readiness and urban transformation”). Unless otherwise noted, we drew quantitative figures from finalist documentation and due-diligence materials produced through the Prize process, and findings relate to the finalist initiatives without claiming direct transferability to contexts beyond this small sample.

### Transformative impacts across Prize finalists

This section examines the climate outcomes and transformative impacts beyond climate across the five Prize finalists. We focus on tracing how concrete improvements in safety, ecology, mobility, or public space began to reshape the systems around them. The analysis is grounded in achieved outcomes, rather than mere intentions. Table 2 provides the organizing structure for the section. It pairs each initiative’s entry point and trigger moment with three layers of observed impact: climate-related outcomes (adaptation and mitigation), broader systemic outcomes, and abstract transformation dimensions. This structure helps connect empirically observed climate and urban transformation impacts to the abstract descriptors used in the literature. Reading comparatively across these layers helps detect patterns, including impact clusters, where they diverge, and how concrete improvements link to deeper forms of systemic change.

## Climate impacts

The first layer of impacts, the concrete climate outcomes, fall into four broad clusters: reductions in flood risk or improved hydrology; measures to reduce urban heat; waste- and pollution-related improvements; and, alongside these, mitigation-oriented gains in low-carbon mobility or energy efficiency. Three initiatives—Rodrigo Bueno, Kham River, and Green Community Schoolyards—produced clear reductions in flood exposure and advanced heat mitigation through shading and vegetation. Waste- and pollution-related benefits appeared in both Kham River and Re-Ciclo, and three initiatives—Rodrigo Bueno, Re-Ciclo, and the Climate Budget—achieved mitigation outcomes through energy- or transport-related improvements. The substance of these clusters becomes evident in the scale of the changes achieved:

- In Rodrigo Bueno, more than 600 new energy-efficient housing units were completed as part of an upgrading effort that elevated the canal edge, improved drainage, and introduced native vegetation, reducing flood exposure for roughly 2,600 residents.
- Along the Kham River, desilting and bank stabilization over about seven kilometers, together with the planting of more than 100,000 saplings, restored hydrological function and sharply reduced sewage-laden overflows during the monsoon.
- Green Community Schoolyards achieved comparable gains on a citywide scale: Across more than 200 sites (TPL 2025), each redesigned yard now captures over one million gallons of stormwater annually while lowering surface temperatures through shading and reflectivity from trees, turf, and permeable materials.
- Re-Ciclo’s electric-tricycle (e-tricycle) logistics and sorting systems reduced neighborhood pollution and supported citywide emissions reductions (City of Fortaleza 2022).
- The Climate Budget in Oslo’s transition to zero-emission buses, ferries, and construction sites reached 77 percent of municipal operations, thereby cutting noise and air pollution alongside greenhouse gases (City of Oslo 2025).

Together, these achievements hint at the importance of layered, mutually reinforcing improvements rather than singular impacts. Restored hydrology, reduced heat, cleaner air, safer housing, and lower emissions each address specific stresses, but their combined effect makes urban systems more stable and reliable. ### Abstract markers of urban transformation

Beyond these concrete and systemic changes, the coded data also surfaced five “abstract” (conceptual) dimensions of urban transformation that the literature identifies (Wolfram et al. 2016; Grandin et al. 2018). Systemic reconfiguration refers to changes in how a service or system works and is most clearly illustrated by Re-Ciclo, where waste logistics, labor conditions, digital routing, cycling mobility, and circular-economy practices were redesigned along the waste supply chain. Cross-sector integration—coordination across domains—is visible in Green Community Schoolyards, where schools, stormwater management, design, and community groups acted together, and similarly in Kham River, which aligned water, waste, ecology, culture, and schools along the basin. Durability comes through strongly in Oslo’s Climate Budget, which embeds climate goals into annual routines. Multi-actor collaboration is especially pronounced in Kham River, with municipal bodies, EcoSattva, the Varroc Foundation, the cantonment board, schools, and community groups acting jointly. Finally, narrative or political-cultural shifts appear vividly in Rodrigo Bueno, where eviction logics gave way to a rights-based understanding of dignified urbanization. We hypothesize that a counterfactual analysis would show that what distinguishes these finalists is not the presence of one or two such features—which many urban projects may exhibit—but the dense and layered presentation of all five dimensions in every case, a pattern that suggests the saturation associated with more mature urban transformation. Such a counterfactual analysis would be a valuable future research avenue.

Taken together, the range of impacts present across finalists illustrates how transformation begins in different places, yet it may be characterized by the cumulative depth and spread of its impacts. The finalists show this quite clearly: In their respective urban contexts, their climate actions opened space for wider social, institutional, and narrative shifts that, in combination, form a dense layering of change—the kind of saturation that transformation scholars describe as an early sign of a system moving in a new direction. While these patterns are not unique to the finalists, they help clarify what transformation looks like as it takes hold.

### Scaling processes that expand and deepen change

This section examines the scaling processes that shaped how the finalists’ early climate actions evolved over time, guiding initial efforts toward more durable forms of resilience and systemic change. Across the five cases, scaling unfolded through a set of recurring dynamics that either expanded the reach of early actions or strengthened their foundations. The three most prominent dynamics—scaling deep, scaling out, and scaling up—structure the subsections that follow. Two additional patterns, scaling across and scaling down, appeared in more selective ways and are discussed in the cross-case synthesis at the end of the section. These dynamics trace a recurring (though non-causal, non-sequential, and non-deterministic) pattern: Small-scale efforts gained depth and legitimacy, extended into new sites and partnerships, and became anchored within governance systems and everyday routines. Understanding how these different forms of growth unfolded is essential for seeing how localized interventions began to contribute to wider transformation. #### Scaling deep

Scaling deep took several forms across the finalists, each reflecting how smaller-scale, early climate actions gained meaning, legitimacy, and a sense of shared purpose. In some settings, deepening emerged through shifts in public understanding as residents came to see familiar places differently (Kham River, Green Community Schoolyards). In others, it appeared through the gradual consolidation of new administrative routines or the strengthening of collective identity (Re-Ciclo, Rodrigo Bueno). Although these changes were often subtle at the outset, they established the internal coherence and early stability that later phases of work depended on.

## Scaling out

Scaling out involved the spatial or operational widening of early climate actions as they moved into new sites, corridors, and districts. For some finalists, actions progressed along continuous physical or ecological systems. For others, they expanded through successive waves of site-by-site replication or through programmatic extensions into new urban areas. Although often modest at first, these forms of widening strengthened the visibility, reach, and cumulative influence of early steps, gradually reshaping how climate considerations appeared across the urban landscape.

One common mode of scaling out involved phased, corridor-based expansion. Along the Kham River in Aurangabad, restoration advanced as local capacity and community engagement grew, linking improvements along the river system and creating ecological continuity. Similar forms of extension

appeared in Oslo, where cycling lanes were expanded into a 100-kilometer network across successive climate-budget cycles. In both cases, widening proceeded through serial additions to interconnected networks.

Another mode centered on adapting a shared model to diverse local contexts. In New York City, the Trust for Public Land applied a common design approach to waves of schoolyard conversions on different campuses, each producing site-specific cooling, drainage, and environmental benefits that extended beyond school boundaries. With each redesign, a new pocket of climate resilience infrastructure appeared, and over time these accumulated into a wider pattern across multiple boroughs.

A third mode involved incremental operational extensions. In Fortaleza's Re-Ciclo program, cargo-bike recycling advanced district by district as riders, routes, and logistical routines expanded. Each step revealed new operational needs that were incorporated into the next phase, allowing gradual but steady enlargement of the initiative's reach.

Together, these forms of scaling out increased the geographic and operational footprint of early actions. As activities widened, they drew in additional partners, required more consistent coordination, and raised expectations for maintenance and resourcing, in particular funding. These pressures often prompted efforts to formalize roles and responsibilities, setting the stage for the institutional anchoring explored through scaling up.

## Scaling up

Scaling up involves embedding climate actions within the formal procedures, mandates, and routines that give them durability. Across the finalists, this anchoring occurred through several mechanisms. In some cases, climate measures became part of regular administrative requirements or budgeting cycles. In others, new mandates, agreements, or protocols clarified roles and responsibilities. Scaling up was also visible where practices were incorporated into broader programs or long-term investment plans. Although these mechanisms differed in emphasis, each shifted climate action from a series of promising activities toward more stable elements of urban governance.

One mode of scaling up centered on the integration of climate considerations into existing administrative routines. In Oslo, the climate budget became a recurring governance tool through which emissions accounting and sector targets were embedded in municipal decision-making. The budget is published annually with clear expectations for departmental responsibilities, and climate considerations have become an explicit requirement in case processing. These procedural anchors helped move climate action from discretionary projects to a regular part of administrative practice.

Across the five finalists, the dominant scaling processes—scaling deep, scaling out, and scaling up—rarely unfolded in isolation. ## Infrastructures of climate readiness and urban transformation

The variation in scaling trajectories observed across the five finalists raises the question of whether and how these trajectories are—at least in part—a product of differences in the underlying conditions that supported or constrained the initiatives' abilities to scale deep, out, and up early climate actions. Across cases, our analysis clustered these underlying conditions, which we term “infrastructures,” consistently into seven domains. Although diverse in form, they behaved less like discrete skills or attributes and indeed more like infrastructures: foundational systems of relationships, routines, and resources that shape what kinds of scaling moves are possible and how far they can travel. We found the concept useful to capture the more practical and context-specific foundations that climate actions depended on as they matured, including the distinction between high and low human and financial resource bases; cities with evolved (or “thick”) governance landscapes; and the presence of human capital, community, and technological assets:

- ■ **Governance infrastructure** influenced whether early actions could secure authorizing environments, cross-agency backing, or procedural footholds—the conditions that made institutional anchoring possible.
- ■ **Human and organizational infrastructure** shaped the operational side of scaling: the ability of teams to mobilize staff, coordinate across departments, carry institutional memory forward, and adapt workflows as projects expanded or became more complex.
- ■ **Knowledge and learning infrastructure** helped actors interpret new information, identify emerging challenges, and refine approaches, creating shared understanding and adaptive routines that sustained scaling over time.

- ■ **Physical infrastructure**—ecological assets, public spaces, street networks—shaped where interventions could extend and how easily they could link into wider systems.
- ■ **Technological infrastructure** provided the data, digital platforms, and operational tools that allowed efforts to be coordinated, monitored, or expanded efficiently.
- ■ **Societal infrastructure**, including networks of trust, civic norms, and community relationships, supported the legitimacy, participation, and collective ownership that were central to deepening and sustaining action.
- ■ Finally, **financial infrastructure** influenced the pace and durability of scaling by enabling continuity across funding cycles and permitting flexibility in moments where adaptation was required.

We take these infrastructures as constituting the practical foundations that shape how climate actions began, evolved, and connected to wider systems in the case of the five finalists. As we discuss in the remainder of this section, we found that it was the particular mix and thickness of these infrastructures—not any single one—that shaped the feasibility, direction, and momentum of scaling.

Oslo offers the clearest example of an outlier at one end of the spectrum, illustrating what becomes possible when several infrastructures of climate readiness are already thick before any new action begins. Governance infrastructure was exceptionally strong: A statutory climate mandate and well-established cross-department routines meant that climate considerations were already embedded in how the city made decisions. Financial and technological infrastructures reinforced this anchoring—budgets were stable and predictable, data systems were mature, and agencies were accustomed to monitoring and reporting performance. Human and organizational infrastructure was similarly well developed, with experienced staff, long-standing interagency relationships, and administrative continuity.

Because so many infrastructures were in place from the start, Oslo's first scaling moves took the form of scaling up: Climate goals and responsibilities were institutionalized early, long before visible deepening or scaling out occurred. This strong platform then enabled scaling out, as sector plans, mobility projects, and emissions-oriented measures expanded across the city with relatively little friction. Scaling deep—the growth of public understanding, professional norms, and shared narratives around the climate budget—emerged more gradually through repeated implementation and reporting cycles. Oslo illustrates how thick infrastructures shape not only the pace but also the sequence of scaling: scaling up first, widening next, and deepening later. It is

a case where climate readiness existed at the outset and matured through iteration rather than being built from scratch.

In Aurangabad's Kham River initiative, the infrastructural configuration was almost the inverse of Oslo's. Where Oslo began with thick governance, financial, and technological infrastructures, Aurangabad's early strengths lay in societal, human and organizational, knowledge and learning, and to some extent technological infrastructures brought in by project partners. EcoSattva's technical approach, Varroc Foundation's early support, and active community networks created a practical basis for action even though formal mandates, routine coordination mechanisms, and budgetary commitments were still thin.

These infrastructures shaped a different starting point: Rather than beginning with scaling up, the initiative began with scaling deep, strengthening awareness, shared norms, and visible improvements that shifted how residents and municipal actors understood the river. The physical river system itself—an ecological spine running through the city—enabled early scaling out, as improvements progressed along the river's length. As widening advanced, it exposed missing governance, human and organizational, and financial infrastructures that were necessary to sustain the work. Over time, these infrastructures thickened through new cooperative agreements, task forces, district-level participation, clearer operational routines, expanded technical teams, and more predictable funding sources. In contrast to Oslo's readiness-from-the-outset model, Aurangabad's case shows how infrastructures of climate readiness can be built through practice: Deepening and widening came first, and scaling up followed as governance, organizational, and financial infrastructures matured in response to the demands created by implementation.

A third pattern emerges in New York City, where infrastructures were neither aligned in the way Oslo's were nor built progressively through practice as in Aurangabad, but were instead strong in some domains and precarious in others. In several cases, scaling deep strengthened societal and knowledge infrastructures as actors developed new routines of collaboration, learned through experimentation, or built trust and recognition through repeated engagement. These relational and cognitive shifts made subsequent stages of scaling—whether spatial expansion or institutional anchoring—more feasible and better aligned.

Scaling up, meanwhile, frequently catalyzed new governance routines and organizational practices as actions became formalized in mandates, protocols, or budgetary processes. Across the finalists, readiness grew through this continuous interplay: Infrastructures enabled scaling, and scaling, in turn, reinforced or reorganized the infrastructures themselves.

## Conclusion: reflections and priority actions

Across the finalists, climate-ready transformation occurred through establishing shared meaning, diagnosing context, creating openings for spillover, learning through early action, anchoring what works, and building the infrastructures that helped new practices take root. Based on a small subset of successful practices (the finalists), these insights offer practitioners a grounded set of actions that help translate early action into lasting, system-shaping transformation. The five climate initiatives did not stay confined to the problem that first brought people together. They became platforms through which wider forms of urban change took shape: increased social inclusion, strengthened institutions, and shifts in mobility, social cohesion, and spatial integration.

Across the five finalists, these shifts accumulated gradually, revealing a set of scaling processes that recurred across very different cities and institutional settings. This section concludes

## 1. Recognizing that transformation has many beginnings and a shared rhythm

Each finalist began in its own way. Some initiatives were sparked by residents, community groups, or frontline workers who understood the problem from their lived experiences. Others began with mandates, budgets, or political directives that authorized early action. Still others took shape through physical interventions—in river systems, schoolyards, or mobility networks—that made new possibilities visible before the institutional scaffolding was fully in place.

Yet across these varied beginnings, a broad developmental rhythm appeared. Early phases typically involved some form of meaning-making or mandate-building that helped legitimize the work. As early wins accumulated, teams steadied the effort—often through light but reliable governance routines—before widening into new places or uses. Over time, these expanding footprints settled into everyday norms and expectations, marking a quieter phase of mainstreaming. This is not a fixed sequence, and not every initiative followed the same order. But taken together, the cases reveal a recurring arc that practitioners can recognize and work with.

**Priority action:** Recognize the phase you are in—early meaning-making, stabilizing routines, widening, or mainstreaming—and strengthen it intentionally.

## 2. Diagnosing your city's starting position

One of the clearest lessons from the finalists is that climate action does not start from a blank slate. Each initiative began with a different mix of strengths and gaps: existing community networks or civic norms; well-defined mandates or budgeting tools; experienced operational teams; ecological corridors or physical assets; existing monitoring systems; or distributed funding channels. These starting configurations shaped how the work could deepen, widen, and anchor in its early phases. Some cities

could formalize early; others needed visible tests or community momentum; still others followed the logic of physical systems.

Practitioners cannot choose their starting point, but they can understand it. A simple assessment of what is already strong and what is thin helps determine which early steps will take hold and which will stall. Climate readiness grows when strategy aligns with the system that exists.

**Priority action:** Conduct a clear-eyed diagnostic early—identify what can carry weight now, what needs strengthening, and where early wins are most feasible.

## 3. Designing efforts that can spill outward into adjacent systems

Across the finalists, the most transformative outcomes emerged when climate actions generated openings into other domains. Schoolyards became climate infrastructure, learning spaces, and community assets (NYC). Waste-collection pilots became mobility and circular-economy platforms (Fortaleza). River restoration became governance reform, cultural revival, and downstream integration (Aurangabad). These shifts were not the product of master plans, but of interventions designed with permeable edges that allowed new partners, uses, and opportunities over time. Transformation unfolded laterally rather than linearly: Ideas, responsibilities, and benefits migrated into adjacent systems because early steps created room for them to do so. Designing for spillover—not containment—is what allowed climate actions to grow beyond their initial remit.

**Priority action:** Build early efforts with open boundaries so that new actors, sectors, and users can join when momentum builds by developing and expanding existing participatory infrastructures, such as decision-making, including which decisions can be influenced by community stakeholders and other multistakeholder engagement processes.

## 4. Using early interventions as learning devices

First interventions mattered not because they were perfect, but because they made the work tangible. They revealed where systems were fragile, where coordination barriers arose, how residents responded, and where new alliances could form. Early changes also built visibility and legitimacy—qualities that proved essential for sustaining attention and attracting partners.

In Kham River and Rodrigo Bueno, early ecological or spatial improvements shifted local expectations. In Re-Ciclo, the first e-tricycle routes functioned as performance tests and narrative catalysts. In New York, prototypes of redesigned schoolyards

## 5. Establishing a governance anchor that is light but reliable

All five finalists relied on at least one form of governance anchor that steadied their work through moments of political, financial, or operational uncertainty. These anchors varied widely—from statutory mandates to recurring budgeting processes, interagency agreements, or memoranda of understanding—but they served the same function: providing continuity. They did not prevent disagreements or turnover, but kept early actions from dissipating when attention waned or circumstances shifted. The lesson is not that climate action requires heavy bureaucracy. On the contrary, the most effective anchors were often simple: a routine, a partnership, a shared protocol. What mattered was their reliability and resilience against political or other contextual changes and challenges.

**Priority action:** Secure a minimal but durable governance anchor—light enough to adapt, strong enough to steady the work.

## 6. Building the infrastructures that sustain mainstreaming

Transformation took root when new practices became ordinary. This occurred when teams invested in the infrastructures—human and organizational capacity, knowledge and learning routines, societal norms, physical upkeep, technology, and predictable financing—that allowed the work to continue without exceptional effort. These infrastructures rarely arrived fully formed; they grew through cycles of practice, feedback, and adaptation. But without them, progress could not settle into everyday expectations. Mainstreaming is the point at which climate-readiness initiatives begin contributing to broader, longer-term transformation.

**Priority action:** Invest in staffing, routines, community practices, maintenance cycles, and learning loops that help new practices settle into everyday life.

While this paper focuses on five high-performing initiatives recognized through the Prize’s 2023–2024 cycle, the findings point toward several directions for further research that could deepen understanding of climate readiness and its role in urban trans-

First, comparative research across a broader range of cities—the wider pool of prize applicants, as well as initiatives that stalled, adapted in different ways, or failed to scale—would help test how the infrastructures of climate readiness identified here operate under less favorable conditions. We include in this the study of cities in different resource contexts, as highlighted by the juxtaposition of the Climate Budget (Oslo) with Kham River (Aurangabad). We believe there is much to be gained in exploring different starting points through the concept of readiness infrastructures. Such work could clarify which infrastructures are most critical at different moments, and how trade-offs or constraints can differently shape scaling trajectories. Second, longitudinal research that follows climate-readiness initiatives over longer time horizons could shed light on how infrastructures evolve as practices move from experimentation into routine governance, and how gains in readiness are sustained or eroded through political and fiscal change. Third, closer examination of how climate-readiness processes interact with equity outcomes—particularly who benefits, who bears costs, and who participates in shaping trajectories—would strengthen links among readiness, justice, and inclusive urban development. Overall, further research grounded in diverse urban contexts would help refine the framework proposed here, support more nuanced policy design, and guide practitioners seeking to align climate action with longer-term urban transformation.

**Location:** Oslo, Norway

Oslo’s Climate Budget is a first-of-its-kind financial mechanism that integrates carbon accounting into the city’s annual budgeting process, making emissions reductions a binding requirement for all municipal departments. First introduced in 2016 by the Oslo Climate Agency, the initiative was designed to ensure that Oslo’s ambitious net-zero emissions goal by 2030 was not just a political commitment but an operational priority. By treating carbon dioxide emissions like financial expenditures, the Climate Budget assigns specific reduction targets to city departments, requiring them to implement measurable, enforceable strategies.

A key factor in the Climate Budget’s success is cross-agency coordination, facilitated by the Oslo Climate Agency, which ensures that emissions targets align with broader city planning and fiscal priorities. The agency has played a central role in refining the budget’s methodology, expanding its emissions tracking capabilities, and strengthening interdepartmental collaboration. Today, the Climate Budget guides Oslo’s efforts in sustainable mobility, procurement, and construction, while influencing national climate policy and serving as a model for cities worldwide.

### Green Community Schoolyards—Trust for Public Land

**Location:** New York City, United States

**Implementing organization:** Trust for Public Land (TPL)

**Key partners:** New York City government, New York City School Construction Authority, community organizations

**Workshop participants:** TPL: Arissa Lahr (Senior Program Coordinator), Maddalena Polletta (Environmental Educator, NYC Playgrounds Program), Joan Keener (Deputy Director, NYC Playgrounds Program), Mary Alice Lee (Director at the Trust for Public Land)

New York City’s lack of accessible green space and growing climate risks—including urban heat islands and flooding—have disproportionately impacted lower-income and historically marginalized communities.
