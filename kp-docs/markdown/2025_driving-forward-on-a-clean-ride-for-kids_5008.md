# Driving Forward on a Clean Ride for Kids

EXPERT NOTE
Expert Note  |  Version 2  |  June 2025  |  1
Summary
The vast majority of our nation’s 480,000 school buses run on diesel fuel, whose 
emissions endanger the health of the more than 20 million children who ride them 
every day (School Bus Fleet Magazine 2020). Diesel engine exhaust, which has been 
classified as carcinogenic, can harm both physical health and cognitive development, 
as well as compromise school attendance and learning (WHO 2012). In response, 
school districts in every corner of the country, in red and blue states alike, are 
seeking the benefits of electric school buses, but more action is needed. Foremost, 
the federal government should continue to disburse funding allocated by Congress 
under the Clean School Bus Program (CSBP).
Electric school buses provide thousands of dollars in operational cost savings every 
year, help grow US manufacturing, and create opportunities for grid resiliency. 
They are currently operating in 49 states. To date, every funding opportunity made 
available through the CSBP has seen significant demand. As school districts across 
the country seek to adopt electric school buses, they need continued support to 
upgrade their aging fleets and provide students with a clean ride to school. This 
expert note synthesizes existing evidence and expert knowledge to recommend how 
federal and state policymakers can help continue to drive the nation forward in 
electrifying school buses and strengthening the clean school bus economy.
Introduction
With about a half million of them nationwide, school buses are among the most 
ubiquitous vehicles in the United States ( Smithsonian Magazine 2022). They 
transport more than 20 million students every day, in every state and every type 
of community (School Bus Fleet Magazine 2020). The vast majority of school buses 
on the road burn fossil fuels, primarily diesel, which is linked to serious health 
problems and can compromise academic and cognitive development (Beatty and 
Shimshack 2011; Austin et al. 2019). Diesel exhaust pollutants can lead to asthma, 
cancer, and other respiratory illnesses (US EPA 2024a). Asthma is one of the 
leading causes of school absenteeism (Hsu et al. 2017).

Electric school buses (ESBs) are better for air quality, health, and operating costs than any other type of school bus. They are 
successfully operating in all types of climates and in urban, suburban, and rural locales—all while safely and reliably transporting 
students, saving school districts fuel and maintenance costs, supporting manufacturing jobs, and creating opportunities for 
resiliency. There is strong and widespread support for school bus electrification across the country. Currently, there are nearly 
14,000 electric school buses on the road or coming soon, to over 1,500 school districts across the United States; there is already at 
least one electric school bus on the road in 49 states (WRI 2025).
Strong interest in ESBs
The Clean School Bus Program (CSBP) is a voluntary program established by the Bipartisan Infrastructure Law to provide $5 
billion over five years (FY 2022–26) to replace existing school buses with cleaner options (US Congress 2021). The CSBP has 
been the single-biggest driver of electric school bus deployment, funding approximately two-thirds of buses (WRI 2025). School 
districts can choose to apply for funding to replace existing diesel school buses with electric buses or other clean-fuel options. As 
of June 2024, the CSBP had announced nearly $3 billion for 966 awards. This investment supports the purchase of more than 
8,500 ESBs and charging infrastructure at more than 1,200 school districts across 49 states, 4 US territories, and Washington, 
DC, including 65 Tribal school districts. Together, these school districts serve 16 million students. Over 2,000 ESBs funded by 
the CSBP have already been delivered to school districts, and more are on their way (Zepka 2024b). 
High demand for CSBP support has far outpaced the available funding, demonstrating that school districts and fleet operators 
are eager to bring the benefits of ESBs to their communities. More than 3,300 school districts and fleet operators applied for the 
first three rounds of CSBP funding. In the first round, requests totaled nearly $4 billion for more than 12,000 school buses—of 
which 90 percent were electric. This was nearly eight times the $500 million that the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) 
had anticipated making available, prompting it to nearly double the funding amount. In two subsequent rounds, high demand 
continued, outpacing the amount of funding originally available. In the fourth and most recent round, which closed in January 
2025, high demand persisted, as nearly 500 applicants from 46 states as well as territories and Tribal districts applied for over 
$900 million in funding (US EPA 2025).
Widespread, bipartisan demand for ESBs
The demand for ESBs and the CSBP has been not only strong but also widespread. Interest has spanned both red and blue states 
and congressional districts, as depicted in Figure 1. States in which a majority voted to elect Republican Donald Trump in the 
November 2024 presidential election were awarded more funding and ESBs under the CSBP than states in which a majority 
voted to elect Democrat Kamala Harris (Zepka 2024a). 
Opportunities for new manufacturing jobs
Electric school buses also offer the opportunity to maintain and grow US-based manufacturing, supporting jobs across the 
supply chain and throughout the country. Over 97 percent of all school buses sold in the United States are manufactured in the 
United States by US workers (Polk Registrations and Commercial Vehicles 2024). The ESB supply chain has outsized economic 
impact in Georgia, Indiana, Michigan, North Carolina, Oklahoma, and West Virginia, where major facilities are located. There 
are 21 manufacturing facilities in 13 states making parts involved in the production of ESBs, as seen in the map in Figure 2. In 
response to the high level of interest in ESBs, three major ESB manufacturers have announced plans for more than 1,500 new 
manufacturing jobs combined in the coming years (GreenPower Motor Company 2022; Blue Bird 2024; High Point Enterprise 
2022). Additional workers are needed to install and maintain charging infrastructure. Further detail is included in Electric School 
Bus Initiative (2025).

Supporting rural and low-income schools
A hallmark of the CSBP has been its focus on ensuring that rural and low-income schools can take advantage of the benefits 
of ESBs. Before the CSBP, the total number of committed ESBs was just under 1,000, and rural districts accounted for only 
20 percent of school districts with at least one ESB. Thanks to rural prioritization criteria set by Congress, that figure is now 
36 percent; moreover, the distribution of ESBs nationwide now aligns with the distribution of all school district locales (urban, 
suburban, town, rural), as demonstrated in Figure 3. In addition, ESB concentrations have shifted from higher- to lower-income 
districts. Before the CSBP, the largest percentage of all ESBs was in districts with the least amount of low-income households. As 
of June 2024, 62 percent of electric school buses were in districts with the highest amount of low-income households. In short, the 
CSBP has provided a boost to rural and low-income communities seeking the health, operational cost savings, and other benefits 
of electric school buses (Zepka 2024b).
Approximately $3 billion in CSBP funding has been awarded, but not all of it has been disbursed, and an additional $2 billion 
remains to award in future rounds. Continuing disbursing funds from the Bipartisan Infrastructure Law and Inflation Reduction 
Act is critical to providing economic, health, and resiliency benefits for children and communities across the country.
Policy implications and recommendations
Maintaining federal support for manufacturing and deploying electric school buses is critical to respond to consumer choice, 
support US manufacturing jobs, and preserve momentum for advancing a cleaner school bus fleet. These efforts need to be 
bolstered by support from state governments and regulatory bodies. 
Federal policy recommendations
 ▪ The US EPA should continue to disburse all awarded CSBP funding to honor awards made to school districts and ensure business 
certainty for manufacturers and dealers . Maintaining this support ensures continuity for American manufacturing and American 
workers and the ability to leverage complementary federal, state and private investments.

 ▪ Congress and the US EPA should ensure that the remaining $2 billion in CSBP funding is awarded to meet demonstrated demand 
and continue support for clean bus manufacturing . The most recent call for applications closed in January 2025, and hundreds of 
applicants are now awaiting responses to those requests. The EPA should issue funding opportunity announcements to spend 
remaining funds and ensure that the funding is reaching communities throughout the country. This support should continue 
to follow the prioritization set by Congress for rural, low-income, high-need, and Tribal schools.
 ▪ Congress should preserve tax credits and elective pay and transferability mechanisms so that school districts can access credits to achieve 
cost savings from electric school buses . As a complement to the CSBP, federal incentives like the Qualified Commercial Clean 
Vehicle Credit (45W) and the Alternative Fuel Vehicle Refueling Property Credit (30C) incentivize school districts to invest 
in cleaner technologies such as ESBs and related charging infrastructure. Elective pay and transferability mechanisms provide 
a new and streamlined way for tax-exempt entities, like school districts, to access these tax credits. Energy tax credits—
and the business benefits, innovation, and market certainty they provide—enjoy bipartisan support. Twenty-one House 
Republicans recently sent a letter to the House Ways and Means Committee to limit changes to recent energy tax credits, and 
Republican members spoke up in support of tax credits during a recent hearing (US Congress 2025; Bikales 2025).
State policy recommendations
 ▪ State governors, legislatures, and agencies should maintain and grow funding support that is essential to strengthening and 
complementing federal measures. States have been at the forefront of ESB deployment, providing incentives, technical 
assistance, and infrastructure and workforce development support. To date, states are collectively providing over $3 billion in 
support for vehicle and infrastructure purchases (WRI 2025). Many states, such as North Carolina, which includes an ESB 
manufacturing facility, began by using funding from the Volkswagen emissions settlement (North Carolina Division of Air 
Quality 2022). More recently, states such as Michigan and Washington have created new programs (Michigan Department 
of Energy 2024; Washington Department of Ecology 2023). Additional states can act to support greater growth and stretch 
federal dollars farther.
 ▪ Not withstanding the potential for federal interventions to overturn the Environmental Protection Agency’s waivers of federal 
preemption for the Advanced Clean Trucks (ACT) vehicle emissions programs, state governors and legislatures should maintain 
existing and add additional transition targets that provide important certainty to the market . Currently 16 states have in place state 
legislative rules or have adopted the Advanced Clean Trucks rule or both (CARB 2025; Chacon et al. 2024). Collectively, such 
measures mean that approximately 35 percent of the school buses in the United States will need to be replaced by ESBs in the 
coming years (WRI 2025). Additional states can act, and allowing states to move forward on these measures provides them 
with the flexibility to achieve state clean air goals and certainty for the market.
 ▪ State legislatures, public utility commissions, and other governing bodies should support utility infrastructure “make-ready” programs 
and fleet advisory services to help support the adoption of ESBs . These state-level actors can support utility programs and direct 
utilities to include ESBs in their planning efforts. According to WRI research, there are over 200 programs across utilities 
in 33 states that may provide support for ESB charging infrastructure or offer rates or other incentives to help school bus 
depot sites get ready for charging infrastructure; states can help add to these offerings (WRI and Clean Energy Works 2024). 
States can also support utilities offering fleet advisory services to help school districts plan how to move forward. For example, 
PSEG Long Island (2025) offers both make-ready support—for upgrades to panels, switchgear, trenching, wirings, and other 
changes—as well as free fleet advisory services to understand the process and how to work to get necessary service.
 ▪ State agencies should provide supportive technical assistance and support workforce training. The transition to ESBs can raise 
questions around new approaches to procurement, deployment, and operation and maintenance that state agencies, such 
as energy offices and environment departments, are well suited to support. This can be especially important for low-
income districts and others that may need extra assistance. For example, the Massachusetts Clean Energy Center (2023) 
provides technical assistance and electrification planning, including training drivers and mechanics through its Fleet 
Deployment Program.

 ▪ State agencies should develop financing mechanisms through state infrastructure banks . The electric school bus market is ripe to 
benefit from financing programs, such as through state infrastructure banks, that can help address higher upfront costs 
by capitalizing future operating savings. For example, Connecticut provides low-interest, long-term financing for electric 
school buses, charging equipment, and electric system upgrades, with additional opportunities for projects in underserved 
communities (Connecticut Green Bank 2023).
Additional details on these and additional state actions are included in Electric School Bus Initiative (2024). 
Discussion
School districts are seeking support for ESBs due to a host of benefits. Top of mind for many schools is the significant operational 
cost savings from each new bus, on the order of $6,000 per year (Levinson et al. 2025). Those savings add up and can help offset 
needs in other areas. In Fleming County, Kentucky, the rural school district has been able to use their savings from deploying 
ESBs to invest in staff and classroom resources. Once they have all 24 planned ESBs on the road, thanks to CSBP grants and 
rebates, Fleming County projects annual cost savings of more than $600,000 (Kowalcyk et al. 2025).
Many school districts are eager to deploy ESBs to help address air quality issues in their community and support related health 
benefits. This is what drove Salt Lake City School District in Utah to begin its ESB journey several years ago (Huntington 2022). 
Thanks to support from the CSBP and other federal and state programs, it is currently on track to deploy 12 ESBs, or 12 percent 
of its fleet of 100 buses (WRI 2025). It hopes to add to that soon, based on the selection of the Utah Division of Air Quality to 
receive additional federal funding in support of 120 ESBs across the state (US EPA 2024b).
Unlike other types of buses, ESBs can mitigate vulnerability to extreme weather events and provide grid resiliency thanks to their 
giant rolling batteries. This feature is especially compelling to areas that experience natural disasters such as wildfires, hurricanes, 
and flooding. In Oklahoma City, which faces frequent extreme weather events, including tornadoes and storms, the Shawnee 
School District is partnering with Oklahoma Gas and Electric, ESB manufacturers, the University of Oklahoma, the Indian 
Nations Council of Governments, and other stakeholders to develop a vehicle-to-grid solution. This will provide energy back to 
the grid during times of need and could develop into a scalable model for community resilience (NSF 2025).
Electric school buses are delivering health benefits to children, driving US manufacturing and jobs, providing cost savings for 
school districts, and increasing resiliency to extreme weather events. To maintain this progress, federal and state incentives need 
to continue to support school districts electrifying their fleets. Together, we can keep driving forward on a clean ride for kids.
