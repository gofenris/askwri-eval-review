### Highlights

- This paper draws on the WRI Ross Center for Sustainable Cities' (WRI Ross Center's) Deep Dive Cities Initiative (DDCI)—a six-year, multicity effort—to examine how flexible funding was used to sustain long-horizon urban transformation work under uncertain political and institutional conditions. The analysis centers on teams within World Resources Institute operating across sectors to support coordination, learning, and implementation in complex urban systems.
- The paper offers insights relevant to peer organizations operating in similar institutional and mixed-funding contexts and consolidates learning from the DDCI for WRI Ross Center's own practice.
- Across the DDCI, flexible funding—funding not tied to predefined activities or delivery timelines through a grant agreement—was used in four recurring ways: seed funding to test fit and enable strategic entry; add-on funding to integrate adjacent agendas; policy life-cycle funding to support continuity from planning into implementation; and gap funding to sustain presence through disruptions.
- The analysis reveals tensions that can emerge when flexible funding is deployed alongside program funds, requiring deliberate organizational consideration.
- The findings surface five practice-relevant questions for intermediaries using flexible funding alongside project grants: what flexible funding is available and for what purpose; what kinds of decisions it is being used to enable; how those decisions are made and revisited; how flexibility interacts with organizational capacity over time; and when continued engagement still serves the mission.

## About this working paper

**This study is analytical rather than evaluative and focuses on the three cycles of the Deep Dive Cities Initiative (2020–26).** The term “deep dive” evolved from describing an informal way of working to becoming the name of a formal internal program that administered a portion of flexible funding for place-based, cross-sector, medium-to-long-term engagement. For the analysis, we drew on documentary and archival records, including internal planning and reporting materials, and semi-structured interviews conducted across seven WRI offices.

Positionality is explicit: Two authors were directly involved in the initiative’s day-to-day management, while one author was part of WRI Ross Center but not part of the initiative. Several limitations shape how findings should be interpreted. As a single-organization case study, findings are not generalizable to the wider field of urban intermediaries, nor representative of WRI Ross Center or WRI as a whole. Projects that stalled early are underrepresented, and internal repositories may emphasize more visible work. The study makes no causal claims as it does not include a comparison group or counterfactual analysis, and outcomes reflect the efforts of multiple actors. The study period (2020–26) coincided with pandemic disruptions and fiscal contractions that may have confounded observed patterns. Finally, not all internal data are reported here; dynamics around resource allocation and

organizational sensitivities informed the analysis but are not elaborated in depth.

## Key findings and practice implications

**Across city engagements, flexible funding was used alongside project-based funding rather than in isolation, shaping how intermediary teams navigated different moments in city work.** Flexible funding was deployed in four recurring ways. Seed funding enabled early exploration, testing, and strategic entry into new cities or sectors. Add-on funding allowed teams to layer adjacent agendas onto existing engagements without reopening core project scopes. Policy life-cycle funding supported continuity from plan adoption into the early stages of implementation. Gap funding supported continuity of engagement during periods of political transition, procurement delays, or funding gaps, helping teams maintain relationships and positioning during intermittent periods of uncertainty of relatively short duration (e.g., one to six months). These uses were selective and overlapping rather than sequential, reflecting adaptation to context rather than a fixed progression. Across these uses, flexible funding created space for experimentation, research, and knowledge production, including the development of data platforms, methodological frameworks, and policy tools. In some cases, these traveled beyond individual projects and city engagements, informing national policy processes and adaptation elsewhere.

**The analysis shows that while flexible funding reshapes intermediary practice by increasing discretion over timing, sequencing, and scope of work, it also introduces potential tensions and questions to be navigated.** For instance, its deployment involved adjusting internal expectations and strategic guidelines, timing, organizational capacity, and judgment about how flexible resources complemented existing project commitments, including when to sustain, extend, or reconsider engagement. Increased discretion introduced trade-offs related to continuity, strategic coherence, accountability, and organizational capacity, particularly as work evolved in response to changing political and institutional conditions.

**The findings point to a set of practice-relevant considerations for intermediary organizations using flexible funding alongside project-based grants.** Rather than prescribing solutions, the analysis frames these implications as questions that tend to arise when discretionary resources are available within a mixed-funding environment.

- ■ **First, what flexible funding is available, and what is it intended to support?** Flexible funding may represent a small share of overall resources, but its influence often stems from the discretion it provides where project funding is tightly scoped or time bound. A first consideration is how flexible funding is positioned relative to other funding, and whether its intended uses—such as bridging

## Introduction

Urban change toward greater sustainability and resilience depends on the interaction of many actors, including city governments, civil society organizations, private firms, and community groups, operating within complex and interdependent urban systems. Cities sit at the intersection of multiple systems of provision—e.g., mobility, energy, land use, housing, and water—whose infrastructures, institutions, and political dynamics co-evolve over extended periods (Hommels 2005). This interwovenness creates path dependency, complexity, and inertia, often making purposeful intervention slow, uneven, and contested (Wolfram et al. 2016; Frantzeskaki

et al. 2017). While the policy imperative to advance climate action, social inclusion, and economic opportunity in parallel is well established (United Nations 2017; IPCC 2018; IRP 2018), progress in cities rarely follows a linear or predictable trajectory.

Within this landscape, intermediary organizations play a distinctive supporting role. Rather than exercising formal authority or delivering isolated projects, intermediaries work by connecting actors across sectors; filling evidence gaps; translating knowledge into usable forms; and sustaining engagement through periods of delay, political transition, or institutional fragmentation. Sustainability transitions and urban transformation scholarship have highlighted the importance of such actors in enabling coordination, learning, and institutional change where responsibilities and capacities are dispersed (Kivimaa et al. 2019b; Kanda et al. 2020).

Despite their importance in supporting complex urban change, intermediary organizations are typically funded through short-term, project-based funding arrangements. As a result, intermediaries often face a persistent misalignment between the temporal and relational demands of their work and the way that work is resourced in practice. Greater financial flexibility is sometimes advanced to address this misalignment, assumed to enable continuity, responsiveness, and adaptation where project-based funding falls short (Buteau et al. 2020; OECD 2024). Yet there is limited empirical insight into how such funding is used in day-to-day practice, particularly in combination with project-based grants. Existing discussions focus largely on the desirability of flexibility or on aggregate funding patterns, rather than on how intermediaries deploy flexible resources operationally, what kinds of decisions such funding enables over time, and what new constraints or trade-offs it introduces alongside project funding.

This paper addresses that gap through an in-depth case study of an initiative of WRI Ross Center for Sustainable Cities (WRI Ross Center), an international intermediary working across multiple geographies and technical domains. The Deep Dive Cities Initiative (DDCI), an internal program launched in 2020, deployed a portion of WRI Ross Center's flexible, multiyear funding alongside a broader portfolio of project-based grants to facilitate long-term, multisectoral city engagement. Rather than treating the initiative as an intervention to be evaluated, we used it as an empirical lens through which to examine how the core financial element of the initiative, this flexible funding, was used in practice within a mixed-funding environment. The findings reflect one organization's experience within a specific institutional and funding context, shaped by the limits of a single-case, insider-informed study. The paper is written primarily for peer intermediary organizations engaged in mission-driven urban work that operate within mixed-funding environments and face similar pressures in sustaining engagement over time.

## Context: why flexible funding matters for intermediary work

Urban intermediaries engaged in long-horizon urban change operate under conditions of persistent uncertainty (Kanda et al. 2020). Transformative change in cities unfolds over these extended time frames, spans multiple sectors and institutional domains, and must endure across political administrations if it is to take hold (Frantzeskaki et al. 2017; Mahendra et al. 2021). As emphasized in the urban transformation literature (e.g., van Doren et al. 2018; Frantzeskaki et al. 2018; Lam et al. 2020), such change is not achieved through the scaling of discrete projects alone, but through the gradual embedding of new norms, practices, and governance arrangements that remain in place as political leadership and priorities shift (see also Ghosh et al. 2021). Within these conditions, intermediaries work to support ambitious forms of change while exercising limited formal authority over decision-making and implementation. Their influence is largely relational, relying on their ability to connect actors, translate ideas into practice, and sustain momentum across fragmented institutional settings, particularly where uncertainty and institutional complexity are high (Kivimaa et al. 2019a). The challenge they face is therefore not only to design effective interventions, but to sustain organizational capacity, relationships, and strategic direction over time in environments where disruption, delay, and political change are routine rather than exceptional.

To play their part in long-horizon urban transformations, intermediaries require a wide repertoire of resources, including financial and human capital, institutional capacity, knowledge infrastructure, political and social trust, and the ability to respond to emerging opportunities (Meyer and Hawlik 2021). Yet many intermediaries are funded in ways that undercut these requirements. Unlike for-profit consultancies or service firms, whose engagement is typically bounded by contracts and revenue streams, urban intermediaries are usually mission-oriented nonprofit organizations whose role often entails

maintaining relationships, credibility, and presence over time even when dedicated project funding is intermittent or absent. In practice, however, they rely heavily on short-term, tightly earmarked project grants (Gregory and Howard 2009; OECD 2024). An analysis by the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development shows that project-restricted funding predominates, with flexible general support accounting for only a small minority of awards to nonprofit intermediaries, representing approximately 16 percent of grants and 19 percent of total value (OECD 2024). Similarly, data from the Center for Effective Philanthropy indicate that multiyear general operating support remains uncommon, at just 12.4 percent of grants (Buteau et al. 2020).

In addition to affecting outcomes, scholarship and practitioner commentary describe how this funding structure can shape organizational practice in consequential ways. Short-term, restricted grants are associated with employment precarity and staff turnover, as well as with managerial attention being diverted toward proposal writing and compliance reporting rather than long-term strategy (Selden and Sowa 2015; Gregory and Howard 2009). Financing gaps or lapses can also disrupt partnerships and weaken collaboration, particularly in contexts of funding scarcity where competition among intermediaries intensifies (MacIndoe and Sullivan 2014).

Beyond their effects on organizational capacity, project-based funding structures also shape the temporal organization of intermediary work. Funding is typically allocated through discrete projects with defined start and end points, approval processes, and reporting cycles. Research on the projectization of public and nonprofit work has noted how such arrangements tend to privilege bounded interventions and delivery phases, even where the underlying problems being addressed unfold unevenly and over much longer time horizons (Torrens and von Wirth 2021). For intermediaries engaged in long-horizon urban change, this temporal structuring can introduce vulnerabilities. Engagement with city governments often depends on continuity, trust-building, and the ability to adapt to emerging opportunities and risks. When work is organized primarily through successive projects, the timing of funding cycles may align imperfectly with political calendars, institutional transitions, or evolving local priorities, limiting capacity for urban transformation and shaping how intermediaries make strategic choices.

It is against this backdrop that flexible funding enters the picture. In this paper, we follow common usage in the nonprofit and philanthropic literature in referring to flexible funding as financial support that is not tightly tied to predefined activities or short delivery timelines, including both fully unrestricted and lightly restricted multiyear support (Buteau et al. 2020; OECD 2024). The literature further suggests that flexible, multiyear funding for intermediary organizations is most commonly provided by large private foundations and, more

Through a case study of one such intermediary—WRI Ross Center for Sustainable Cities—it focuses on how discretion over timing, sequencing, and scope becomes relevant in practice, and on how such discretion interacts with enduring tensions that intermediaries must navigate over time. Specifically, the paper focuses on one of WRI Ross Center’s key programs, the Deep Dive Cities Initiative. Launched in 2020, the DDCI used a flexible funding model to advance work in cities, especially across periods of uncertainty or political change. The analysis is not concerned with evaluating whether this flexible funding is superior to project-based funding, nor with assessing outcomes or impacts. Rather, it treats flexible funding as part of the operating environment for intermediaries, shaping the choices and trade-offs they face over time. We propose that these tensions do not need to be fully resolved; however, surfacing them and assessing them helps reap the full benefits that come with including flexible funds in the overall funding mix.

## Methodology

We adopted a mixed-methods case study approach that was deliberately analytical rather than evaluative. The aim was to examine processes and decision-making in context, rather than to assess results, impacts, or effectiveness. Formal evaluation of effectiveness and impacts is optimally conducted by entities that are structurally separate from program design and delivery (OECD 1992; UNEG 2016; World Bank IEG 2020). In contrast, insider and near-insider research can be well suited to explaining how organizational practices evolve

over time, particularly where the focus is on mechanisms, routines, and interpretation (Dwyer and Buckle 2009). When positionality is made explicit and reflexive practices are used to manage bias, such approaches can provide access to tacit knowledge and informal decision dynamics that are difficult to observe externally (Brannick and Coghlan 2007). This orientation follows established guidance on case study research for addressing “how” and “why” questions about change over time (George and Bennett 2005; Yin 2018).

The analysis drew on a mixed set of qualitative materials that captures both day-to-day practice and longer-term reflection within the DDCI. These included documentary and archival records, recordings and transcripts of internal meetings and learning exchanges from all country offices and the global office in Washington, DC, and semi-structured interviews with staff across seven WRI offices¹ (see Table 1; see also Tables A-1–A-4 in Appendix A). Together, these sources provided insight into how decisions were made, revisited, and adapted over time, as well as into how flexible funding was understood and used alongside project-based resources. Data analysis proceeded iteratively. We developed broad coding categories from the research questions and interview guide, and added inductive codes as patterns emerged through review of transcripts and documents. Coding focused on identifying recurring constraints, enabling conditions, and decision dynamics, as well as on how teams navigated funding boundaries, timing pressures, and shifts in institutional context. Triangulation across sources was used to check interpretations and distinguish individual perspectives from shared patterns.

The authors’ positionality is a feature of the research design. Two authors were directly involved in the DDCI, while one author was part of WRI Ross Center, but not this initiative. This combination of perspectives provided access to tacit knowledge, informal routines, and decision dynamics that would have been difficult to capture through external observation alone. At the same time, differences in proximity and role among the authors enabled internal questioning and cross-checking of interpretations against each other and against the documentary record. To manage the risks associated with insider research, the analysis was guided by an explicit and transparent research protocol. We used reflexive practices to surface assumptions, record interpretive choices, and test emerging explanations against the documentary and interview evidence (Brannick and Coghlan 2007; Wittmayer and Schäpke 2014). Where the write-up involved sensitive material, we generalized or removed geographical and personal identifiers to protect individuals and teams while preserving the substantive meaning of the cases.

## Limitations

This study has several limitations that shape how the findings should be interpreted:

- ■ **Scope and representativeness:** This is a single-organization case study that captures one intermediary's learning trajectory rather than a generalizable model. WRI Ross Center is a program, housed within a large international nongovernmental organization (World Resources Institute) with specific systems and mandates; the DDCI represents a selectively supported subset of work. Findings are therefore not representative of the wider field of urban intermediaries, particularly smaller, grassroots, or activist organizations operating under different constraints, nor is the DDCI representative of the whole of WRI Ross Center or WRI.
- ■ **Selection and survivorship:** Projects within the DDCI that persisted long enough to generate records and visible outcomes are more likely to appear in the analysis, while initiatives that stalled early are underrepresented. Internal repositories, including outcomes archives, were curated and may emphasize more visible strands of work.
- ■ **Attribution and causality:** Outcomes in complex urban systems reflect the combined efforts of multiple actors and funding streams. The study does not include a comparison group, a counterfactual analysis, or systematic process tracing, and does not make causal claims about the effects of flexible funding.
- ■ **Data and positionality:** Interviews are subject to recall and social desirability effects and were conducted in English, within WRI Ross Center's main geographies. Insider

research raises the risk of bias, which we addressed through reflexive practice and triangulation but couldn't eliminate entirely. Perspectives from funders and city counterparts were not systematically collected beyond what appears in internal records.

- ■ **Temporal context:** The study period (2020–26) included pandemic disruptions, fiscal contractions, and leadership changes in several cities. These contemporaneous shocks may have confounded relationships between funding conditions and observed organizational practices.
- ■ **Confidentiality and reporting boundaries:** While we drew on detailed internal data regarding allocation processes, decision-making structures, and the use of flexible funding, not all aspects are reported here. The account presented provides a partial view of internal processes, reflecting appropriate boundaries around proprietary information and organizational sensitivities including, for example, how allocation decisions were negotiated, perceptions of fairness and potential competition for resources. These dynamics can be sensitive in organizational and donor contexts and while they inform the analysis, they are therefore not elaborated in depth in this paper.

Taken together, these limitations reflect constraints of time, access, and scope rather than methodological oversight. We worked to mitigate them through triangulation, transparent links between claims and evidence, explicit calibration of conclusions, and reflexive practice.

## Background and scope of the case study: the Deep Dive Cities Initiative

This section provides background on the DDCI as the empirical setting for the analysis that follows. It clarifies how the term “deep dive” has been used within WRI Ross Center to refer to a long-standing way of working in cities to solve large-scale, interconnected urban problems (Martínez et al. 2021; Chanchani et al. 2022). More recently, building on this track record, the approach evolved into a formal internal program that deployed a portion of flexible, multiyear funding. This section describes the origins and institutional evolution of this initiative, as well as its basic structure, scope, and relationship to project-based funding. Figure 1 summarizes the timing of the DDCI in relation to the creation of WRI Ross Center for Sustainable Cities in 2014, and its predecessor organization, EMBARQ established in 2002. The purpose of this section is to orient readers to the organizational and programmatic context in which the practices examined in section “Flexible funding in practice” take place. As previously noted, it is descriptive rather than evaluative and does not attempt to assess the effectiveness or outcomes of the initiative.

In its earliest usage, deep dive referred informally to WRI’s approach to urban work: place based, long term, and embedded in specific city contexts. WRI’s original work in cities was carried out through EMBARQ founded in 2002 with a grant from the Shell Foundation, which emphasized sustained engagement with city governments and local partners, primarily through in-country teams that worked on strategic urban transport projects over extended periods (WRI 2025). These early, deep engagements often centered on specific technical entry points, particularly in urban mobility. EMBARQ’s work in cities such as Mexico City, and later in those in Brazil,

China, Turkey, and India, focused on supporting the planning and implementation of interventions including bus rapid transit, pedestrianization, and public transport reforms.

These efforts typically began with a defined sectoral focus, then expanded to engagement with city governments and local partners to address broader institutional and policy questions related to urban transport, land use, and the built environment. Rather than denoting a formal program or funding mechanism, deep dive emerged to describe an approach characterized by ongoing presence, relationship-building, and iterative engagement within particular urban settings. As one senior staff member recalled, “deep dive really was that, just the way we worked” (INT-01—see Appendix A, Table A-1).

Over time, this way of working became more deliberately focused on city-level transformations, through a multisectoral approach, operationalized by allocating resources in particular cities over longer periods. Within EMBARQ and later WRI Ross Center, this involved prioritizing continued presence in selected cities and deepening relationships with local governments and partner organizations. Senior staff described this approach as emphasizing continuity across political cycles—“to keep the momentum going through the cycle of a long, complex and politically precarious process” (INT-04)—and deliberately engaging with multiple actors within a city, rather than delivering isolated projects or working only in a specific sector (INT-01; INT-04; INT-10). This shift marked a move from an informal ethos of long-term engagement toward a more intentional focus on place-based multisectoral work within the organization.

The establishment of WRI Ross Center in 2014 marked a significant institutional shift in scope and ambition. Building on EMBARQ's sectoral place-based work, the new center expanded its focus beyond urban mobility to include land use, energy, resilience, housing, and equity. During this period, deep dive began to be used more explicitly to describe a strategy of transformation, driving how the newly established WRI Ross Center understood its work in cities. It also became a model in which work in selected cities was expected to inform broader engagement across cities and geographies. This framing emphasized the potential for lessons learned and approaches developed in one context to be adapted elsewhere, alongside continued attention to local political and institutional conditions.

In 2020, the DDCI was created as a formal internal program to administer a portion of the flexible, multiyear funding that had been granted by Stephen M. Ross to set up and operate WRI Ross Center (WRI 2014). While deep dive continued to describe an established way of working as well as a place-based transformational strategy, the initiative introduced a defined structure through which selected city engagements were supported over a multiyear horizon. The DDCI was organized through a series of cycles, with relatively equal distribution of flexible funding provided to each WRI office for each cycle. Each office then selected cities to work in and thematic priorities to focus on based on a set of internal guidelines reflecting WRI's strategic focus and vision (WRI 2024) as well as the opportunities the offices identified in each city. Flexible funding under the initiative was purposefully deployed alongside flexible funding for other purposes, including operational costs, project-based grants, and other resources, and was accompanied by expectations regarding focus, duration, and reporting. ## Flexible funding in practice: four modes of use

This section examines how flexible funding was used in practice within the DDCI, drawing on recurring patterns observed across city engagements. The analysis focuses on how flexible funding was deployed alongside project-based funding to manage timing, scope, and uncertainty over the course of long-horizon urban work. This complementary way of deploying flexible funding—rather than as a stand-alone resource—closely reflects how the team used financial flexibility in practice. Across cases, we identified four distinct but overlapping modes of use:

1. Seed funding to test fit and enable strategic thematic entry
2. Add-on funding to layer new agendas onto existing work
3. Policy life-cycle funding to support continuity from planning through implementation
4. Gap funding to sustain presence through periods of uncertainty

These modes describe how flexible funding was mobilized at different moments and for different purposes. They do not represent stages in a linear process, nor do they imply that flexible funding resolved the structural constraints of project-based funding. The four modes were not specified in advance but were derived inductively from the data. During iterative coding of interviews and internal documents, references to the use of flexible funding clustered around recurring decision points tied to timing, scope, and stage of engagement. Through cross-case comparison and triangulation across sources, these clusters stabilized into four distinct but overlapping modes of use. Together, they provided the empirical basis for the analysis of cross-cutting tensions and trade-offs developed in section “Potential tensions in using flexible funding alongside project funding.”

### Seed funding: testing fit and enabling strategic entry

Flexible funding was used to support early-stage exploratory work in contexts where formal project commitments or major donor resources were not yet in place. During early DDCI cycles (2020–22), offices were allocated a small development fund, representing approximately 27 percent of total DDCI resources, to explore new cities and thematic areas and to shape agendas prior to securing project-based funding. One team member described this as having “built opportunity-seeking into the process” (INT-01), allowing teams to respond to emerging openings without waiting for fully developed and funded projects.

In Campinas, Brazil, an initial small-scale investment supported early work on nature-based solutions, which at the time was a relatively new area of engagement for the team and ran in parallel with donor-funded urban mobility work. This early engagement coincided with a new mayor’s interest in a green city agenda and enabled the team to establish technical collaboration and relationships that later supported broader efforts, including a city-wide climate action plan, a multisector adaptation program backed by a loan from Brazil’s federal development bank (BNDES), and the integration of nature-based approaches into complete street designs (INT-10; INT-02). In several cases, seed investments also produced analytical outputs that extended beyond the immediate city relationship. In Kigali, Rwanda, responding to a technical request to develop a data platform on informal settlements helped position WRI as a trusted planning partner, subsequently informing national-level policy work, as seen in Box 1 (RET-02 [see Appendix A, Table A-3]; Republic of Rwanda 2024, 2025).

Across cases, seed funding supported exploration, relationship-building, and co-design at moments when scope, partners, and pathways were still uncertain. It provided an agile base from which teams could develop more formal proposals and pursue subsequent project funding. At the same time, it is important to note that in practice early investments did not consistently lead to deeper or sustained engagement. In some instances, exploratory work remained stand-alone when political attention shifted, institutional priorities changed, or donor pipelines did not materialize.

## Add-on funding: layering new agendas onto existing work

While seed funding created entry points into new cities or agendas, add-on funding enabled teams to expand the scope of existing engagements by integrating adjacent themes that lacked dedicated donor backing. In these cases, teams were already operating under active project grants with defined scopes, and flexible funding was used to respond to emerging priorities or integrate across themes without reopening grant agreements or securing new project funding.

In China, DDCI resources made it possible to integrate climate resilience into long-running urban development and urban mobility efforts. As one team member explained, “because of the Deep Dive [Cities Initiative], we can do some climate resilience work in China, which is not funded otherwise [by] international donors” (INT-06). With this support, the team conducted a neighborhood risk perception survey in Beijing’s Dongsi area; piloted a climate resilience planning initiative in a community; and facilitated capacity-building activities with planners and nongovernmental organizations, which received a municipal planning award in 2023 (RET-04). In Shenzhen, add-on funding extended an existing municipal Near-Zero Carbon Communities program through additional training and risk assessments, and later provided co-funding for research on voluntary carbon markets, zero-emission freight logistics, and a data platform (INT-05). In Guadalajara and Monterrey, Mexico, Deep Dive Cities Initiative funding facilitated integrated research across previously separate urban development, air quality, and mobility projects, allowing the team to layer previously siloed agendas together to assess the potential impacts of new transit lines (RET-07). A further example from Delhi, India, can be found in Box 3.

Across cases, add-on funding enabled teams to deepen engagement and translate emerging agendas into ongoing city processes, particularly in constrained operating environments. At the same time, layering new agendas introduced practical limits. Add-on use occasionally stretched teams thin or blurred boundaries among projects, requiring teams to balance integration against focus and capacity within ongoing engagements. In the next section, we return to both these themes as potential tensions to be carefully considered.

## Policy life-cycle funding: seeing plans through to delivery

Flexible, multiyear funding was also used to sustain engagement beyond policy adoption and into the early phases of implementation. In Mumbai, India, this form of policy life-cycle funding enabled the team to remain engaged with the Brihanmumbai Municipal Corporation (BMC) following the formal launch of the city’s Climate Action Plan. Flexible resources supported activities such as the development of a project-prioritization framework for adaptation, officer training, and coordination around budgeting processes. These efforts coincided with the introduction of a climate budget (also supported by WRI India) and the establishment of a Department of Environment and Climate Change within the municipal administration, creating institutional arrangements intended to support continued implementation of the climate plan.

In Campinas, continued engagement after the adoption of the city’s Local Climate Action Plan and Road Safety Plan enabled follow-up work on nature-based solutions in road safety design, adaptation projects for linear parks, and climate investment strategies aligned with newly adopted plans, as seen in Box 2 (RET-03). The process and methodology developed through continued engagement has since been taken up by other Brazilian and Latin American cities, illustrating how flexible funds generated transferable knowledge beyond the original city context. In both cases, teams described the importance of remaining involved after formal policy milestones, when implementation needs, institutional responsibilities, and political attention were still in flux.

Staff emphasized that sustained engagement often required adjusting the focus of work as implementation challenges emerged. As one team member noted, “it might mean that you have to work in a new area of work because that’s the current ongoing conversation in the city. OK, now in that process you might build a new skill because that’s going to help you with it” (INT-04). Flexible funding supported early operational steps such as guidelines, training, coordination, and budget alignment, and helped maintain relationships with counterparts during initial delivery phases. At the same time, continued engagement did not guarantee long-term institutionalization. In some contexts, maintaining momentum after plan adoption proved difficult once initial champions moved on, underscoring the limits of flexible support in the absence of durable institutional incentives.

## Gap funding: sustaining presence through uncertain phases

Flexible funding was used to sustain engagement during periods when no active project funding was in place, including gaps between project phases, delays in donor disbursements, and shifts in city leadership or priorities. Teams described using flexible resources to maintain relationships, preserve a basic staff presence, and continue preparatory or coordination activities during these uncertain phases. In India, DDCI resources “allowed us to ... continue to be in cities through various kind of shifts and turns in government, a lot of slow periods,” covering activities such as training, coordination, and preparatory work so teams could “continue to kind of exist in that city” (WRI India staff in RET-09). In Brazil, where progress similarly depended on municipal planning cycles and external approvals, discretionary funds helped keep engagement active despite delays: “our work depends on others ... this type of fund gives us ... long-term engagement and even [lets us] postpone things or drop and then try to do it again or do differently” (INT-02).

This use of flexible funding operated alongside, rather than in place of, project-based grants, which continued to fund the vast majority of WRI Ross Center’s work. Project funding typically supported defined technical outputs and time-bound scopes of work, but did not cover the informal coordination, sustained dialogue, or waiting periods that often characterized these interim phases. Teams expressed that flexible funding allowed them to maintain continuity when formal project activities could not proceed. At the same time, the ability to do so did not guarantee that the external conditions, such as political support or future funding opportunities, would shift.

Across the DDCI portfolio, these recurrent four modes illustrate how flexible funding was used at different moments and for different purposes alongside project-based funding. Rather than operating as a single instrument, flexible funding functioned as an adaptive tool shaped by timing, judgment, and organizational context. Taken together, these modes reveal recurring patterns in how DDCI teams navigated uncertainty, sequencing, and scope within long-horizon urban work. The cross-cutting tensions and trade-offs that emerged across these uses are examined in the following section.

## Potential tensions in using flexible funding alongside project funding

The four modes of flexible funding use described in the preceding section illustrate how the presence of flexible funding for one transformation intermediary reshaped its ability to navigate the challenges of long-horizon urban work. Flexible funding can open opportunities for teams to experiment, respond to opportunity, build staying power, and develop and deepen relationships, and allow teams the time and space for research and knowledge production. However, these cases also reveal a set of potential tensions that can emerge inside organizations as flexible funding is deployed alongside program funds. Importantly, these tensions are a by-product of the additional options and discretion that flexible funding makes available, rather than a sign that flexibility is undesirable or ineffective. ## Continuity (defining engagement in disrupted periods)

Across the cases examined, flexible funding most visibly reshaped how intermediaries navigated continuity over time. Maintaining a presence through political turnover, administrative delay, and shifting priorities was widely treated as a core competency of intermediary work. Flexible funding supported this continuity by enabling engagement during periods when project-based funding was unavailable or ill-suited, including gaps between project phases, slowdowns in implementation, and moments of institutional uncertainty.

At the same time, sustained presence introduced a persistent trade-off. While continuity helped preserve relationships, trust, and readiness for future opportunities, it also reduced the immediacy of strategic pauses and withdrawal. Across several cases, flexible resources enabled light-touch engagement to continue with key stakeholders when political attention had waned or pathways forward were unclear. Flexible funding did not create these conditions, but it altered how long DDCI teams could sustain presence by allowing work to be postponed, re-sequenced, or paused. While teams did not specifically encounter a situation where maintaining presence through flexible resources postponed difficult exit or transition decisions, it is conceivable that such a situation could arise in practice.

This tension was particularly evident in gap funding and policy life-cycle funding. In both modes, flexible resources extended engagement beyond the formal boundaries of projects or policy milestones, often with the intention of anchoring work more deeply in administrative practice or documenting lessons learned through a knowledge product or internal learning memo. Where follow-on opportunities emerged, this continuity positioned teams to contribute at critical moments. While in the case of the DDCI this usually paid off, it is conceivable that other intermediary organizations would encounter a situation where continued engagement defers difficult judgments about whether to disengage, pivot, or conclude work altogether.

Taken together, the evidence suggests that in discussions about program strategy, flexible funding adds additional weight to decisions about the timing and intensity of continuity. By reducing the immediate pressure to disengage when project funding lapses or political conditions change, flexibility extends the temporal horizon over which judgment must be exercised. The challenge for intermediaries is therefore not whether continuity matters, but how to determine when, and at what level, continued engagement remains strategically justified under persistent uncertainty.

## Strategic coherence (aligning choices over time)

If continuity raises questions about how intermediaries may remain engaged under conditions of uncertainty, flexibility raises a parallel question about how such engagement is coordinated and held together over time. This tension is primarily forward-looking: It concerns how to navigate the need for dispersed, locally responsive choices while maintaining alignment with shared priorities and longer-term organizational direction. For the DDCI, flexible funding was paired with specific internal guidelines: alignment with WRI’s internal strategy, city selection recommendations, and light narrative and financial reporting. While flexible funding distinguishes the DDCI from other tightly structured projects, these guidelines also differentiate the DDCI from flexible funding deployed for general operating costs or with less oversight.

Across the cases examined, flexible funding expanded the scope for local judgment by allowing teams to adjust timing, sequencing, and focus in response to shifting political and institutional conditions. This discretion was central to the adaptive uses of funding described in section “Flexible funding in practice.” At the same time, increased discretion placed greater demands on strategic coherence. Rather than eliminating the need for coordination, flexibility shifted it inward, requiring WRI Ross Center to articulate priorities, align interpretations of long-term goals, and clarify how locally responsive choices would fit within a shared organizational direction.

Across cycles of the DDCI, teams exercised autonomy, within a shared framework of institutional guidance, over city selection, thematic emphasis, and the sequencing of activities in response to local conditions. Most importantly, teams were able to change course and activities as political realities shifted. While this responsiveness was widely valued, it also surfaced divergent interpretations of the boundaries of a deep dive and limitations on the use of flexible resources in relation to WRI Ross Center's broader mission. In the case of the DDCI, coherence came most often through the illumination and execution of WRI office and city priorities, to be negotiated and shaped through their relationship to organizational priorities. This tension between flexibility and strategic coherence recurred across contexts and cycles, shaping how DDCI teams balanced responsiveness to local conditions with the need for shared direction over time.

## Accountability (tracing choices after the fact)

Flexible funding reshaped not only what intermediaries could do, but also how their choices were expected to be accounted for. By expanding discretion over timing, sequencing, and scope, flexible resources loosened the *ex ante* controls typical of project-based donor funding. Yet the need for accountability did not recede. Instead, it took new forms, as decisions made under uncertainty had to be rendered intelligible to internal leadership, peers, and funders after the fact. The resulting tension was not between flexibility and control per se, but between discretion in practice and the institutional demands for justification, traceability, and measured impact.

This tension became visible in how teams navigated adaptive decision-making alongside ongoing expectations for documentation, explanation, and reporting. Under project-based funding, accountability was largely embedded in compliance

with predefined outputs and timelines. Under flexible funding, many choices were contingent and iterative, shaped by political shifts, emerging opportunities, or stalled processes. As teams used flexible resources to extend engagement, layer agendas, or re-sequence work, they were often required to explain why these adjustments were appropriate, even when outcomes were uncertain or not immediately visible. Flexible funding thus reduced the rigidity of formal constraints while increasing the interpretive burden placed on intermediaries to document changes and retrospectively demonstrate that discretionary choices remained aligned with organizational purpose.

The tension between discretion and accountability was particularly evident in the relationship between reporting and learning. Early cycles of the initiative relied on progress reporting as the primary accountability mechanism, emphasizing adherence to established activities and outputs. This often meant that accountability defaulted to familiar compliance routines, even as the substance of the work became more contingent and iterative. Several teams experienced this as a mismatch between the intent of flexibility and the forms through which it was monitored. As the initiative matured, the DDCI's cycle-based structure allowed for adjustments to management practices based on ongoing conversations across the teams. Over time, reporting shifted more toward a focus on documenting process: what decisions were made, and why, as well as learning and exchange across geographies. This paper itself is one piece of a broader effort to document processes and learning in cases of flexibility.

Accountability pressures were not experienced only at the level of country teams. From the perspective of the initiative's core team and senior leadership, flexible funding also introduced heightened responsibility to ensure that dispersed and adaptive activity remained intelligible, defensible, and aligned with organizational commitments. In the absence of tightly specified outputs, accountability served as a stabilizing function, helping to forge shared reference points across cities, cycles, and thematic areas. This required articulating priorities, tracking how discretionary choices related to longer-term goals, and providing assurance to internal and external stakeholders that flexibility was being exercised purposefully and judiciously.

Taken together, the evidence suggests that flexible funding reconfigures accountability. Where project-based funding emphasizes compliance with predefined plans, flexible funding emphasizes explanation, justification, and shared interpretation of evolving choices. This shift increases the interpretive work required of intermediaries, which must continuously render discretionary decisions visible and meaningful to multiple audiences while operating under uncertain conditions. ## A note on responsiveness and organizational capacity

Across the cases examined, increased responsiveness also carried implications for organizational capacity. As flexible funding enabled teams to respond to shifting political priorities, integrate adjacent agendas, and sustain engagement across extended time horizons, the scope and diversity of work expanded beyond what formal project boundaries alone would suggest. This expansion required teams to absorb new thematic areas, build skills iteratively, and manage a growing web of relationships, often while sustaining visibility and impact and continuing to deliver against existing project commitments.

These dynamics were not framed by teams as failures of planning or execution, but as a structural feature of intermediary practice under conditions of flexibility. Responsiveness relied on organizational bandwidth that was finite, unevenly distributed, and shaped by existing obligations. In this context, flexible funding sometimes shifted pressure inward, concentrating demands on teams’ time, coordination, and absorptive capacities. This did not negate the value of flexible funding; rather, it clarified its limits. The ability to adapt and respond is constrained not only by funding and political conditions, but by the organization’s capacity to sustain expanded scope over time. Recognizing this boundary condition helps situate the preceding tensions within a realistic account of intermediary practice, where flexibility reshapes possibilities but does not remove the need for prioritization and judgment.

## Navigating trade-offs: practice implications

The analysis above shows that flexible funding neither removes the structural constraints of project-based funding, nor resolves the uncertainty that characterizes long-horizon urban work. Instead, it alters how intermediary organizations make decisions within those constraints—expanding discretion while increasing the importance of judgment and deliberation on continuity, strategic coherence, accountability, and staff capacity. Flexible funding is therefore best understood not as a solution in itself but as a different kind of resource that intermediaries deploy alongside project funding, with distinct implications for how work reaches success and is planned, sequenced, adapted, and sustained over time.

This section translates those findings into practice-relevant considerations for peer intermediaries. It frames the implications of flexible funding as a set of questions that arise when such resources are available. These questions follow the progression of decision-making that intermediaries face in practice: from defining the scope and purpose of flexibility, to managing its use over time, to deciding how choices are explained to partners and funders. Together, they highlight what decisions intermediaries need to actively manage when deploying flexible funding in real-world urban settings.

## What flexible funding is available and what is it intended to support?

Across the cases examined, flexible funding represented a small share of overall program resources. Its influence stemmed not from scale, but from the discretion it afforded, particularly in contexts where project-based funding was tightly scoped, time bound, or misaligned with political and institutional rhythms. For intermediaries, therefore, an early decision concerns not simply whether flexible funding exists, but how it is situated in relation to the rest of the organization’s funding mix.

In practice, this involves clarifying both the bounds and the purpose of flexible funding. DDCI teams worked with varying proportions of flexible resources and navigated different expectations about what those resources were meant to support. In some instances, flexible funding was treated primarily as a buffer to sustain engagement during gaps between projects. In others, it was used to explore new cities or thematic areas, extend the scope of ongoing work, or remain engaged through early phases of implementation. In still more, it was used to conduct research and expand the evidence base for decision-making. Making these uses explicit, whether internally within teams or across organizational leadership, can shape how flexible funds are drawn upon and how competing demands can be prioritized.

Even in situations where some institutional guidance exists, teams may risk conflating uses of flexible funding or stretching limited resources across too many purposes at once. By contrast, when teams articulate what flexible funding is for, and what it is not expected to cover, they will likely be better positioned to deploy it strategically alongside project-based grants. This does not eliminate difficult choices, but it does provide a clearer framework for deciding when and how flexible resources should be mobilized as work evolves.

## What kinds of decisions is flexible funding being used to enable?

Once the scope and intended role of flexible funding are clarified, a second set of questions concerns how that funding is used in practice. Across the DDCI, flexible funding was mobilized to support different kinds of decisions at different moments, including sustaining engagement during gaps between projects, exploring new cities or agendas, extending the scope of ongoing work, and remaining involved through early phases of implementation. These uses did not follow a fixed sequence, nor were they mutually exclusive. Instead, they reflected how the use of flexible funding was shaped by both institutional guidance and evolving political, institutional, and organizational conditions over time.

The cases suggest that these different uses placed distinct demands on teams and on the organization. Using flexible funding to bridge gaps requires maintaining a basic presence and relationships during periods of delay or uncertainty. Seed uses emphasize exploration, relationship-building, and agenda-shaping before formal commitments are in place. Add-on uses involve layering new priorities onto existing engagements without reopening project agreements, while life-cycle uses focus on sustaining involvement after formal policy milestones have been reached. Recognizing the distinct ways flexible funds are used also clarifies the activities these uses require, making clearer how much time, attention, and risk teams are willing to absorb at a given moment.

For intermediaries, this raises the question of whether flexible funding is being deployed in ways that are consistent with its stated purpose and with organizational priorities. Having institutional guidance—a “North Star”—and making distinctions among uses of flexible funding can help teams reflect on whether resources are being drawn down reactively or strategically, and whether particular uses are crowding out others. While flexible funding was valued by DDCI teams precisely because it allowed adaptation, the analysis suggests that a shared understanding of the overall purpose of the initiative and the kinds of decisions it is meant to enable will support more intentional use over time, particularly when multiple demands compete for limited discretionary resources.

## How are decisions about the use of flexible funding made and revisited over time?

As flexible funding increases discretion over timing, scope, and sequencing, it also shifts where responsibility for decision-making sits. Across the DDCI experience, flexible funding reduced the need for advance specification tied to predefined deliverables, which allowed for a stronger focus on process, learning, and exchange. It placed greater weight on internal judgment, shared understanding, and the ability to explain decisions retrospectively as work evolved.

Teams described making frequent adjustments in response to changing political conditions, partner readiness, or emerging opportunities. Flexible funding made such adjustments possible, but an unintended consequence was also that decisions were less often anchored in formal project documents or externally approved scopes of work. In this context, how discretionary choices were discussed internally, recorded, and revisited over time became an important part of practice. Where teams had a shared sense of intent and could articulate why priorities shifted, flexible funding supported adaptation without undermining coherence. Where that shared understanding was weaker, discretionary use was more difficult to account for, particularly as staff changed or activities accumulated over multiple years.

For intermediaries, this points to the importance of deciding how discretionary decisions are surfaced and reflected upon, even when formal reporting requirements are light. The cases suggest that flexibility does not remove the need for accountability, but relocates it. Accountability becomes less about compliance with predefined outputs and more about the ability to explain how and why resources were deployed in response to unfolding conditions, and to facilitate ongoing learning institutionally about when adaptation is needed and how it occurs. Attending to this shift can help organizations ensure that flexibility supports learning and strategic intent over time, rather than becoming opaque or difficult to justify as circumstances change.

## How does flexible funding interact with organizational capacity over time?

Flexible funding increased the ability of teams to respond to emerging opportunities, shifts in political attention, and requests from city partners. Across the DDCI, this responsiveness was widely valued, particularly in contexts where rigid project scopes limited timely engagement. At the same time, the analysis shows that responsiveness was not cost free. Each adjustment in direction, addition of scope, or extension of engagement drew on finite organizational capacity, including staff time, managerial attention, and technical bandwidth.

Making space to assess cumulative workload and strategic focus over time can help ensure that flexibility supports sustained engagement rather than leading to a gradual dilution of effort.

## When does continued engagement supported by flexible funding still serve the mission?

Across the DDCI, flexible funding made it possible for intermediaries to remain engaged through periods of political change, institutional delay, or uncertainty. This staying power was often central to maintaining relationships and positioning teams to contribute when conditions shifted. At the same time, the analysis shows that continued engagement was not automatically strategic. In some cases, flexible funding extended involvement in contexts where anticipated momentum shifts or political support did not emerge as intended. These situations are difficult to predict *ex ante*, and therefore they—as any engagement—carry a degree of operational risk.

This raises questions about how intermediaries assess the value of continued presence over time. Flexible funding reduced the pressure to exit when project funding ended, requiring teams to periodically revisit whether ongoing engagement was still aligned with mission priorities and long-term political context. In practice, this involved distinguishing between periods where a shift in contextual conditions was unlikely and periods where continued presence preserved trust and readiness.

For intermediaries, therefore, the question is not necessarily whether to remain engaged whenever flexibility allows, but how to recognize when continued involvement supports longer-term objectives and when it may defer difficult decisions about pause, transition, or withdrawal—which would very likely affect city relationships and staffing. ## Conclusion

This paper examines how flexible funding is used in practice by an intermediary working under conditions of long-horizon change and persistent uncertainty. Drawing on the Deep Dive Cities Initiative at WRI Ross Center, the analysis traces how flexible resources were deployed in combination with project-based funding to manage timing, scope, and continuity across city engagements. Rather than treating flexible funding as a discrete intervention or a superior alternative to project funding, the paper shows how it functioned as a different kind of resource within a broader funding mix, reshaping how intermediaries navigated uncertainty without eliminating it.

The contribution of the paper is analytical rather than evaluative. It does not assess the effectiveness or impact of flexible funding. The findings reflect one organization's experience within a specific institutional and funding context, shaped by the limits of a single-case, insider-informed study. As the DDCI expanded over six years from 5 to 21 cities in WRI Ross Center's main operating geographies and increased the depth and breadth of WRI's work globally, it also produced practice lessons and internal documentation about how resources were used, how teams navigated political shifts, and how internal practices of reporting and learning shifted over time. While the cases reveal recurring patterns in how flexible funding is used, they also underscore that such use depends on organizational judgment, context, and capacity,

and that flexibility introduces both its own constraints and risks as well as new possibilities for overcoming the limitations of project-based funding. While we did not systematically examine regional differences in funding priorities or the relative importance of the four funding types across contexts, this could constitute a promising avenue for future research to better understand how flexible funding is shaped by contextual variables.

This paper was written primarily for peer intermediary organizations engaged in mission-driven urban work that operate within mixed-funding environments and face similar pressures of uncertainty, political change, and long time horizons. By making visible how flexible funding reshapes practice—and where it does not—the analysis aims to support more deliberate reflection on how such resources are deployed in real-world settings and the importance of this reflection in enabling strategic use of funds. In doing so, it contributes to a more grounded understanding of flexible funding not as a blanket solution to the challenges of intermediary work, but as a condition of strategic practice.
