Highlights
 ▪ Kenya’s transport sector is the second-greatest contributor to the 
country’s greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, accounting for 13 percent 
of total emissions. Over 75 percent of Kenyans can access clean energy, 
so switching to electric vehicles (EVs) could help Kenya curb air pollu-
tion and reach its decarbonization goals. But far fewer than 1 percent of 
vehicles sold in Kenya annually are electric.  
 ▪ This working paper analyzes the obstacles that are hampering the transi-
tion to cleaner vehicles, identifies opportunities to tackle these problems, 
and compares Kenya’s efforts to promote electrification with what other 
countries are doing. 
 ▪ It finds that, to increase EV uptake, the Kenyan government will need 
to bolster policy and regulatory interventions; improve access to EV 
technology, infrastructure, and financing; enhance the economic and 
commercial environment; raise awareness; and build technical capacity.
 ▪ Interventions should be data driven and employ targeted modelling 
to weigh budgetary costs, estimate business value, and design and 
conduct pilot programs.
 ▪ Africa is now regarded as the continent where sales of vehicles will 
surge the most, so tipping Kenya’s market toward EVs could have an 
impact beyond its borders—on other countries and on the climate.
Executive summary
Context
Vehicle electrification has been identified as among the most impact-
ful ways to cut GHG emissions, with a potential to bring down Kenya’s 
transport sector emissions by 4.5 percent below emissions under a 
business-as-usual scenario by 2050, with the right mix of conservative 
electrification policies (Benedikt and Felix 2018). 
World Resources Institute’s 2023 State of Climate Action report points to 
global transport electrification as the only measure on course to achieve 
sectoral targets under the Paris climate agreement (Boehm et al. 2023). 
Governments around the world, such as those in the United States, China,

and India, are developing policies on EV manufacturing and 
deployment to spur growth in EV adoption (IEA 2023). But 
governments in sub–Saharan African countries have yet to 
intervene comprehensively to accelerate electrification. Africa’s 
contribution to the global EV transition has been meager. This 
reflects the relatively low share of the population that drives 
motor vehicles (UNEP 2020) as well the absence of an elabo-
rate vehicle manufacturing industry (Agarwal et al. 2022). 
Kenya has recently moved aggressively to support elec-
trification. For example, it has lowered the value-added tax 
(VAT) to zero for lithium-ion batteries, electric bicycles, 
motorcycles, and buses, and adopted a special subsidized 
electricity tariff for EV charging. However, like even the most 
advanced economies, Kenya can maximize the impact of 
efforts to support EV supply chains by continuously evaluat-
ing them. While research has been conducted on parts of 
the supply chain, Kenya’s entire EV supply chain has not 
previously been evaluated. This paper fills this research gap 
by exploring the country’s EV supply chain to uncover both 
barriers to and opportunities for road transport electrification 
with a systems-wide support infrastructure. 
About this working paper
This paper analyzes Kenya’s EV ecosystem, markets, poli-
cies, and regulations. It compares what Kenya is doing with 
steps other governments have taken to identify gaps and 
opportunities to accelerate progress in vehicle electrification. It 
also synthesizes insights from EV companies, financiers, gov-
ernment stakeholders, and users, and assesses the low-hanging 
fruit in EV advancement. We acknowledge that rail, marine, 
and aviation electrification have sets of barriers and opportu-
nities that may be different from those of road transport and 
these are not explored in this paper. Neither do we delve into 
the details about raw material sourcing for EV batteries.  
Key findings 
EVs comprise less than 1 percent of Kenya’s vehicle fleet 
and significantly less than 1 percent of new annual vehicle 
registrations. However, more than 75 percent of the Kenyan 
population has access to electricity, which is generated from 
over 90 percent renewable sources, so Kenya could drastically 
reduce emissions if more Kenyans drove EVs.  
Kenya is counting on the electrification of transport to help 
reach its mitigation goal of reducing national GHG emis-
sions by 32 percent below a business-as-usual scenario by 
2030. Apart from air quality and climate mitigation benefits, 
EV adoption offers economic advantages as the total cost of 
ownership of EVs is 15–50 percent lower than that of internal 
combustion engine (ICE) equivalents (depending on the type 
of vehicle and operating period) (IEA 2022a; Carello 2024). 
Without the right mix of policies for electrification, the popu-
lation is likely to continue to choose polluting ICE vehicles 
(ICEVs), which would aggravate air quality deterioration and 
continue to contribute to climate change.
What are the major obstacles  
to EV adoption?
While the Kenyan government has been proactive in 
promoting electrification, there are some policy and regula-
tory barriers. These include the absence of a comprehensive 
electric mobility policy, inadequate incentivization of produc-
tion, and suboptimal classification of vehicles and utilization 
of standards to ensure the quality and safety of registered and 
operating EVs. 
While EVs are undergoing research and development 
(R&D), EV technology cannot currently offer the same 
performance and durability as ICE vehicles at an equivalent 
upfront cost, posing a significant obstacle to electrifica-
tion. Batteries that would pack enough energy to cover a 
similar distance as ICEVs on a tank of fuel are still expensive. 
Uncertainty surrounding end-of-life management of EVs is 
another hurdle. In addition, infrastructure that would increase 
the utility of EVs is growing, but still largely concentrated 
in the capital, Nairobi, with a very small footprint beyond 
the city. This spotty availability of adequate and acces-
sible charging and battery-as-a-service (battery swapping) 
infrastructure causes range anxiety concerns, which reduce 
the uptake of EVs.
Another key obstacle keeping more people from purchasing 
EVs is their high upfront cost. This stems from, among other 
things, the cost of obtaining loans to manufacture and buy 
them. Most electric-mobility (e-mobility) companies are start-
ups, and typically lack extensive banking history or collateral. 
For this reason, they are unable to access affordable commer-
cial lending from banking or financing institutions and rely 
on equity investors, venture funds, and grants. Most end users 
also face several limitations to accessing credit to purchase an 
EV, including increased evaluation risk; unaffordable inter-
est rates for available loans (usually more than 50 percent 
per annum; pa), largely provided by specialized lenders; and 
stringent criteria to access loans from mainstream commercial 
banks, which could offer better interest rates (between 18 and 
24 percent pa).
In addition to expensive credit, other economic- and 
commerce-related obstacles take the form of currency 
fluctuations and devaluation, a lack of economies of scale in 
production because of the early market stage for EVs, as well 
as limitations in global shipping regulations for batteries. 
The net effect of these barriers is to increase the cost of doing 
business, which in turn raises the cost of the final products. 
There is also inadequate technical and institutional capacity 
(e.g., an insufficient number of skilled workers to repair and

With a motorization rate estimated to be about 30 vehicles 
per 1,000 people, Kenya has low vehicle ownership. But, 
like other African countries, its population is growing 
rapidly and there will be increasing demand for transporta-

that could affect its emissions trajectory and influence other 
African countries. 
How can Kenya address these obstacles? 
Kenya has developed some policies and standards such as 
the National Automotive Policy, a preferential electricity tariff 
targeted to EVs, 35 safety standards, and Extended Producer 
Responsibility Regulations. However, these regulatory inter-
ventions were developed without a comprehensive electric 
mobility policy in place, which would provide the coordina-
tion mechanism Kenya needs to accelerate the EV transition. 
Research in advanced markets has been focused on grow-
ing local capacity for EV production and maintenance. This 
R&D has contributed to progressive growth in countries’ 
competitive advantages in manufacturing. Countries like India 
and China have implemented comprehensive policies that 
have grown their production and uptake of EVs using various 
instruments like tax incentives and subsidies. Kenya has the 
opportunity to promote local component manufacturing and 
use production incentives to meet quotas for local content. 
Current policies and regulations also need to be enforced and 
monitored to ensure maximum impact. 
Companies in Kenya are already collaborating with EV 
producers for technology transfer, but the government has 
not expressly focused on building local technical capacity. 
Targeted R&D initiatives could help grow Kenya’s production 
and assembly based on the country’s competitive advantage in 
manufacturing. There is also an opportunity for a government-
led initiative to provide strategic technical capacity building by 
incorporating EV education in the national curriculum.
For charging infrastructure, Kenya’s government has 
lowered the electricity purchase tariff. But the private sector 
is leading the deployment of charging infrastructure. More 
government involvement in rolling out charging infrastructure 
would help increase EV acceptance. 
Improving access to affordable financing will be key to 
increasing EV uptake on both the supply and demand sides. 
Advanced markets have used incentive grants and crediting 
systems whereby companies earn credits for producing and 
selling EVs. While the government may not provide direct 
grants, there is an opportunity to facilitate the develop-
ment of green financing and provide de-risking mechanisms 
for investors in manufacturing and assembly and charging 
infrastructure to access affordable financing. There are signifi-
cant opportunities to lower risks for EV purchasers through 
guarantees, innovative financing products, and leveraging on 
carbon financing.  
The Kenyan government also has an opportunity to provide 
an attractive economic environment that overcomes hurdles 
and helps increase the uptake of EVs. It could set EV sales 
requirements; drive EV demand through captured fleets; and 
do more to promote the utility of special economic zones, 
which provide tax exemptions and preferential tariffs. 
Raising awareness of available EVs and charging solutions, 
electrification incentives, and processes and regulations 
concerning electric mobility will also be necessary to 
enable sector stakeholders to benefit from government 
interventions to promote e-mobility and spur demand for 
the technology. 
Companies will need to better position themselves by 
promoting vertical integration, adopting outsourcing strat-
egies, and tapping into the wider East African Community 
regional market. All stakeholders will need to take advantage 
of a range of opportunities in a number of areas: policy and 
regulations, technology, financing, economics, commerce, 
awareness, and capacity enhancement. Investors and govern-
ment leaders will need to deploy targeted modelling and make 
data-driven decisions concerning policies, regulations, and 
business value proposals.

Introduction
Africa has the world’s lowest motorization rate compared with 
other continents, at 42.7 vehicles per 1,000 people (SLOCAT 
2023). Kenya is estimated to have fewer than 30 vehicles per 
1,000 people (GIZ 2021). 
Africa accounts for less than 1 percent of global vehicle 
production and approximately 4 percent of vehicles currently 
in use around the world. But these figures are climbing. The 
African market depends primarily on imported used light 
duty vehicles (UNEP 2020) and on two- and three-wheelers 
for passenger transport. In 12 years, between 2010 and 2022, 
the number of two- and three-wheelers jumped more than 
fivefold, from 5 million to an estimated 27 million (Bishop 
and Courtright 2022). Globally, electric vehicles (EVs) are 
gaining popularity, with sales exceeding 10 million units 
in 2022 (approximately 14 percent of automotive sales). In 
Kenya, however, most are internal combustion engine (ICE) 
vehicles and less than 1 percent of the vehicle fleet is electric. 
Kenya’s transport sector is the second-greatest contributor to 
the country’s greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, accounting 
for 13 percent of total emissions. The sector is expected to 
produce over 20 million tonnes of carbon dioxide equivalent 
by 2030 assuming no intervention (GoK 2020d). Mitigating 
transportation emissions is a high priority in Kenya’s nation-
ally determined contribution, contributing to the national 
target of a 32 percent reduction in GHG emissions from the 
business-as-usual scenario, which would increase emissions 
by 46 percent in 2030 above 2015 emissions. In the entire 
Kenyan transport sector, which includes rail, marine, road, 
and air transport, vehicular electrification has been identified 
as among the most impactful mitigation measures, with a 
national mitigation potential of 4.5 percent by 2050 over base-
line transport emissions (Benedikt and Felix 2018). 
Over 75 percent of the Kenyan population has access to 
electricity, which comes from 90 percent clean energy sources 
(Kenya Power 2023). In addition, Africa, the continent with 
the world’s second-largest population, is expected to see a 
surge in vehicle sales (Agarwal et al. 2022). With a projected 
increase in demand for vehicles and without the right mix 
of policies for electrification, it is likely that the population 
will continue to choose polluting ICE engines, which would 
aggravate air quality deterioration and continue to contribute 
to climate change. For this reason, electrifying road transport 
is the low-hanging fruit for reducing greenhouse gases emis-
sions, improving energy independence, and protecting the 
public from air pollution. 
Apart from their air quality and climate mitigation benefits, 
EVs have economic benefits, with a total cost of ownership 
for EVs between 15 and 50 percent lower than that of ICE 
equivalents, depending on the type of vehicle and operating 
period (Conzade et al. 2022). 
The International Energy Agency’s (IEA’s) Global EV Outlook 
2023 suggests that governments must develop policies on 
EV manufacturing and deployment to boost EV adoption. 
Countries, like the United States, China, and India, deployed 
different but aggressive incentives targeted at EV supply chain 
elements in 2022–23 (IEA 2023). Africa is seen to make a 
meager contribution to this global EV transition because 
fewer motorized vehicles are driven and manufactured there 
than on other continents. However, whether the African 
population chooses ICEVs or EVs matters.
About this working paper
This paper examines Kenya’s options for increasing the uptake 
of EVs. It makes use of the IEA’s Global EV Outlook 2023, 
which documents other countries’ recent efforts to promote 
EV manufacturing in a bid to stimulate their EV supply 
chains (IEA 2023). Some examples of these initiatives include 
the European Green Deal, the US Inflation Reduction Act, 
and India’s Production Linked Incentives. 
How this paper differs from and builds  
on existing research 
Much of the existing research on speeding up electrification 
and EV supply chains has been centered on batteries and the 
minerals used to make them (IEA 2022b; Roland Berger 
2022; Campagnol et al. 2022; Horowitz et al. 2021; Barman 
et al. 2023). EVs, and especially their batteries, require many 
minerals in their production, including lithium, nickel, cobalt, 
and graphite (IEA 2021). Kenya does not have significant 
mineral reserves for battery production and therefore will 
continue to rely on battery importation in the medium to long 
term. This paper focuses on other barriers to and opportunities 
for the widespread adoption of EVs in Kenya and elsewhere in 
Africa from a supply chain perspective.  
Some of the obstacles Kenya is experiencing have been 
observed by researchers looking at the case of India (Mane et 
al. 2024). In their study of India’s EV supply chain, Mane et 
al. (2024) identified inadequate charging infrastructure as well 
as technological and financial barriers affecting the country’s 
EV supply chain. 
Studies in Africa have looked at barriers to and opportunities 
for the transition to road transport electrification (Manufac-
turing Africa 2021a; ZE-Mobility 2023; Nyoni et al. 2023;

Methodology
We applied both qualitative and quantitative research meth-
ods: desktop research for regulatory and policy analysis, a 
vehicle operator/user survey, informant interviews, and market 
and industry analysis. 
User survey
Our sample size for users included 531 responses (n=531) 
collected from vehicle operators (individual and corporate 
owners of two- and three-wheelers, cars, and buses) in Kenya, 
16 responses from financing institutions, and 16 responses 
from distributors of two- and three-wheelers. The number of 
total vehicles in use was estimated at 2.4 million in 2016 and 
projected to reach 5 million units by 2030 (Ogot et al. 2018). 
It is common knowledge that the coastal region has compara-
tively more three-wheelers than other regions. The central 
region has mixed modes while the western region has higher 
numbers of two-wheelers. We therefore carried out the user 
survey in these representative regions: Kilifi County in the 
coastal region, Nairobi metropolitan area in the central region, 
and Kisumu County in the western region (see Figure 1). We 
interviewed users based on random sampling by cluster and 
distributed over various vehicle modes. We designed the sur-
vey to engage respondents and collect gender-sensitive data. 
Key informant interviews 
We interviewed key informants including policy and 
regulation experts; EV industry experts; and officials from 
government institutions involved in the supply chain, such 
as individuals in the ministries of trade and industrialization, 
energy, transport, and environment. We sampled industry 
firms based on their maturity in the subsector. We conducted 
additional interviews with financial institutions from a sample 
of the 39 licensed commercial banks in Kenya (CBK 2024). 
Interviews revealed processes, challenges, and opportunities 
encountered by EV companies, and the context of imple-
menting proposals identified in this paper. We classified 
responses into policy and regulatory, technological, economic 
and commerce related, financing, and capacity and awareness 
categories. We collected time series data where appropriate.

Limitations of the research methodology
Sample size: We conducted the survey and interviews from 
a randomly chosen sample to represent the entire EV ecosys-
tem. The EV industry is in its early stages of development so 
the sample size is small, a potential limitation of the study.
Scope: We acknowledge the complexity of the battery sup-
ply chain with global geopolitical interactions influencing 
availability as well as the market leadership of other countries 
in battery manufacturing. Kenya lacks significant mineral 
reserves for battery production. The research does not delve 
into battery supply chains. 
Conclusions: This paper does not outline how to achieve 
our proposed recommendations in detail. Further studies 
would be needed to crystalize the recommendations outlined 
in this study.

Findings
Automotive landscape and policies  
in Kenya
The Kenyan automotive sector dates to the 1960s with auto-
motive production peaking at 13,000 vehicles in the 1980s. 
Market liberalization brought a rapid decline in assembly and 
a rapid increase in the importation of used vehicles. By 2018, 
local installed manufacturing and assembly capacity had fallen 
to less than 20 percent of the 1980s’ capacity (KIPPRA 2022). 
Used imports now account for more than 85 percent of annual 
light duty vehicle registrations. 
The number of two- and three-wheelers on the road increased 
from 16,000 in 2007 to over 186,000 in 2017, with average 
sales rising 37 percent annually since 2008, when the govern-
ment created a tax exemption for assemblers and sellers of 
two- and three-wheelers in a bid to spur job creation. Several 
two- and three-wheeler assemblers have been licensed to 
assemble knocked-down units. This refers to a manufacturing 
process whereby vehicles are partially or completely disas-
sembled at the country of origin and reassembled in another 
country. In 2017, however, assemblers were operating at 50 
percent of their production capacities (KIPPRA 2022) and 
had not maximized local production capacity.  
Licensed automotive component producers make components 
for two- and three-wheelers. Established coach builders for 
buses and trucks also develop coaches appropriate for the 
Kenyan market’s loading capacity and road conditions. 
Several government ministries (transport, trade, treasury, 
and energy) develop policies, implement regulations, over-
see automotive assembly and manufacturing, set standards, 
and enforce them.
In 2018, the active national vehicle fleet in Kenya was 
estimated at between 1.4 million and 1.6 million units (Ogot 
et al. 2018) and could likely be more than 2 million currently. 
Motorcycles dominate annual vehicle registrations, compris-
ing an average of 67 percent of the total over the 2017–2022 
period (see Figure 2). 
EVs in Kenya
Since 2018, Kenya has seen a significant increase in EVs 
on the road, with over 3,700 registered as of December 
2023. Eighty-six percent of these vehicles are two-wheeled 
motorcycles, excluding e-bicycles, while 4 percent are three-
wheelers, reflecting similar trends in ICE vehicle registration 
(see Figure 3).

The number of EVs registered on Kenyan roads makes up less 
than 1 percent of all registered vehicles. Although there are 
efforts by the government, industry, and development partners 
to increase EV awareness, overall available and ready demand 
for EVs is relatively small compared with those in devel-
oped countries. 
Market assessment: What vehicles 
do Kenyans drive? How do they 
finance them?
Motorcycles, also known as boda-bodas, account for 66 percent 
of annual vehicle registrations in Kenya, primarily used for 
commercial purposes and last-mile mobility (see Figure 4). 
They are owned by individuals and corporate fleet operators. 
Three-wheelers, also known as tuk-tuks, are used for commer-
cial cargo and public transport, especially in areas not served 
by the public transport system. They are owned by individuals. 
The market, as estimated by our survey, for two- and three-
wheeler riders is dominated (71 percent of respondents) by 
young people, ages 18–35 years (Figure 5), who either operate 
two- and three-wheelers as their primary source of income or 
as a supplementary means of earning. They own the two- and 
three-wheelers or are operating them on lease or as employed 
riders. Among surveyed riders, 85 percent had monthly house-
hold incomes of up to KES 50,000 (approximately US$330) 
with a majority (56.42 percent) earning less than KES 25,000. 
About 75 percent of respondents owned one unit (Figure 6). 
Our survey reflects a male-dominated market for two- and 
three-wheelers as only 5 percent of respondents were female.
Most ICE two- and three-wheelers (about 64 percent of 
two-wheelers and 50 percent of three-wheelers) were acquired 
through dealerships that have business arrangements with 
original equipment manufacturers to supply two- and three-
wheelers and provide after-sale service. Distributors have 
established financing arrangements with microfinance institu-
tions. While distributors did not divulge what shares of their 
fleets are sold through microfinance credit, 64.4 percent of 
sampled two-wheeler riders indicated acquiring their vehicles 
through these institutions (see Figure 8a). Our survey also 
suggests there is a significant local secondhand ICE two-and-
three-wheeler market, especially in satellite towns in Kenya. 
This market accounts for 31 percent of two-wheelers and 46 
percent of three-wheelers.

establish mainstream distributors as in the ICE segment. They 
partner with microfinancing institutions and ride-hailing 
companies to market their products. They also use their bat-
tery swapping stations as sales points for their products, which 
are sold with a financing option from the microlender. The 
companies also act as agents for lending institutions by con-

Our study shows that three-wheelers operate on predefined 
routes and have fixed fares while two-wheelers operate across 
various terrains and routes and do not have designated stops. 
Despite two- and three-wheelers having different operating 
models, they are acquired in similar ways with cash purchases 
being the most common (see Figure 7). Most cash purchases 
for both modes (62 percent) are secondhand purchases

Where do the vehicles Kenyans 
drive come from?
Vehicles in Kenya are either sourced as fully built units 
(FBUs), which are predominantly passenger vehicles, or semi-
knocked-down (SKD) or completely knocked-down (CKD) 
two- and three-wheelers and buses, which are partially or fully 
disassembled at the point of origin to be reassembled in the 
country. Where assembly infrastructure exists, using CKD or 
SKD vehicles results in lower taxation and therefore makes 
a better business case for the industry players. Assembling 
two- and three-wheelers does not require heavy investment in 
machinery and can be done by hand. 
Pre-export verification of conformity to standards is required 
for FBU imports. ICEVs must adhere to vehicle inspec-
tion code KS1515 (KEBS 2019), which focuses on ensuring 
that safety and emission standards are met. Aspects spe-
cific to e-mobility, e.g., battery state of health (SoH), are 
not addressed. 
Vehicles meeting importing country requirements get issued 
a conformity certificate and are shipped to Kenya. Upon 
arrival, random inspections are conducted but due to a lack of 
e-mobility testing facilities at ports of entry, the Kenya Bureau 
of Standards conducts only visual inspections. Thereafter, 
imports go through custom clearance, inspection, and pay-
ment of import duties and insurance before entering the local 
distribution system.
Electric two-wheelers (bicycles and motorcycles) and three-
wheelers are largely assembled locally. Most e-mobility 
companies in Kenya are dealing with two- and three-wheeler 
products. Between 2018 and 2021, companies imported 
knocked-down units with no local content. However, the 
companies have increased their local content input by incor-
porating parts common to both ICEVs and EVs. They source 
some of these components locally (e.g., seat, harness, footrest) 
and supplement unavailable parts by sourcing from both India 
(chassis) and China (batteries). 
The only two e-bus companies in Kenya at the time of the 
study confirmed they largely source components from China. 
There are ongoing efforts to adopt local manufacturing and 
assembly with both companies partnering with Kenya Vehicle 
Manufacturers and Associated Vehicle Assemblers to meet 
e-bus assembly requirements. E-buses have gone through cus-
tomization to meet the unique needs of the Kenyan market, 
including modifications for use as cargo or passenger carriers 
and improved sturdiness to drive on unpaved roads.
The current used electric passenger car market is domi-
nated by Nissan Leafs imported from Japan, which is also 
a major source of ICEVs. New FBUs are dominated by 
Chinese EV brands.
EV energy solution companies import batteries, chargers, 
and battery management systems largely from China. Other 
parts are sourced locally, e.g., BaaS prefabricated ship-
ping containers.  
Electric mobility investments
There are over 35 electric mobility companies in Kenya 
dealing with electric two- and three-wheelers, passenger cars, 
buses, trucks, and charging infrastructure (EMAK 2024). 
These companies range from assemblers and retailers to energy 
solution and technology providers. Most of them are start-ups 
with total capital investments under $1 million in the form 
of grants and investor equity. Only three companies have over 
$10 million in investments (see Figure 11). Companies with 
larger funding have external backing, which contrasts sig-
nificantly with locally backed start-ups. The largest company 
has over 200 employees, but most start-ups interviewed have 
fewer than 10 employees. The companies adopt differing and 
mixed business models (e.g., business to business, pay as you 
go, business to customer, battery-as-a-service) and mostly 
identify as technology or energy companies. 
All companies carrying out local assembly have trade relations 
with component or vehicle suppliers from India, China, or 
both. They are also seeking partnerships within the Kenyan 
market to improve competitiveness. Though ICE equivalents

Financing: Supply side 
Most e-mobility companies are start-ups, typically lacking 
extensive banking history or collateral. Therefore, they are 
unable to access commercial lending from banking or financ-
ing institutions and rely on equity investors, venture funds, 
and grants. Funding available from these sources is usually 
insufficient to support working capital requirements. This is 
comparable to start-ups in other sectors in Kenya where there 
is a perception of high risk, causing financing institutions to 
be hesitant to provide requisite funding (Mwasi and Aluoch 
2023; Nakalembe et al. 2023). Debt financing is usually 
required for start-ups to scale, but proof of creditworthiness 
becomes a key barrier. Where start-ups can secure credit, the 
effects of foreign exchange (forex) depreciation and currency 
devaluation further erode the value of the acquired funding. 
Financing: Asset acquisition
There are several asset financiers in Kenya: specialized lend-
ers, mainstream banks, SACCOs, and microlenders. These 
institutions offer various credit facilities to cater to different 
customer segments. 
Mainstream banks largely finance the acquisition of passenger 
vehicles, trucks, and buses. Specialized lenders predominantly 
finance the acquisition of two- and three-wheelers with 
special credit terms (e.g., flexible repayment terms, minimal 
down payment requirements, and no collateral requirement 
besides asset co-ownership). These features target low-income 
earners to compensate for risk, but the interest charged is usu-
ally prohibitive, about three times higher than that charged by 
mainstream commercial banks. 
After-sales support
ICEV distributors in Kenya offer post-sales support for sold 
vehicles, but operators can also seek support from independent 
service providers. In the EV segment, after-sales support is 
provided by EV sellers or BaaS providers that are usually 
linked to assemblers and/or manufacturers. 
Presently, no third parties offer after-sales support for battery 
EVs in Kenya. However, there is an emerging market for EV 
battery diagnosis and servicing. 
Because Kenyan companies do not produce batteries, they 
rely on battery warranties from parent companies in China 
for locally assembled vehicles. In cases where a warranty 
must be claimed, the process of reshipping the batteries 
becomes complicated. None of the companies interviewed had 
reshipped faulty batteries, attributing this to high cost and 
logistical complications.
End-of-life management
Kenya has fewer than five companies currently working on 
reusing and recycling lithium-ion batteries and lead acid 
auxiliary batteries from vehicles. 
The recently assented Extended Producer Responsibility 
Regulations require product manufacturers to take respon-
sibility for proper product disposal or otherwise pay hefty 
fines as prescribed in the Sustainable Waste Management Act 
(GoK 2022c). While the regulations are forward looking, they 
have yet to be enforced. 
Two e-mobility companies mentioned they are collaborating 
with end-of-life battery companies to be able to sustain-
ably manage their battery waste. Battery reuse and recycling 
interventions will be crucial as soon as the technology matures 
and battery SoH for first-generation vehicles declines below 
the usable phase for EVs.

EVs are costly to acquire and to manufacture. Establishing 
and running production companies is often prohibitively 
expensive. As factory gate costs for energy-dense battery 
cells are still high at between $53 and $95 per kilowatt-hour 
(kWh) (IEA 2024), the overall upfront cost of EVs is rela-
tively expensive compared with ICEVs, even though the total 
cost of ownership for EVs is less than that of ICEVs (Con-

zade et al. 2022). People seeking to purchase EVs must then 
look for alternatives to finance the acquisition and face several 
downstream financing limitations:
 ▪ Unfavorable economic conditions limit purchasers from 
securing favorable financing terms, commonly referred to 
as evaluation risk.
 ▪ Mainstream financial institutions offering affordable 
interest rates are reluctant to extend credit facilities for 
two- and three-wheeler riders, who are perceived as high 
risk. Specialized lenders have exploited the opportunity 
by offering loans at exceptionally high interest rates, 
sometimes more than 50 percent pa.
 ▪ Mainstream banks offer low interest rates but higher 
upfront down-payment requirements, ranging from 40 to 
50 percent of the asset value, making it difficult to access 
affordable finance. 
 ▪ Two- and three-wheeler operators from low-income 
households are required to agree to short repayment 
periods (up to 18 months), making it difficult for 
individuals to pay.   
In addition, EV manufacturers, assemblers, and charging solu-
tion providers require significant resources to set up and fund 
their working capital requirements. For the EV transition to 
accelerate, these upstream industry players must produce more 
EVs and establish sufficient charging solutions to cover the 
market. Financing of these players has largely been through 
grants and equity investments. The small size of the Kenyan 
EV market has also led to limited financing activity in the 
Kenyan EV supply chain. In many instances, EV start-ups 
can’t access debt financing due to the following: 
 ▪ Start-ups often lack the collateral necessary to access loans 
(e.g., assets for term loans or cash covers for short-term 
working capital facilities). 
 ▪ Charging and BaaS infrastructure are intensive from 
a capital expenditure perspective and have longer 
repayment periods, thus fewer financiers are interested in 
investing in them.
 ▪ Some companies lack the audited financial statements 
required by banks to extend credit facilities. 
 ▪ Most companies lack sufficient bankable history to show 
historical cash flows in their bank accounts.
 ▪ Some business investments are capital intensive and 
require patient capital with longer payback periods.
 ▪ Mainstream financial institutions perceive electric mobility 
as a risky subsector and shy away from extending credit 
facilities, preferring the more traditional and well-
funded ICEV sector. 
 ▪ Established automotive companies with financial capacity 
to invest in the sector are hesitant to do so due to a 
perception that EVs are a relatively new technology. 
Technology and infrastructure  
(in more detail)
Infrastructure and other disincentives to purchasing EVs 
include the following:
 ▪ There is inadequate charging infrastructure across the 
country, which is largely due to the low fleet numbers 
beyond Nairobi, which do not provide enough economic 
incentive for companies to invest. 
 ▪ A robust charging infrastructure network is crucial for 
the widespread adoption of EVs, as range anxiety is a 
significant barrier to their purchase.
 ▪ Because of inadequate countrywide charging infrastructure, 
EVs are considered appropriate for only short commutes. 
Battery swap stations are concentrated in Mombasa 
and the Nairobi metropolitan area. And while there are 
a few standalone four-wheeler chargers in other towns, 
they are only single chargers operated on a pilot basis 
(Mboa et al. 2022).
 ▪ While most trips within cities are short and could be made 
by EVs, there is concern that EVs could not handle longer 
intercity trips.
 ▪ Current public charging infrastructure is inadequate to 
meet a higher level of demand as most are slow chargers 
and not sufficiently distributed in the cities.
 ▪ Electric two-wheeler providers mostly use a swapping 
model, which requires riders to return to swapping stations 
to obtain a recharged battery. These stations are often 
located in central areas with high demand to ensure the 
model is profitable. Unfortunately, this limits two-wheelers 
in terms of the distances they can cover as drivers must be 
able to return to these stations to recharge or swap for a 
recharged battery.
 ▪ The underdeveloped resale market for EVs causes resale 
anxiety (Zhang and Zhao 2023; Lim et al. 2014). EVs’ 
long-term value and ability to resell is unclear because 
of the limited number of EVs on the road compared 
with the number of ICEVs. This could possibly be a 
deterrent to uptake. 
 ▪ Due to the relatively young age of EVs in Kenya, the 
country hasn’t yet had to deal with EV waste and end-of-
life management for batteries. Despite some companies 
already rebuilding batteries for hybrid vehicles, the full 
extent of what’s required to manage waste in the sector 
isn’t understood.

Economic- and commerce- 
related challenges
 ▪ A lack of economies of scale and reliance on imports 
hinders companies’ abilities to scale. Kenya has over 
50 e-mobility companies selling fewer than 2,000 EVs 
annually. Due to the split market from the booming 
start-up scene, companies have not reaped the benefits of 
economies of scale. For example, most start-ups mentioned 
annual production targets of 100 units, meaning that 
the cost of production per unit remains relatively high. 
Moreover, foreign suppliers’ minimum order requirements 
in the high season are between 500 and 1,000 units, which 
disadvantages local start-ups, which must wait for the low 
season to have their orders fulfilled.  
 ▪ While forex depreciation has raised the cost of fossil fuels 
(Figure 13) and provided a better case for electric mobility, 
it has also eroded start-up profits and delayed breakeven 
timelines. The shilling, for example, depreciated 42.7 
percent between January 2022 and January 2024. Ordering 
EVs or components from other countries where currencies 
are stronger also puts an extra strain on local players, 
requiring them to supplement capital from other avenues. 
Currency depreciation affects the price offering of EVs, as 
locally assembled vehicles are sold in Kenyan shillings.
 ▪ Battery shipping also poses a challenge to managing costs. 
Due to the risk of thermal runaway, which can cause 
fires, shipping lines must adhere to United Nations (UN) 
regulations UN3480, UN3481, and UN3490, which limit 
the number of lithium-ion batteries that can be packed in 
shipping containers. Assemblers incur additional upfront 
costs for EV units, which undermines the desired market 
penetration of products. We couldn’t ascertain how these 
regulations affect company costs as they impact all global 
battery shipments. 
Awareness and capacity
 ▪ Varying interpretations of government policies have been 
problematic. There have been instances where agency 
personnel have had different interpretations of regulations 
regarding imports, such as how to classify CKDs or SKDs. 
Usually, CKD units are taxed at lower rates compared 
with SKD units. The incorrect classification of EV 
imports could lead to assemblers overpaying taxes and 
duties, indicating a capacity gap in government personnel 
handling EV imports. 
 ▪ Government institutions have insufficient capacity to 
implement electric mobility policies and regulations. 
The Kenya Bureau of Standards, for example, which 
is mandated to ensure product quality, does not have 
e-mobility testing facilities at ports of entry and 
therefore must rely entirely on visual inspections of 
imported vehicles.

Assessment of current policies  
and regulations
We analyzed current policies and regulations addressing 
different parts of the electric mobility ecosystem in Kenya 
and compared the interventions with progressive measures 
other countries have deployed to stimulate electrification. 
We chose to study China as it has the largest EV fleet and 
market globally (IEA 2023) and selected India as a developing 
country that has created initiatives quickly to scale electrifica-
tion. We chose Rwanda as a peer country since it has been 
applauded for its ambitious and effective policies to accelerate 
electrification. 
Overarching policies 
The Kenyan transport sector is governed by the following 
overarching policies:
 ▪ The Traffic Act (2012), a law regulating traffic on Kenyan 
roads (GoK 2012)
 ▪ The Integrated National Transport Policy (GoK 2020a), 
which provides a framework for development, regulation, 
and management of all transport modes
 ▪ The Green Economy Strategy and Implementation Plan 
(2016–2030) (GoK 2017), which guides the country’s 
transition toward a low-carbon, resource-efficient, 
equitable, and inclusive development path 
 ▪ The National Climate Change Action Plan (2023–2027) 
(GoK 2023a), which cites the transport sector’s 
contributions to the national emissions footprint and to 
Kenya’s plans for a green mobility transition
Targeted policies to promote electrification
The following other regulatory interventions target the 
e-mobility sector: 
Electric Mobility Policy and Strategy: At the time of publica-
tion, the government was working to develop an overarching 
electric mobility framework to enable coordination among 
government institutions and guide the countrywide 
EV transition. 
Electrification target: Kenya set a target to reach 5 percent 
annual EV sales by 2025 (GoK 2020b), despite EV sales com-
prising less than 1 percent of vehicle sales in 2023. Meeting 
this target will require instituting initiatives to encourage the 
industry to provide sufficient EVs for sale. 
The Finance Act (2019 and 2023) outlines various fiscal 
interventions that the government will use in the financial 
year targeting various sectors in line with development priori-
ties. These measures include taxation, exemptions, government 
expenditures, and revenue generation, among others. Finance 
Act 2019 (GoK 2019) and its 2023 amendment (GoK 
2023b) established incentives to increase fuel efficiency and 
electric mobility: 
 ▪ 10 percent excise duty for imported EVs; ICEVs pay 
20–35 percent depending on engine capacity
 ▪ The importation and sale of electric bicycles, motorcycles, 
buses, and lithium-ion batteries are zero-rated for VAT 
 ▪ Motorcycles under tariff heading 87.11 expanded to 
exclude electric motorcycles, exempting them from 
the excise duty
 ▪ Reduction of corporation tax from 30–37.5 percent to 
15 percent for five years from the start of motor vehicle 
assembly operations. A five-year extension was provided 
for companies whose products were comprised of 50 
percent locally sourced materials. EV companies setting 
up in Kenya would have significantly reduced their 
cost of doing business while promoting localization of 
manufacturing.

been experience in managing accidents, especially where there 
have been fires caused by batteries getting punctured. There 
will be a need to evaluate this in the future as more EVs are 
purchased and accident claims are made. 
The Electric Mobility Electricity Tariff (EPRA 2023a) 
reduced the electricity tariff for EV charging facilities to KES 
16 ($0.11) per kWh during peak periods and KES 8 ($0.05) 
per kWh during off-peak periods for energy consumption up 
to 15,000 kWh. This compares with the normal domestic tar-
iff rate of KES 20.97 ($0.14) per kWh for consumption over 
100 kWh. The maximum energy consumption cap of 15,000 
kWh will be limiting in the future for companies with signifi-
cant deployments of vehicles with larger-capacity batteries. 
The Energy and Petroleum Regulatory Authority’s guidelines 
for installation of charging and BaaS infrastructure (EPRA 
2023b) limit the number of charging points in an area and 
provide minimum requirements. These measures aim to ensure 
grid reliability and moderate power loading, but could give 
early market entrants an undue advantage by dominating 
charge points in certain areas. As EVs reach critical mass, 
the government will need to reevaluate this and develop 
regulations to make the guidelines enforceable as required 
by Kenyan law. 
Thirty-five electric mobility standards specify requirements 
for safety, performance, and testing of electric passenger cars 
and light commercial vehicles, motorcycles, and mopeds. 
The standards help protect the population from harmful or 
substandard products but do not hamper innovation.
Sustainable Waste Management Act 2022 provides regula-
tions for sustainable sourcing by placing a strong emphasis 
on circularity, and the need for responsible and sustain-
able waste management practices including managing 
end-of-life batteries to minimize their impact on the environ-
ment (GoK 2022c).
Extended Producer Responsibility Regulations, 2022, though 
not enforced, are regulations to operationalize the Sustainable 
Waste Management Act by holding producers accountable 
for their products’ entire lifespans, including EV batteries and 
end-of-life vehicles (GoK 2022b).
What isn’t Kenya’s government 
doing that it could be doing?
Policy and regulatory barriers include the absence of a 
comprehensive electric mobility policy, the inadequate incen-
tivization of production, and the suboptimal classification of 
vehicles and utilization of standards to ensure the quality and 
safety of registered and operating EVs. 
 ▪ Overarching policy. The absence of a comprehensive 
national e-mobility policy has impeded the roll out of 
well-thought-out interventions in e-mobility. 
 ▪ Production incentives. The country’s NAP (GoK 2022a), 
which limits the importation of used vehicles while 
incentivizing the local assembly of vehicles, has not been 
implemented, resulting in insufficient incentives for local 
automotive manufacturing including EV assembly. Further, 
the government could create greater awareness about the 
financial benefits associated with its SEZs.
 ▪ Standardization for quality and safety. The current 35 
electric mobility standards cover only safety and testing 
protocols, missing key metrics like battery SoH checks that 
could ensure that only quality products are allowed into 
the country. This could potentially compromise the quality 
and lifespans of EVs in Kenya and erode gains made by 
early adopters.

How does Kenya’s policy 
environment compare with 
policies in China, India, and 
Rwanda to mainstream EVs?
In markets where the EV transition has scaled quickly, 
governments have typically encouraged local manufacturing 
through several initiatives like production subsidies (see Table 
1). The strategy aims to increase affordable EV access in the 
country, reduce transportation costs, and improve turnaround 
times, while fostering employment, stimulating the economy, 
enhancing local skills, and promoting sustainability. 
Tax incentives and subsidies 
Kenya relies largely on imports for automotive assembly 
components and vehicles. China and India, on the other 
hand, have advanced automotive production (MinInfra 2021). 
Incentives adopted for Kenya have focused on VAT exemp-
tions for imported batteries, buses, and bikes and excise duty 
reductions for FBUs. On the other hand, India and China 
have provided aggressive, multistage sector tax incentives 
aimed at promoting the production and purchase of EVs. 
Kenya can promote local component manufacturing and use 
incentives to meet its quota for local content.   
Skills and R&D 
Research in the advanced markets has been focused on grow-
ing local capacity for production and maintenance of EVs. 
This R&D has contributed to the progressive growth of the 
countries’ competitive advantages in manufacturing. On the 
other hand, Kenya and Rwanda have not expressly targeted 
building local technical capacity. A similar targeted R&D 
initiative in Kenya could help grow the country’s production 
and assembly based on its competitive advantage in manufac-
turing. Companies in Kenya are already collaborating with EV 
producers for technology transfer. There is also an opportunity 
for a government-led initiative to provide strategic technical 
capacity building by incorporating EV education into the 
Ministry of Education’s national curriculum and promoting 
government-to-government technology exchanges. 
Charging infrastructure
Promoting access to charging infrastructure is a key interven-
tion area that has been led by line ministries. Rwanda has 
provided free government land to set up this infrastructure. 
On the other hand, Kenya has only reduced the electric-
ity purchase tariff. Deployment of charging infrastructure 
has been led by the private sector. More aggressive govern-
ment involvement in rolling out charging infrastructure in 
Kenya is required. 
Financing
The advanced markets have used incentive grants and a credit-
ing system where companies earn credits from the production 
and sale of EVs. While the Kenyan government may not 
provide direct grants, there is an opportunity to facilitate the 
development of green financing and provide de-risking mech-
anisms for the private sector to access affordable financing. 
End-of-life management
Like India and China, Kenya has Extended Producer Respon-
sibility Regulations. However, these guidelines have yet to be 
implemented. Kenya should consider successes in India and 
China in combining requirements to transition to EV tech-
nology together with a scrappage policy for polluting ICEVs.

Recommendations:  
Opportunities for electrification
Policy and regulatory 
Having in place functional enabling policies is key to a vibrant 
electric mobility ecosystem. We’ve identified the following 
opportunities in this area:
 ▪ An overarching electric mobility policy and strategy would 
boost investor confidence as the electric mobility sector 
continues to grow. This electric mobility policy and strategy 
should remain dynamic to accommodate anticipated 
changes in electric mobility uptake. The policy should 
provide a comprehensive framework that incorporates 
incentives to support EV manufacture and use.
 ▪ The government can support automotive and component 
assembly through production-linked incentives in line 
with the NAP’s objectives, promoting innovation, R&D, 
and investment schemes. Tax policy should encourage 
local manufacturing over import reliance, and sound fiscal 
balance is necessary for a pragmatic and implementable 
policy. A rigorous review of the tax policy would be 
required, possibly following Juhász et al. (2023) who 
proposed using a broader range of policies rather than 
individual instruments and including a policy that 
promotes productivity. 
 ▪ Kenya should introduce EV sales requirements—
regulatory requirements for automakers to increase their 
share of EV production—as done by the countries that 
joined the zero-emission vehicles (ZEV) declaration at the 
26th UN Climate Change Conference (COP26) (GoUK 
2023). The California Air Resources Board program 
has a sales mandate whereby vehicle manufacturers are 
required to increase ZEV sales from 4.5 percent in 2018 
to 22 percent in 2025 (CARB 2024b). Success of the 
program resulted in California achieving its goal of 1.5 
million ZEVs sold two years ahead of the 2025 target 
(CARB 2024a). In China’s New Energy Vehicle mandate, 
automakers are awarded credits for producing EVs and 
are allowed to trade excess credits to other automotive 
manufacturers. China’s EV manufacturing dominance can 
be partly attributed to the existence of this credit system. 
Kenya could evaluate how such sales mandates would 
work in-country. 
 ▪ To attract quality new and used EVs, there needs to be a 
requirement for a certification of conformity that limits 
the importation of EVs with batteries with reduced 
SoH. This would prevent negative publicity resulting 
from short lifespans of vehicles with depleted batteries. 
The government already has a program for pre-export 
verification of conformity for ICEVs. Metrics of evaluation 
for EVs are different from those for ICEVs; providing a 
similar protocol for EVs will be necessary. 
 ▪ Available regulations and policies (e.g., Extended Producer 
Responsibility Regulations, National Automotive Policy) 
must be implemented.
 ▪ The energy regulator should operationalize the guidelines 
for establishing battery swapping and charging 
infrastructure by developing regulations as required by law. 
 ▪ The Kenyan government should balance the use of fiscal 
policy to de-risk investment flows into the supply chain 
while ensuring countries are not held hostage by a reliance 
on imports. De-risking investments through attractive 
and predictable fiscal policies would ensure the country 
positions itself as an appealing investment destination 
for foreign investors. Constant monitoring is necessary 
to ensure the country’s objective of promoting local 
manufacturing is not compromised, which would lead to a 
reliance on imports.   
 ▪ The Finance Act amendment of 2023 introduced tax 
exemptions for companies in the e-mobility space. While 
fiscal expenditures deriving from these exemptions 
are not yet public, and the act has since been deemed 
unconstitutional, the government should consider 
extending such benefits to the entire EV ecosystem (e.g., 
to companies that manufacture specific components of 
EVs) to further support the industry as part of a broader 
set of policies. 
 ▪ Leveraging the EAC CET , countries can adopt 
additional regulations to eradicate barriers to regional 
trade. Despite having the CET , countries in the region 
have repeatedly petitioned to lift the application of the 
CET , which effectively disallows other countries in the 
region from claiming concessions on taxes for products 
exported within the region. Additional regulations would 
limit country petitions that lift the application of the 
tariff in their countries. Also, the EAC needs to create a 
Harmonized System code for completely knocked down 
electric motorcycles.

Technological and infrastructural 
 ▪ Companies providing superior products that can compete 
with ICEVs in terms of sturdiness and longevity will 
influence consumer choice. To ensure EVs entering the 
Kenyan market are of good quality, batteries should go 
through a pre-export verification process, which should 
include an SoH check.
 ▪ Local EV companies should invest in R&D so they can 
provide products and business models that can compete 
with those of ICEV companies. For example, industry 
firms mentioned they have progressively worked to adjust 
imported products to fit riding patterns, road quality, and 
local circumstances. 
 ▪ Target modes should be expanded to include urban freight 
vehicles and e-bikes to leverage the rising demand for 
delivery services via e-commerce platforms and appeal to a 
younger demographic that appreciates physical activity. 
 ▪ Since batteries that pack more energy are more expensive, 
costs affiliated with energy-dense batteries will ultimately 
determine the overall cost competitiveness of EVs. Any 
intervention providing solutions to energy access and 
availability is bound to increase the adoption of EVs (e.g., 
provision of adequate charging facilities).
 ▪ The opportunity exists to decrease a reliance on imports 
and lower local assembly costs by promoting local 
manufacturers of automotive components such as steel 
frames, footrests, lighting, direct current converters, seats, 
and storage compartments.  
 ▪ Kenyan policymakers have not addressed the need for 
after-sale support for EVs. Battery recycling and reuse 
would provide an opportunity for new business ventures 
(e.g., reuse of EV batteries as solar back-up batteries) 
(Chen et al. 2023).  
Economic- and commerce-related 
Trade-related opportunities are largely centered around driv-
ing demand for EVs and creating a conducive environment 
for trade growth. We have identified the following opportuni-
ties in this area:
 ▪ The government’s special economic zones have been 
used to attract foreign direct investment, though research 
shows there is hesitancy among independent evaluators 
to vouch for them as lucrative investment zones (Oringo 
et al. 2023). That notwithstanding, companies looking 
to set up in the zones could save on capital expenditures 
and operating expenses by, for example, reducing their 
corporate income tax for five years from 30–37.5 percent 
outside the SEZs to 15 percent inside the SEZs.
 ▪ Current ICEV manufacturers could pivot to also 
manufacture EVs. With their infrastructural advantage, 
they could easily shift—partially or wholly—to EV 
manufacturing with an interim assembly opportunity. 
Batteries could also be assembled in Kenya, though not 
battery making as the country does not have reserves of 
critical minerals. 
 ▪ Promoting electrification in shared mobility and captured 
fleets, such as taxis and ride-hailing platforms, could 
drive demand opportunities. Policymakers can promote 
such use by providing financial incentives to operators 
willing to invest in EV marketing, sales, and distribution 
opportunities. These also need to be part of measures that 
ensure the government maintains a fiscal balance (e.g., that 
carbon taxes cover proposed incentives). 
 ▪ Outsourcing manufacturing or warehousing could be 
used as a way to reduce cost (provide cost efficiency 
and aggregation of expertise as well as manufacturing 
infrastructure).
 ▪ Increased collaboration among countries along the 
supply chain would reduce costs as logistical processes are 
streamlined. For example, China has developed a vertical 
integration strategy for the production and distribution 
of lithium-ion batteries where companies collaborate 
with upstream and downstream players to increase cost 
efficiency and reduce overhead (Wang et al. 2022).
 ▪ As Kenya is the country with the highest number of 
start-ups in the subregion, it can position itself to meet the 
subregion’s EV demand through exports. EV companies 
would further benefit from terms of the EAC CET . 
Financial 
 ▪ Loan guarantees should be provided to de-risk 
upstream companies (EV manufacturers and energy 
solution providers) as most don’t meet debt financing 
criteria. Providing such guarantees would eliminate 
the requirement for start-ups to provide their banking 
history, collateral, and audited accounts to access debt 
financing. Guarantees have been used to de-risk the energy 
sector in Kenya (Amolo et al. 2020) and lessons learned 
(Saadani et al. 2011) should be used to design effective 
guarantee schemes. 
 ▪ First loss guarantees on currency devaluation could protect 
firms from high interest rates and forex fluctuations, 
providing financial cushioning to businesses involved in 
electric mobility. This measure could help boost investor 
confidence and reduce the impact of currency-related trade 
fluctuations (Lubis and Karim 2021; Ogbonna 2016).

 ▪ Financing institutions could explore providing consumer 
subsidies through a results-based financing approach 
whereby payments are made after achieving predefined 
results. Consumer subsidies can help lower the initial 
purchase price of EVs, making them more accessible and 
attractive. More research and modelling are required to 
map out how such financing would look for EVs in Kenya. 
 ▪ Carbon markets could be leveraged for financing (e.g., as 
SunCulture has done to accelerate access to and use of 
solar irrigation systems for farmers) (Shell Foundation 
2023). This has been on a pilot basis, and therefore would 
need to be developed for practical applicability in electric 
mobility in Kenya. Other countries and regions, such as 
China, the United States, and the European Union (Zhou 
and Li 2019), have used carbon markets to finance zero-
emission projects, and Kenya could draw lessons learned 
from their experiences.  
Awareness and capacity 
 ▪ It is essential for the government to provide clear 
information on new changes, interventions, and incentives 
in the electric mobility sector. Transparent policies, 
regulations, and incentives can encourage businesses and 
consumers to take advantage of the benefits of electric 
mobility while reducing market uncertainty.
 ▪ The government’s institutional capacity should be 
strengthened so it can implement its mandates and 
support electrification. Government staff and infrastructure 
capacity (e.g., testing facilities) should be strengthened 
and, where nonexistent, be provided. 
 ▪ A targeted technical curriculum on electric mobility will be 
necessary to produce a sufficiently large skilled labor force 
for EV production as well as after-sale support. The current 
competencies curriculum should therefore be revised to 
include technical skills for electric mobility. 
 ▪ An open, simple-to-understand platform containing all 
interventions geared toward sector stimulation and tackling 
misinformation on EVs should be created. Countries 
leading in electrification have websites containing this 
information, and these could be used as models.1 
Conclusions
This working paper has provided a high-level analysis of 
barriers to and opportunities for electrification in Kenyan 
road transport. 
There is a need for data-driven solutions to the opportunities 
described in this paper. Investors and government leaders will 
need to employ targeted modelling to weigh budgetary costs, 
estimate business value, and design and conduct pilot pro-
grams, among other activities.
Interventions should address access to adequate and affordable 
financing for EV manufacturers, assemblers, energy solution 
providers, and end users. 
Investors should evaluate proposed economic- and commerce-
related opportunities with a view to driving and sustaining 
demand for EVs. 
For policies to be successful, investors and the government 
will need to use innovative and wide-reaching means to ensure 
that awareness about the benefits of EVs becomes widespread 
and capacity gaps are filled.

About WRI Africa 
Our vision is an inclusive transformation so that Africa’s people 
and landscapes can flourish. WRI has been working in Africa for 
more than 30 years toward a more sustainable society that can 
meet people’s essential needs, protect and restore nature, and 
stabilize the climate.
Our Approach
To realize this vision, WRI Africa works directly with local partners 
and governments to drive action across four strategic pillars: vital 
landscapes; thriving and resilient cities; clean energy; and climate, 
economics, and finance. These pillars encompass the systems that 
most directly impact people, nature, and the climate — from the 
ways we grow food and produce goods to how we power buildings 
and transportation. Effecting change in all four areas will be critical 
to achieving a truly green, inclusive, and resilient transformation 
throughout Africa.
