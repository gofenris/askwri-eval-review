HIGHLIGHTS
 ▪ Climate commitments are not in line with the pathways needed to 
achieve net zero emissions and meet the goals of the Paris Agreement.
 ▪ National governments must significantly enhance the ambition of their 
Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs) to accelerate economy-
wide, low-carbon development transformations and limit global 
temperature rise.
 ▪ Subnational governments like cities, states, and regions fill a 
significant governance gap in climate action and offer a key space 
for meaningful collaboration and integration among local actors, 
geographies, and contexts.
 ▪ This document provides guidance to national governments on how 
to develop their NDCs in partnership with subnational governments 
through eight recommendations and accompanying practical steps, tools, 
and case study examples.
 ▪ By following this guidance, countries can produce NDCs that 
incorporate strong subnational content, reflect the highest possible 
ambition, and increase likelihood of implementation.

Conclusion 
Multilevel partnerships are key to catalyzing and optimizing 
climate action across scales. By collaborating with subnational 
governments throughout the NDC development process, and 
by integrating subnational content, national governments can 
significantly improve the ambition, credibility, and implemen-
tation of their NDCs.
INTRODUCTION 
Climate change poses an unprecedented threat to societies 
and ecosystems worldwide. Without the rapid reduction of 
greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions across the globe and bold 
action to reduce climate impacts, the world will face devas-
tating consequences to be felt for generations to come. Yet 
current climate commitments are not in line with pathways 
needed to achieve net zero emissions and meet the goals 
of the Paris Agreement (UNFCCC 2023). Countries can 
significantly enhance the ambition of their Nationally Deter-
mined Contributions (NDCs) through a more collaborative 
and integrated approach with subnational governments to 
fully align with the Paris Agreement goals (Lydén et al. 2020; 
Tollin et al. 2024).
This document supports national governments in the facilita-
tion of input from subnational governments in the NDC 
development process. It has been written with the United 
Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change’s 2025 
NDC deadline in mind, but is designed to support countries 
beyond this milestone and can be considered a tool for devel-
oping all successive NDCs. The document demonstrates how 
the climate action potential presented by subnational govern-
ments can not only encourage greater confidence among 
national governments to meet climate targets but also enhance 
the credibility of the NDCs themselves (ICAT 2020). 
Following this section, “Research Approach” describes the 
methodology informing this document. “Background” dis-
cusses the role of multilevel partnerships in achieving net zero 
GHG emissions and enhancing climate resilience. “Orienta-
tion” situates the reader with a legend and additional context 
to navigate this document. “Developing Stronger NDCs with 
Cities, States, and Regions” puts forward a series of eight 
recommendations for action across three stages that support 
national governments to strengthen their NDCs by collabo-
rating with and integrating subnational content (see Box 1). 
Finally, “Conclusion” summarizes the key findings, recommen-
dations, and implications of this research.
RESEARCH APPROACH
Exploratory insights from a literature review and iterative 
consultations with subject matter experts on the NDC-setting 
process and multilevel partnerships are the main methods 
informing this document. Two rounds of consultation were 
conducted with a group of 20 experts, composed of Coalition 
for High Ambition Multilevel Partnerships (CHAMP) for 
Climate Action partners, World Resources Institute (WRI) 
staff, and regional climate experts (see Appendix A).
Literature review and expert 
interviews
The authors conducted an in-depth literature review encom-
passing 75 publications from WRI’s global database, including 
gray and academic literature. A narrative review enabled the 
authors to explore the wide range of intersecting topics with 
more flexibility. From here, the first round of expert consulta-
tions were conducted to discuss initial research scoping and 
identify common themes. These key themes were collated into 
a coding structure to review literature (Appendix B) and guide 
the second round of expert interviews (Appendix C). Results 
from the coded literature served to identify recommended 
actions for countries and inform practical steps for each of the 
common themes, all of which were further refined through 
the second round of interviews. Additionally, an analysis of 
the NDC Partnership’s knowledge management system, the 
kNook database, was undertaken to identify the support needs 
of national governments relating to subnational engage-
ments and ensure that the recommendations addressed these 
needs (NDCP 2024b).

Case studies
Case studies and best practices were methodically identified 
in the literature and through expert interviews via the coding 
structure. Examples included in this publication were based 
on the ability of the case to illustrate the recommendations 
for each NDC development stage in practice. Colombia 
was selected as an in-depth case study (see Appendix D) 
because it was mentioned by several experts as a best practice 
example and has been highlighted as a country with strong 
urban content in its NDC (Tollin et al. 2024). Furthermore, 
Colombia as a CHAMP endorser is committed to the goals 
mentioned in Figure 3 and to the ongoing collaboration with

for greater insight into the process. 
Limitations
This research has limitations due to varying governance 
structures and the underutilized potential of subnational 
NDC engagement. It focuses on general actions related to 
NDC development but excludes climate financing and NDC 
implementation. Future work could address these areas. Due 
to time constraints, the authors were unable to interview 
a greater diversity of stakeholders, but additional experts 
reviewed the paper for gaps in content. The recommendations 
are exploratory and preliminary but offer valuable guidance for 
strengthening the NDC development process and centering 
the role of subnational governments (GCoM and WRI 2023).
BACKGROUND 
Across geographies and sectors, all countries must accelerate 
economy-wide, low-carbon development transformations to 
limit global temperature rise and meet the goals of the Paris 
Agreement. This means cutting global GHG emissions 43 
percent by 2030 and 60 percent by 2035, relative to 2019 
(Srouji et al. 2024). With nearly half the global population 
highly vulnerable to climate change impacts, there is an 
additional and urgent need to scale climate adaptation efforts 
and safeguard communities, ecosystems, and local economies 
(Boehm and Schumer 2023; Hussein et al. 2024). 
National governments have many tools at their disposal 
to achieve these targets and guide domestic policymak-
ing toward a sustainable climate future. However, current 
climate commitments put the world on track for nearly 3°C 
of global warming by 2100, which can lead to catastrophic 
and irreversible economic, social, and environmental con-
sequences (UNEP 2023; Wong and Mackres 2024). The 
impetus for rapid and ambitious climate action has never 
been more urgent.
The state of national climate 
commitments
NDCs are one of the primary mechanisms by which countries 
can effectively tackle the climate crisis (Srouji et al. 2024). 
Following the 2015 Paris Agreement, countries are required 
to update these commitments every five years to reflect the 
highest possible ambition, as shown in Figure 1. By early 
2025, countries are expected to submit a new round of more 
ambitious NDCs, signifying an important milestone en route 
from the adoption of the initial NDCs to achieving net zero 
GHG emissions by mid-century. Consequently, NDCs can be 
considered a benchmarking mechanism to assess progress on 
global climate goals (IPES-Food 2023).
Rapidly transforming our collective global systems cannot be 
done with country-level commitments alone. NDC imple-
mentation can only be as robust as the collaboration taking 
place among various government levels and other non-state 
actors. Often overlooked by both national governments and 
international climate negotiations, subnational governments 
like cities, states, and regions fill a significant governance 
gap in climate action and offer a key space for meaningful 
collaboration and integration among local actors, geogra-
phies, and contexts.
Current NDCs, however, remain difficult to translate into 
relevant action at the subnational governance level (Plescia-
Boyd et al. 2020), while sector-based commitments fall short 
of empowering subnational governments to pursue ambitious 
climate actions (CUT 2019). Coordination among different 
levels of government is a key but underutilized mechanism 
for designing effective NDCs, especially considering the pace 
and scale of transformational change required (Tollin et al. 
2024; UNFCCC 2023).
The multilevel partnership opportunity 
All levels of government have a critical role to play in the 
transition to a net zero and climate resilient future. An 
estimated 90 percent of global urban GHG emissions can be 
cut by technically feasible and widely available low-carbon 
measures (CUT 2019). Yet few of these identified measures 
fall exclusively within the sphere of national or subnational 
government influence. For much of the urban (and wider 
subnational) abatement potential, there is a significant amount 
of overlap in the roles various government levels can play. 
To unlock this opportunity, it is critical to build national 
climate plans on a foundation of multilevel partnerships that 
are fully integrated with subnational perspectives and con-
tent (ICAT 2020). NDCs have the unique role of informing 
national development agendas for long-term climate action, 
which can promote investment in public infrastructure, 
prepare for future risks, and support a just transition (Climate

The subnational perspective
Climate commitments set by national governments will have 
profound implications for how we plan our cities, administer 
our public health systems, and manage our economies. With 
over 10 additional megacities anticipated by 2030 and a 
further 2.5 billion people living in cities by 2050, there is a 
particular opportunity for countries to focus on low-carbon 
and resilient development pathways as urban populations con-
tinue to expand (United Nations Department of Economic 
and Social Affairs 2019). This is important since unplanned 
urban growth may lead to even greater climate change impacts 
(Tollin et al. 2024).
A review conducted by the United Nations Human Settle-
ments Programme (UN-Habitat) on the urban content of 
NDCs submitted before July 2023 demonstrates that national 
climate commitments continue to struggle with operational-
izing urban climate action (Tollin et al. 2024). The analysis 
reveals that only 27 percent of the 194 NDCs have strong 
urban content (Figure 2), while less than half (40 percent) 
of NDCs with such content mention climate hazards at the 
urban level (compared to 89 percent of NDCs identifying 
climate hazards at the national level). UN-Habitat defines 
strong urban content as NDCs with specific urban sections or 
where urban areas are identified as priority sectors. Moderate 
urban content are NDCs with mentions of urban content in 
the body of the text, and low content is considered low to no 
mentions (Tollin et al. 2022).

every stage of NDC development and implementation is

The Coalition for High Ambition Multilevel Partnerships 
(CHAMP) for Climate Action was established to help 
national governments realize this multilevel partnership 
opportunity. At the 28th United Nations Climate Change 
Conference (COP28), 72 countries endorsed a set of prin-
ciples to enhance cooperation with subnational governments 
in the planning, financing, implementation, and monitoring 
of climate strategies—including NDCs—for the collective 
pursuit of climate change mitigation, adaptation, and resil-
ience (WRI 2024).
CHAMP aims to foster more inclusive development of 
NDCs with meaningful and substantial input from sub-
national governments, resulting in more ambitious and 
collaborative climate actions. This document aims to support 
CHAMP endorsing countries and other national govern-
ments in achieving these goals.
Common, but differentiated
Not all governments have the mandate or capacity to act on 
climate. Capacity, especially in terms of staffing, is a primary 
barrier to ambitious climate action for subnational govern-
ments (Wong et al. 2023). This aligns with a recent analysis 
of 136 countries that submitted conditional NDCs as of 
June 2019, in which 83 percent identified capacity-building 
as the type of support most frequently requested (Pauw et al. 
2020). Both capacity and responsibility must be considered, as 
countries with high carbon footprints need to make ambi-
tious climate mitigation commitments while simultaneously 
supporting geographies facing developmental challenges with 
adaptation planning. Indeed, adaptation is a climate justice 
issue (C40 Cities 2020). Cooperation among government lev-
els and nations is key for an equitable, sustainable, and climate 
resilient low-carbon transition.
Furthermore, political, fiscal, and administrative powers are 
decentralized to varying degrees around the globe. Despite 
significant differences in the delegation of competencies, sub-
national governments possess a unique array of assets that can 
be leveraged for ambitious action (Schultz et al. 2015). This 
includes hard powers such as direct authority over key sectors, 
and regulatory or financial powers (e.g., carbon taxes). Addi-
tionally, subnational governments can utilize their soft powers 
to influence decisions beyond their jurisdiction by exercis-
ing their advocacy, diplomacy, and convening powers (e.g., 
linking the renewable energy transition to improved health 
care outcomes). These capacities may not be accessible across 
all political contexts and are hindered by resource limita-
tions, competing priorities, and legislative constraints (Wong 
et al. 2023). National governments should strive to address 
these limitations, within the contexts of their circumstances, 
to support subnational action and facilitate the cooperation 
necessary to achieve the goals of the Paris Agreement.

Sectoral targets as a strategic NDC 
approach
National governments can foster a cross-cutting approach to 
climate action by integrating sectoral targets in their NDCs. 
For example, buildings, transport, and waste are the three larg-
est sources of direct GHGs in cities, which offer high-impact 
opportunities that can deliver co-benefits on local issues such 
as public health, housing, and employment (CUT 2019). 
Countries can accelerate sectoral transformations through 
both GHG (e.g., shift to public transit) and non-GHG 
(e.g., protected forest area) targets, which can serve to mutu-
ally enhance NDC impact (Sato et al. 2019). Appendix E 
describes key sectoral opportunities to reduce GHG emissions 
and enhance climate resilience that can be unlocked through 
multilevel partnerships. This includes shifting energy supply 
to renewables, increasing access to sustainable transport, and 
compact urban design. 
ORIENTATION
This document gives national governments guidance on how 
to develop their NDCs in collaboration with subnational 
governments to increase climate ambition and likelihood 
of implementation. The recommendations are structured 
into chronological stages, as depicted in Table 1. It should 
be noted, however, that the stages are interconnected. The 
document is designed to be useful at any stage in the NDC 
development process, acknowledging that different countries 
are at different stages. We acknowledge that some recom-
mendations and good practices shared may not be directly 
applicable in every case and will need to be tailored to the 
local context. Therefore, this document is not intended to be 
prescriptive and should rather be considered a guiding tool. 
While the recommendations for each stage have a particular 
focus on subnational governments, the role of nongovernmen-
tal actors such as universities or community groups cannot 
be overlooked. It is critical that national governments take a 
whole-of-society approach and ensure that the principles of 
inclusivity and equity underpin not only the NDCs but all 
climate policies (NDCP 2024a). 
National governments should keep in mind the complemen-
tary nature of NDCs and other climate plans such as National 
Adaptation Plans, National Biodiversity Strategies and Action 
Plans, and National Urban Policies (see Abubakar 2022,  
pp. 24−25). Such formal mechanisms require coordinated and 
consistent action from various levels of government and other 
stakeholders. Lastly, the scope of this document is limited 
to the NDC development process. While several recom-
mendations aim to ensure that the NDCs are more likely to 
be implemented, financing and implementing the NDC is 
beyond our scope. 
Incorporating strong subnational 
content in the NDCs
This document has two interconnected priorities to support 
national governments in producing more ambitious and 
credible NDCs (see Figure 3). The first is to empower national 
governments with practices to meaningfully collaborate with 
subnational governments throughout the NDC development 
process. The second is to integrate subnational content into 
the NDCs to enhance both vertical (i.e., national and sub-
national strategies) and horizontal (i.e., coherence of climate 
priorities) coordination across government levels.
The NDC development process should be considered a 
participatory process for facilitating ongoing and meaning-
ful dialogues with key actors to reach a consensus on climate 
priorities (Gerhard et al. 2022). Subnational governments are 
critical partners in this regard, since their convening power, 
combined with their proximity and accountability to local 
actors, can help ensure that diverse interests are represented 
while improving transparency and securing wider buy-in.

DEVELOPING STRONGER 
NDCS WITH CITIES, STATES, 
AND REGIONS 
This section presents eight recommendations for national 
governments to collaborate and integrate subnational content 
in NDCs. The recommendations are grouped into three stages 
and summarized in Table 2. 
Stage 1: Review
Stage 1 encourages national governments to review the 
present state of multilevel partnerships and clarify insti-
tutional arrangements. This helps ensure a more informed 
and coordinated approach to NDC development. Expert 
interviews highlighted the need for clear focal points and 
greater awareness of domestic governance processes, since 
many NDC-related plans and policies may be scattered across 
government agencies. See Appendix F for a table on climate 
responsibilities for various levels of government. 
Rationale
Developing more ambitious NDCs requires the most updated 
knowledge and data to inform effective climate targets. Con-
textually relevant information from subnational governments 
has never been more pertinent, as climate change research 
remains skewed regarding the perspectives represented. 
Through our analysis of NDC Partnership’s kNook database, 
we found that only about 10 percent of the 6,300 national 
government requests for support submitted since 2017 were 
related to cities or subnational governments (NDCP 2024b). 
This could indicate a large gap in national government 
awareness and consultation practices related to the capacity 
of subnational governments to support national climate goals. 
Expert interviews also emphasized the importance of collec-
tion, exchange, and transparency of local data for the effective 
integration of subnational content in NDCs.

Stage 2: Plan
Planning the approach to NDC development ensures that 
priorities are strategically aligned and fosters a shared sense of 
ownership among stakeholders. This stage lays the foundation 
for effective collaboration to develop a more ambitious and 
actionable NDC that reflects the diverse needs and capacities 
of subnational governments. Expert interviews described how 
the platforms and frequency of engagement were just as criti-
cal to effective NDC consultation. Additionally, formulating 
a clear vision and objectives to guide the development process 
and establishing or identifying a dedicated coordination body 
will ensure an effective approach to designing an ambitious 
NDC. Expert interviews discussed the advantage of subna-
tional governments internalizing the NDC in local policy for 
effective environmental, social, and economic outcomes.
Rationale 
Intentional coordination between government levels is crucial 
to overcome policy incoherence and ensure that investments 
align with climate targets (Shawoo et al. 2020). Recent 
research demonstrates that countries with a comprehensive 
and transparent stakeholder consultation process are more 
likely to strengthen their GHG reduction targets (Peterson 
et al. 2023). National governments can establish effective 
pathways for collaboration that go beyond mere consultation 
to ensure a shared responsibility for climate commitments 
and implementation. Subnational governments are often in 
the best position to inform national governments of pressing 
mitigation and adaptation needs and have the infrastructure 
to realize such climate targets locally (NDCP 2024a). Under-
standing the political context and establishing clear principles 
for accountability and inclusion are important to allow for 
transparent and responsible decision-making (CIF 2024). 
Recommendation 2.1.  Create a vision and stakeholder engagement plan for the NDC in collaboration with subnational governments (see Adriázola et al. 
2018; GCoM 2022; Lydén et al. 2020; Rodríguez Granados et al. 2020; WWF 2024).
Practical steps:
 
 ■Identify key subnational government stakeholders and categorize based on their influence and interest (see Reed et al. 2009). Ensure that 
both technical and political leadership at various levels are included. 
 ■Assess the current level of understanding of the NDC development process among subnational governments, and the technical and time 
availabilities to contribute. Consider a training program to build the capacity of subnational governments in stakeholder engagement 
plans. 
 ■Outline different methods for engaging subnational governments, such as workshops, public consultations, surveys, focus groups, and 
online platforms. Identify existing engagement opportunities that could be leveraged for NDC consultation (GCoM 2021).
 ■Host a series of initial consultations to understand the extent to which they might be able to support stakeholder engagement activities 
at local levels.
 ■Outline the resources (financial, human, and technical) required to implement the stakeholder engagement plan. Assign clear roles and 
responsibilities for coordinating stakeholder engagement activities and create a detailed timeline including key milestones.
 ■Establish a collective vision to align stakeholder perspectives and objectives.
       
Clear messaging is crucial when engaging with subnational governments. Emphasize the importance of the NDCs and their relevance to 
regional and local realities. Publicly presenting NDC development plans can showcase ambition, incentivize action, and attract political 
attention (NDCP 2019).
Consider emphasizing alignment between the NDC and other existing development objectives like the Sustainable Development Goals, 
which have strong alignments related to poverty alleviation, land use, and infrastructure (Fransen et al. 2017; NDCP 2019).
National networks can assist with stakeholder identification and coordination. For example, Rwanda’s Sector Working Group has existing 
governance infrastructure to facilitate effective environmental coordination, minimizing the need to duplicate efforts (NDCP 2019). Brazil’s 
National Front of Mayors represents the country’s 400 largest cities and approximately 60 percent of the population and has been critical 
to improving public health, urban mobility, and forest conservation (Vital Strategies 2019).

Recommendation 2.2.  In consultation with subnational governments, develop clear objectives to guide NDC development, incorporate strong 
subnational content, and enhance the visibility of subnational contributions (see C40 Cities 2022; Pauw and Klein 2021; Tollin et al. 2024;  
Vener et al. 2019).
Practical steps:
 
 ■Conduct consultations with subnational governments to co-develop objectives for NDC development.
 ■Where an NDC coordination body exists, consider including a technical representative from subnational governments and a range of 
national ministries. Consider establishing an NDC political oversight committee with representation from political leaders at a national, 
regional, and local level. For greater ambition and transparency, consider including nongovernmental stakeholders.
 ■Highlight specific opportunities for subnational government contributions to help achieve existing targets and reflect the highest 
possible ambition in new and updated NDCs. 
 ■Document roles, responsibilities, timelines, and expectations of the NDC coordination body publicly and/or establish mandates through 
legislation for accountability and transparency.
 ■Encourage endorsement of the NDC development plan by all associated parties. 
 ■Document ideas beyond the current revision scope that could feed into a long-term plan for iteratively improving NDCs.

      Uruguay’s National Response System to Climate Change and Variability developed a framework for the NDC revision process that included 
several working groups to draft ambitious commitments inclusive of adaptation, mitigation, gender, programming, monitoring, reporting, 
and verification. Municipalities of various coastal departments ensure the implementation of climate actions in partnership with other

In 2016, Argentina conducted a national consultation process to align climate targets with subnational government efforts. Discussions

Stage 3: Develop 
Stage 3 focuses on direct collaboration with subnational 
governments to design and update NDCs to achieve rapid, 
ambitious, and long-term climate impact. Collaboration 
requires proactively removing barriers to participation, assign-
ing clear responsibilities, and building strong accountability 
measures (NDCP 2024a). Through multilevel partnerships, 
various regulatory instruments such as performance stan-
dards, licensing, building codes, or zoning can be utilized to 
accelerate mitigation and adaptation (GCOM 2022). Expert 
interviews revealed the importance of not just developing the 
NDCs in collaboration with subnational governments but also 
following an iterative process to further increase ambition.
Rationale
Despite subnational governments being key implementers 
of climate action, the development of most NDCs still lacks 
meaningful involvement from subnational governments 
(Tollin et al. 2024). Subnational governments are at the center 
of regional climate responses. This includes regulating sectors 
like transportation or waste with high climate mitigation 
potential, as well as climate disaster response and adaptation 
(C40 Cities 2020). For countries most vulnerable to climate 
change, adaptation measures must be prioritized (Pauw et al. 
2020). Collaborating with subnational governments in the 
development of NDCs can help local and regional govern-
ment actors gain ownership over the climate targets, creating 
an incentive for joint implementation across different levels of 
government (Voïta 2020).

CONCLUSION 
NDCs are a crucial tool for addressing the climate crisis, but 
their effectiveness is hindered by limited subnational involve-
ment despite being on the front lines of climate change 
impacts and responses. Multilevel partnerships offer a promis-
ing solution to bridge this gap. This document provides eight 
recommendations to help national governments leverage mul-
tilevel partnerships with subnational governments to achieve 
their climate commitments. By collaborating with subnational 
governments during the NDC development process and 
integrating subnational content, national governments can 
increase NDC ambition and implementation.

APPENDIX D. APPLYING THE 
STAGES IN PRACTICE WITH 
COLOMBIA’S NDCS
The practical example of Colombia is provided in the case 
below, demonstrating some of the ways in which a national 
government can implement the stages and practical steps listed in 
this document. 
Colombia’s 2025 NDC revision process offers a leading example 
of effective strategy, enhanced impact, and collaborative NDC 
development. A Colombia focus was chosen due to the country’s 
significant dedication to integrate subnational input in its NDC and 
its transformational approach to a climate-resilient society and 
economy by 2050 (Tollin et al. 2024).
Colombia’s 2020 NDC increased emission reduction ambitions to 
an absolute limit of 169 megatons of carbon dioxide equivalent, an 
impressive 51 percent reduction compared to business as usual, 
which significantly exceeds previous 2015 climate goals (Climate 
Watch 2024). Following the updated NDC, Colombia launched the 
2021 Long-Term Climate Strategy containing a comprehensive set 
of mitigation and adaptation targets that intersect across all NDC 
sectors (UNEP n.d.). This includes a shift to 100 percent electric or 
zero emission vehicles for transportation systems in all major cities 
(Uribe-Botero 2021). The strategy also recognizes the pivotal role 
of local and regional actors like municipal mayors and regional 
environmental authorities in managing rates of deforestation 
among other environmental issues. While this was progressive 
for the nation, expert interviews revealed that measures proposed 
by various stakeholders were not feasible, goals were unaligned, 
and some indicators were not measurable. Multilevel partnerships 
and collaborations were identified as a critical solution to the next 
phase of setting climate targets.
For the 2025 NDC revision, the national government is hosting a 
series of open discussion meetings as well as an open forum on its 
government website for subnational government actors—including 
governors, mayors’ offices, and environmental authorities—to share 
initiatives (Ministry of Environment and Sustainable Development, 
n.d.). Workshops and practical exercises with subnationals are 
being conducted to better understand the implications and 
outcomes of proposed climate activities. 
The national government is offering support to subnationals to draft 
proposals, which require letters of institutional support and other 
documentation supporting the proposed climate measures, as 
well as bilateral meetings to further clarify concerns. Stakeholder 
consultations can help support scaling implementation by 
identifying and promoting local climate change solutions that 
can be replicated in other parts of Colombia (Dixit and O’Connor 
2022). The Colombian Association of Capital Cities (Asocapitales) 
is also facilitating training among city governments on local climate 
actions alongside WRI Colombia.

APPENDIX E. KEY SECTORAL 
OPPORTUNITIES
National governments can foster a cross-cutting approach to 
climate action by integrating sectoral targets in their NDCs. 
Countries can accelerate sectoral transformations through 
both GHG (e.g., shift to public transit) and non-GHG (e.g., 
protected forest area) targets, which can serve to mutually 
enhance NDC impact (Sato et al. 2019). Table E-1 describes key 
sectoral opportunities for countries to reduce GHG emissions 
and enhance climate resilience that can be unlocked through 
multilevel partnerships. 
Table E-1 | Key sectoral opportunities that can be unlocked through multilevel partnerships
Buildings 
(IEA 2023)
More than a third of global energy consumption and emissions are driven by the buildings sector. To achieve net zero 
goals, energy consumption by buildings must fall by 25% and fossil fuel use must decrease by more than 40% by 
2030. Multilevel partnerships can facilitate regulations (e.g., building codes), incentives (e.g., loans), and facilitation 
(e.g., educating the workforce) to drive this outcome. Mandatory building codes have been found to be the most 
effective tool for improving efficiency. Partnerships between national and subnational governments support the 
effective implementation of these standards, fostering a more sustainable construction industry. Maximizing existing 
building stocks and retrofitting are also key to reducing GHG emissions and enhancing resilience to future disasters.
Energy generation
(Boehm et al. 2022; IEA 2023; Melica 
et al. 2018)
To remain on track to achieve the goals of the Paris Agreement, the rate at which electricity generation is 
decarbonizing needs to increase by a factor of 6 by 2030. National governments often have control over electricity 
supply, transmission, and interconnection and can provide overarching policies, funding, and incentives that set the 
stage for large-scale projects. Subnational governments can tailor policies to their context, lead engagements with 
communities and businesses to secure their support, and facilitate the implementation of small-scale energy projects. 
They can help aggregate demand and manage flexibility on both supply and demand sides, capturing synergies from 
sector coupling and from integrated action on mobility, heating, cooling, and storage and supporting infrastructure 
connectivity. Linking clean energy generation with electric vehicles, for example, can create more flexible energy 
systems that optimize resource use and reduce emissions across sectors. Similarly, integrating renewable heat 
solutions with district heating systems can improve energy efficiency and resilience.
Food system
(IPES-Food 2023)
Food systems are responsible for one-third of global GHGs. Despite representing 50% of food system emissions, 
only 11% of countries have committed to reducing food loss or food waste in their NDCs. Action on the food system 
is critical since 67% of deforestation is driven by agriculture. Subnational governments are leading the way on 
sustainable food strategies, including through increased plant-based food consumption and reducing food loss and 
waste. Multilevel partnerships are key to further unlocking this critical sustainability opportunity.
Fossil fuel phaseout
(IEA 2023; UNFCCC 2023)
The transition away from a fossil fuel−based economy will have a profound impact on economic performance, 
employment, and industry practices around the world. Tackling methane emissions, eliminating flaring, and increasing 
electrification are the three top interventions to realize this transition. Subnational governments have a critical role 
to incentivize this shift, through actions like carbon pricing and other policy developments that regulate the fossil 
fuel industry. At least 30 regional governments plan to phase out coal, compared to zero in 2019. Most national 
governments continue to subsidize fossil fuel consumption and/or production, reducing the cost and limiting revenues 
that could be utilized for climate action. Multilevel partnerships are necessary for policy cohesion and consistency 
across regulations. Transitioning from fossil fuels requires careful consideration to ensure economic burdens are 
distributed to those responsible.
Transport 
(C40 Cities 2022; IEA 2023)
To reach net zero emissions, global transport emissions must fall by about 25% by 2030. Presently, 91% of transport 
continues to rely on oil products—only 3.5% below rates from the early 1970s. Multilevel partnerships are critical to 
increasing access to safe, affordable, low-carbon, and resilient transport options. High-impact strategies include 
implementing restrictions on high-polluting vehicles, investing in infrastructure for cycling and walking and an 
integrated public transit system, e-bus procurement, and improving freight logistics through electrification and green 
corridors.
Urban planning 
(C40 Cities 2022)
Unregulated and unplanned urban expansion can lock in GHG emissions, increase vulnerability to climate change, 
and exacerbate inequalities. Multilevel partnerships can drive integrated urban planning by supporting densification 
strategies, better connectivity through transit-oriented development, and the colocation of jobs and housing by 
establishing mixed-use zones along major transport corridors and eliminating requirements for parking minimums. 
Urban planning can enhance resilience by integrating nature-based solutions to reduce heat stress and flood risks 
and by upgrading informal neighborhoods that are vulnerable to climate hazards.

Waste
(C40 Cities 2022 ; Ritchie et al. 2024)
Landfills are the third-largest source of methane emissions globally. Without urgent action, municipal waste 
generation is projected to increase by 56% by 2050 due to industrialization and urbanization. National governments 
can help subnational governments address methane emissions in the treatment of solid waste and wastewater by 
restricting or banning nonrecyclable materials and single-use plastics, establishing requirements and incentives for 
source segregation, promoting recycling services and circular economy approaches, enabling composting and/or 
anaerobic digestion of organic waste, and ensuring sanitary waste disposal with landfill gas capture.
Urban green and blue infrastructure
(Lwasa et al. 2022)
Urban green and blue infrastructure, also known as nature-based solutions, such as urban forests, street trees, 
permeable surfaces, and green roofs help by sequestering and storing carbon and cooling the environment. These 
solutions lower energy demand for thermal comfort and water treatment. Urban trees worldwide store around 7.4 
billion tonnes of carbon and sequester approximately 217 million tonnes of carbon annually. Additional benefits of 
nature-based solutions include mitigating the urban heat island effect, reducing heat stress, managing stormwater 
runoff, improving air quality, and enhancing both the mental and physical well-being of urban residents.

APPENDIX F. GOVERNMENT 
ROLES IN CLIMATE ACTION
There are a variety of climate and development responsibilities 
that fall under different levels of government. Table F-1 pro-
vides high-level examples of the range of these duties, sorted 
by national, regional, and local levels of government.
Table F-1 | Roles of different levels of government in climate change action 
National Government  ■National governments are the country representatives and signatories of international climate agreements.
 ■They are responsible for mapping out and setting strategic orientation for national climate policy frameworks and economic 
development (short- and long-term targets).
 ■National governments are in charge of creating an enabling legal and institutional framework (such as regulating building 
codes & standards and establishing national GHG inventories) to facilitate mitigation and adaptation as well as develop and 
enforce regulations, laws, and policies in key sectors.
 ■They are responsible for formulating national policies related to climate change while benefiting from and bringing forth 
bottom-up perspectives of communities and governments at other levels.
 ■Responsible for allocating funds and authorizing national infrastructure projects (roads, power plants, water supply systems).
 ■National governments are responsible for supporting regional and local governments by providing tools, data, and guidance for 
climate action planning and monitoring the performance of national climate policies.
Regional government 
(i.e., states, provinces, 
regions)
 ■Regional governments play a key role in implementing national laws and standards.
 ■They support and oversee the development of regional climate policy and economic development frameworks (targets and 
strategic orientation).
 ■Regional governments are uniquely placed to help scale local perspectives as well as bring the national laws to other levels of 
the government in a context-specific and implementable manner.
 ■They support the creation of regional laws and policies in sectors like energy, air pollution, and water management while 
enabling a coherent consideration of regional circumstances. 
 ■Regional governments are responsible for regulating performance in specific sectors as permitted by national law (building 
codes, appliance standards) and providing funding and incentives for local governments to implement climate solutions.
 ■They help characterize risks at the regional scale and define risk management principles as well as monitor regional GHG 
emissions and policy performance over time.
 ■Regional governments are responsible for ensuring decision-makers at all levels have the necessary tools and information for 
effective climate decisions.
Local government  
(i.e., cities, towns)
 ■Local governments are responsible for implementing climate actions locally as required by national or regional laws and 
exercise autonomous decision-making where authority exists (e.g., land use, urban planning).
 ■Local governments help identify local priorities and manage local infrastructure (roads, flood control, water supply, waste 
management).
 ■They are positioned such that they can help raise awareness and be a vehicle for learning and broader dissemination as well 
as lead to bottom-up diffusion of successful locally adapted approaches.
 ■Local governments are responsible for elaborating in-depth city resilience profiles from a multihazard, multisectoral, and 
multistakeholder perspective, and producing actionable guidance for long-term capacity building.
Note: GHG = greenhouse gas.
Source: Adapted from Abubaker 2022.

GLOSSARY
CHAMP Coalition for High-Ambition Multilevel Partnerships for Climate Action. Launched by the COP28 Presidency in partnership with 
Bloomberg Philanthropies in December 2023 to enhance cooperation with subnational governments (e.g., cities, provinces, states, 
regions) in the planning, financing, implementing, and monitoring of climate plans and strategies, and to better integrate subnational 
mitigation and adaptation actions within the next round of Nationally Determined Contributions (WRI 2024).
CHAMP endorsement National governments that endorse CHAMP signal their support for the objective of CHAMP and, specifically, to act according to the 
six pledge clauses.
Climate change 
adaptation
Actions to make populations and geographies less vulnerable to the ongoing impacts of climate change; for example, building 
seawalls or restoring wetlands to protect against the increasing risk of flooding (Fransen et al. 2019).
Climate change 
mitigation
Actions to limit climate change by reducing or elimination GHGs from the atmosphere.; for example, transitioning away from fossil 
fuels and increasing the uptake of alternative renewable technologies (Fransen et al. 2019).
Hard power Financial and regulatory mechanisms, such as taxes, set by any level of government that can serve to mobilize actions and allocate 
spending to address an issue of concern (Abubakar 2022).
Just transition The process of moving toward a greener world in ways that won’t create or exacerbate inequalities or cause other unintended 
social and economic harm. It also means taking action to mitigate such harms, creating opportunities that benefit all people and 
communities, and promoting sustainable development (Climate Strategies 2024).
Multilevel governance 
(partnerships; 
collaboration)
A set of structures and institutions that designate and coordinate responsibilities among different government levels for the 
implementation of policies, decisions, and actions (Abubakar 2022). 
NDC enhancement 
(revision; 
development)
The process of developing new or updated NDC revised targets and ambition. According to the Paris Agreement, countries are 
supposed to update new targets every 5 years (Fransen et al. 2017).
Net zero emissions A condition in which metric-weighted anthropogenic GHG emissions are balanced by metric-weighted anthropogenic GHG removals 
over a specified period (GCoM and WRI 2023).
Soft power The ability to influence actions by any level of government through agenda setting to align government levels on a specific issue and 
link the issue to the larger landscape of development planning (Abubakar 2022).
Subnational content Climate- and development-related data, trends, targets, risks, policies, and actions associated with subnational government levels, 
including cities, states, and regions (Tollin et al. 2022).
Subnational 
governments (cities, 
states, and regions)
Any geographically discernable subnational entity such as a town, city, metropolitan authority, or province, including all levels of 
subnational jurisdiction and local government as legal entities of public administration (GCoM and WRI 2023).
Urban content NDCs that have one or more sections specifically dedicated to urban areas and/or NDCs in which urban areas are explicitly identified 
as priority sectors (Tollin et al. 2022).
Whole-of-society/all-
of-society
 
A whole-of-society/all-of-society approach is when governments build on each iteration of stakeholder engagement to incorporate 
new actors and deepen engagement with those who have already participated in NDC processes; i.e., engaging across all levels of 
the government, all sectors, and all actors (NDCP 2024a).

ABOUT WRI 
World Resources Institute is a global research organization that 
turns big ideas into action at the nexus of environment, economic 
opportunity, and human well-being. 
Our challenge
Natural resources are at the foundation of economic opportunity 
and human well-being. But today, we are depleting Earth’s 
resources at rates that are not sustainable, endangering economies 
and people’s lives. People depend on clean water, fertile land, 
healthy forests, and a stable climate. Livable cities and clean 
energy are essential for a sustainable planet. We must address 
these urgent, global challenges this decade.
Our vision
We envision an equitable and prosperous planet driven by the wise 
management of natural resources. We aspire to create a world 
where the actions of government, business, and communities 
combine to eliminate poverty and sustain the natural environment 
for all people.
Our approach
COUNT IT
We start with data. We conduct independent research and 
draw on the latest technology to develop new insights and 
recommendations. Our rigorous analysis identifies risks, unveils 
opportunities, and informs smart strategies. We focus our efforts 
on influential and emerging economies where the future of 
sustainability will be determined.
CHANGE IT
We use our research to influence government policies, business 
strategies, and civil society action. We test projects with 
communities, companies, and government agencies to build a 
strong evidence base. Then, we work with partners to deliver 
change on the ground that alleviates poverty and strengthens 
society. We hold ourselves accountable to ensure our outcomes 
will be bold and enduring.
SCALE IT
We don’t think small. Once tested, we work with partners to adopt 
and expand our efforts regionally and globally. We engage with 
decision-makers to carry out our ideas and elevate our impact. We 
measure success through government and business actions that 
improve people’s lives and sustain a healthy environment.
