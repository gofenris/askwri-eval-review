HIGHLIGHTS
 ▪ Vietnam’s 2020 nationally determined contribution (NDC) updates aim 
to reduce greenhouse gas emissions by 9 percent (through domestic 
efforts) and 27 percent (with international support) by 2030, compared to a 
business-as-usual scenario. 
 ▪ Electric mobility is considered a key strategy to mitigate greenhouse gas 
(GHG) emissions and generate individual and social benefits, especially in 
large cities like Ho Chi Minh City (HCMC). 
 ▪ This paper evaluates the potential costs of implementing financial incen-
tives for electric vehicles (EVs)—purchase subsidies and registration 
rebates—in HCMC and the benefits related to individual savings and 
GHG emissions reductions from increasing EV adoption. We examine two 
scenarios reflecting different levels of funding availability.
 ▪ The analysis indicates that implementation could deliver net positive 
benefits during the early stages of EV adoption in HCMC, with a benefit-
cost ratio estimated at 1.86 (discounted at 2 percent) and 1.69 (discounted 
at 6 percent) over a period of eight years, which is equal to a net present 
value (NPV) of $275 million (discounted at 2 percent) and $190 million 
(discounted at 6 percent). 
 ▪ Our analysis and evidence from leading EV markets show that incentives to 
make EVs more cost-competitive, along with support for the deployment of 
charging stations, will be crucial for increasing EV adoption in HCMC.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY 
Context
Since the launch of its foreign economic policy, Đổi Mới  (Reno-
vation), in 1986, Vietnam has undergone a series of reforms 
to boost its economy. During this time the country’s popula-
tion has rapidly increased and urbanized. The population of 
HCMC—Vietnam’s largest city—is expected to continue to rise 
significantly through 2050. 
Vietnam’s economic expansion has been accompanied by a 
substantial increase in its GHG emissions. Amid global efforts 
to mitigate climate change, Vietnam’s national government has 
outlined emissions reduction targets for 2030, including the goal 
of reducing transportation-related emissions by 9–20 percent 
compared to business-as-usual levels, the higher target being 
dependent on international support.
As a major population and economic hub, HCMC will play 
a primary role in Vietnam’s decarbonization efforts. Within 
HCMC and most other urban areas in the country, internal 
combustion engine (ICE) two-wheelers are the most common 
mode of transportation and a significant source of GHG emis-
sions, along with passenger cars. In this context, expanding the 
use of EVs in the city is being discussed as a key component of 
Vietnam’s transportation decarbonization strategy.
About this working paper
Concurrent research by World Resources Institute is evaluating 
key components of vehicle electrification roadmaps and identi-
fying main barriers to EV adoption, as well as policies different 
governments have used to boost EV adoption in leading EV 
markets. WRI’s roadmap research indicates that evaluating local 
budget viability, as well as technological and institutional factors, 
and designing financial incentives to encourage consumers to 
switch to EVs, are key steps in developing EV roadmaps and 
vehicle electrification targets. To build on WRI’s EV roadmap 
research, this working paper explains how leading EV markets 
have implemented financial incentives to address existing 
market barriers to EVs. It evaluates the potential costs and 
benefits of implementing different EV incentives to encourage 
EV sales in HCMC during the early stages of EV adoption, 
under assumptions that reflect the city’s market conditions and 
budget availability.
We conduct a cost-benefit analysis (CBA) to compare the costs 
of implementing demand-side policies versus individual and 
social benefits that such policies may generate. This analysis was 
supported by several underlying pieces of research. Specifically, 
we focused on four main tasks: literature reviews and engage-
ment with local partners to better understand the national and 
local contexts and policy goals; literature reviews to identify 
consolidated practices, policies, and tools from leading countries; 
selection of financial incentives and estimation of a feasible 
budget that may be adopted to increase the local market share 
of electric cars and two-wheelers based on different scenarios; 
and a CBA that estimates the economic benefits for EV users 
in terms of realizable cost savings, the social benefits associated 
with avoided damages resulting from emissions reductions, 
and the costs of implementing EV incentives over a period 
of eight years. 
Decarbonizing the transportation sector will require a suite of 
policies and actions to address travel behaviors and different 
travel modes, including public transit. Public transit, especially 
electrified, is an important feature of urban mobility. While 
the Transportation Urban Mobility Initiative (TUMI) eBus 
Mission and the HCMC Department of Transportation have 
launched an electric public transit program, the scope of this 
study is limited to personal vehicles, including two-wheelers and 
passenger vehicles. The demand for both these transport modes 
is expected to increase, and fuel-powered passenger vehicles and 
two-wheelers are currently the dominant mode of transport. 
Hence we focus on collecting and analyzing evidence that can 
help national and local officials support the transition toward 
electric vehicles and two-wheelers during the early stages of 
adoption through incentives that reduce the gap between the 
price of fuel-powered vehicles and two-wheelers and that of 
their electric alternatives. We also seek to complement existing 
implementation and analyses of public transit. 
Findings and conclusions
 ▪ Financial incentives are necessary to make EVs more 
attractive to consumers during the early stages of EV 
market development. This lesson emerges from leading EV 
markets, including China, the United States, and Europe, 
where demand-side financial incentives have supported 
EV adoption by making EVs more price-competitive with 
ICE vehicles. Financial incentives are generally adopted 
for less than 10 years. The literature suggests that financial 
incentives should be available for a reasonable duration to 
ensure predictability, and policymakers should regularly 
evaluate their impacts and use different criteria to phase out 
such incentives, replacing them with other complementary

 ▪ Multiple incentives can be adopted in the early stages of

 ▪ Of the ICE cars available in HCMC and included in our

The CBA yields four principal findings:
 ▪ Based on our analysis of the existing vehicle market, we 
evaluate an electric car purchase bonus of $1,400, an electric 
car fee registration rebate of $500, and an electric two-

 ▪ Identifying the initial budget can improve understanding 
of potential targets and estimation of vehicle growth across 
time. We estimate that on average during the early stages 
of deployment, 11,000 electric cars and 130,000 electric 
two-wheelers may be subsidized in eight years under a low 
scenario. Under a high scenario, 30,000 electric cars and 
370,000 electric two-wheelers could be subsidized. 
 ▪ Providing financial incentives for EV adoption generates 
individual and social benefits. During an eight year-period, 
the benefits-costs ratio is estimated at 1.86 (discounted at 2 
percent) and 1.69 (discounted at 6 percent), which is equal to 
an NPV of $275 million (discounted at 2 percent) and $192 
million (discounted at 6 percent). Based on our calculations, 
each new EV may generate $76 per year in net benefits 
(discounted at 2 percent) and $65 per year in net benefits 
(discounted at 6 percent), which is equal to 1.7 million and 
1.4 million Vietnamese dong (VND), respectively.
 ▪ Savings in costs generated by transitioning to EVs can 
free up resources that can be channeled back into the 
economy and help induce further benefits from other 
economic activities. 
As experienced in different international cities, designing 
effective financial incentives is the first step toward making EVs 
more affordable and attractive to consumers. While our analysis 
indicates that the availability of EV incentives could deliver net 
positive benefits in HCMC, HCMC officials should carefully 
evaluate the local vehicle market and considerations such as 
budget availability to design and implement effective incentives 
to overcome the main barriers restricting EV uptake in the city.
BACKGROUND 
Since the launch of its foreign economic policy, Đổi Mới  (Reno-
vation), in 1986, Vietnam has undergone a series of successful 
reforms to make the country more competitive (Path 2020; 
World Bank 2020a). Between 2000 and 2020, the Vietnamese 
economy grew eightfold, to over $271 billion, solidifying Viet-
nam as one of the 50 leading economies in the world (World 
Bank 2020b; World Bank 2021). In addition, the country’s 
population has been increasing rapidly for decades, rising by 
almost 22 percent over this period to over 97 million people in 
2020 (World Bank 2020b). Vietnam is also urbanizing; about 
60 percent of the population is projected to live in major urban 
centers by 2050 (Hai 2018). Ho Chi Minh City (HCMC), the 
country’s largest city, will play an increasingly important role 
in the national economy. It already contributes over 20 percent 
of the country’s gross domestic product (GDP), and HCMC’s 
population is projected to grow an additional 48 percent, to 13.2 
million people, by 2050 (Iris Marketing Agency 2021; HCMC 
Statistical Office 2019). 
Alongside its economic success, Vietnam has tremendously 
increased its greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, which reached 
approximately 365 million tons of carbon dioxide equivalent 
(MtCO2e) in 2018, with per capita emissions increasing to 
4.5 tCO2e in 2018, from 0.03 tCO2e in 1990 (Climate Watch 
2020). Vietnam is the 11th-highest carbon emitter among 
Asian Pacific countries on a total basis (BP 2022). Amid global 
efforts to combat climate change, the national government has 
pledged to reduce Vietnam’s GHG emissions by 9 percent below 
business-as-usual levels by 2030, as outlined in its most recent 
nationally determined contribution (NDC) submitted to the 
UN Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNDP 2020). 
The transportation sector accounted for 18 percent of Vietnam’s 
total GHG emissions in 2014, with emissions in 2030 expected 
to be 168 percent of 2014 levels, and is thus a core area of focus 
for Vietnam’s NDC targets (Oh et al. 2019). Available estimates 
indicate that the country could reduce its transportation-related 
emissions in 2030 by 9 percent compared to business-as-usual 
levels through domestic efforts and by 15–20 percent with inter-
national assistance (Oh et al. 2019). Achieving the latter range 
of emissions reductions would require that electric two-wheelers 
make up 30 percent of the two-wheeler fleet and that electric 
vehicles (EVs) account for at least 30 percent of the passenger 
vehicle fleet (Oh et al. 2019). 
As a major population and economic hub, HCMC will play 
a primary role in Vietnam’s decarbonization strategy. Within 
HCMC and most other urban areas, internal combustion 
engine (ICE) two-wheelers are the most common mode of

In HCMC alone, ICE two-wheelers make up 93 percent of

such as solar and wind (Ritchie et al. 2021). Vietnam’s transition 
to a cleaner electricity generation profile in the coming years will 
help reduce the emissions of the country’s burgeoning electric 
mobility sector. 
Vietnam’s domestic EV market is already growing. Several elec-
tric two-wheeler options are available in Vietnam from domestic 
and international manufacturers, including VinFast, Pega, and 
Terra Motors, which are becoming more popular (NNA Business 
News 2018; Nguyen 2020). VinFast, part of Vietnam’s biggest 
conglomerate, Vingroup, is Vietnam’s first domestic car manu-
facturer, and the company has been touting its electric models 
(Coghill 2021). Most recently, the company announced that 
it has started constructing its own multimillion-dollar battery 
cell plant, which will allow the company to own its EV bat-
tery supply chain (Coghill 2021). Furthermore, VinFast plans 
to expand in North American markets by establishing an EV 
manufacturing facility in the U.S. state of North Carolina by 
2024, aiming to start production at 150,000 vehicles per year 
(VinFast Automotive 2022).

METHODOLOGY 
This analysis builds on concurrent research by WRI that explains 
key components of national and subnational vehicle electrifica-
tion roadmaps and different actions policymakers need to take 
to develop an effective EV roadmap. WRI’s roadmap research 
is based on case studies from different leading EV markets and 
uses findings on barriers to EV adoption and policies imple-
mented by different governments to increase EV adoption. It 
identifies two primary steps for developing EV roadmaps and 
vehicle electrification targets: the evaluation of local budget 
capacities as well as technological and institutional factors, and 
the design of financial incentives to encourage consumers to 
switch to EVs. This working paper evaluates potential costs and 
benefits of implementing different EV incentives to encour-
age EV sales in HCMC, under assumptions based on available 
data that reflect the city’s existing market conditions and 
budgets, and it explains how different leading EV markets have 
implemented financial incentives to address existing market 
barriers to EVs.
Public transit is an important feature of urban mobility. How-
ever, the scope of our research focuses on personal vehicles, 
including passenger vehicles and two-wheelers, and comple-
ments existing implementation and analyses of public transit. 
The TUMI eBus Mission and HCMC Department of Trans-
portation have an electric public transit program underway. This 
paper offers HCMC planning officials supporting analyses and 
policy recommendations on personal vehicle segments.
Our analysis focuses on three main tasks. First, we conducted 
a literature review to identify policy and practices from lead-
ing international EV markets. Our literature review aimed to 
address three key questions: 
 ▪ How can consumers’ demand preferences be changed and 
their adoption of electric vehicles increased? 
 ▪ What is HCMC’s potential role and policy in changing 
demand preference to increase EV adoption? 
 ▪ Why take a demand approach in HCMC? 
To answer these research questions WRI examined peer-
reviewed literature published in English as well as reports from 
relevant research institutes and international organizations 
from the past 10 years, covering the latest advancements in EV 
technology and policy. 
Second, we engaged with local partners to collect local data 
regarding the transportation market and socioeconomic and 
public finance characteristics of Vietnam and HCMC. We used 
these data to develop cost-benefit analysis (CBA) assumptions 
and scenarios, identifying the financial incentives that HCMC 
officials could adopt to increase the local market share of electric 
cars and two-wheelers over an eight-year period. Then we iden-
tified a benchmark from available HCMC public finance data to 
quantify a potential budget that could financially support these 
policies based on different scenarios.
Third, we conducted a CBA based on a multiscenario assump-
tion that estimated the cost of the expected financial incentive 
program, the cost savings EV buyers could realize, as well as the 
social benefits associated with avoiding damages by mitigating 
GHG emissions ($/ton of CO2) over a period of eight years. 
We discounted costs and benefits to their present value using 
a 2 percent and 6 percent real discount rate, respectively.1 We 
then compared the discounted costs and benefits using a CBA 
and the metric of net present value (NPV) as a common metric 
because it converts costs and benefits into a single value by 
discounting so they can be compared in present value terms. 
NPV allows policymakers to determine whether an investment 
generates losses or gains, and to compare investment options 
(Cubbage et al. 2013).
Overall, this research presents a pilot analysis of key EV policy 
considerations highlighted by WRI’s vehicle electrification 
roadmap research, providing evidence of potential economic 
costs and benefits of specific actions to incentivize EV adoption 
in HCMC, and explaining how national and local governments 
can play an important role in incentivizing the demand for EVs 
by reducing the current gap between the prices of ICE vehicles 
and EVs and supporting the implementation of enabling infra-
structure like charging stations.

DEMAND-SIDE MEASURES 
IN THE EARLY STAGES OF EV 
ADOPTION: POLICIES AND 
PRACTICES FROM LEADING 
INTERNATIONAL MARKETS 
Over the past decade, EV sales have steadily increased world-
wide, 17 percent in the United States, for example, between 
2016 and 2020, compared with 36 percent in China and 60 
percent in Europe (Desilver 2021). This growth is due, in part, 
to actions by national and local policymakers to identify effective 
means of increasing EV adoption. 
Previous studies on S-curves of low-carbon technology adop-
tion and market penetration show that technologies like EVs 
are nearing a tipping point, meaning their sales could grow 
exponentially, due to cost reductions driven by improvements 
in battery and vehicle manufacturing and enabling factors such 
as financial incentives for vehicle purchase and manufactur-
ing, vehicle sale requirements for automakers, and improved 
access to charging infrastructure (Dennis 2021; Boehm et al. 
2021). In the coming years, such rapid sales growth and wide-
spread adoption will require strong incentives that continue 
to make EVs more affordable and convenient during the early 
stages of adoption.
Saha et al. (2021), who evaluate the role of financial incen-
tives like tax credits and complementary policies in supporting 
low-carbon technologies through different stages of market 
adoption, also explain that during the early stages of adoption 
purchase incentives including tax credits, rebates, and grants are 
essential to making emerging clean technologies more afford-
able in their competition with incumbent fossil fuel–based 
alternatives (Figure 2). As emerging technologies like EVs reach 
cost parity with incumbents like ICE vehicles and experience 
market diffusion, purchase incentives can be supported and 
replaced by policies such as emissions standards, a carbon tax, 
or other environmental regulations. These policy tools can help 
maintain a level playing field for cleaner technologies in the 
market and avoid technology “lock-in”—the prolonged use of 
high-emissions technologies because it is cheaper to use existing 
equipment and infrastructure until it reaches its natural end of 
life (Saha et al. 2021).
E-mobility is still in the emerging stages of market adoption in 
Vietnam and HCMC. In terms of two-wheelers (which make 
up more than 90 percent of Vietnam’s vehicle fleet), electric 
models accounted for 8.3 percent of sales in 2020 compared 
to 4.9 percent in 2019 (Le and Yang 2022). EVs, including 
passenger vehicles and two-wheelers, represented 2.7 percent 
of vehicle registrations in 2020 (Vietnam Register 2020). Cost, 
limited driving range, and long charging times are barriers 
that continue to inhibit widespread EV adoption (Higueras-
Castillo et al. 2021). 
Different leading EV markets have implemented various 
demand- and supply-side incentives (Table 1) to address these 
barriers. As emerging markets consider their future e-mobility 
strategies, they can refer to actions taken by leading cities and 
countries and determine best practices. Through financial incen-
tives, such as tax credits and purchase subsidies, and nonfiscal 
incentives, such as fee exemptions and parking and driving 
privileges, policymakers have attempted to make EVs more 
attractive to consumers (Zhang et al. 2014). Policymakers have 
also pursued their EV adoption goals through indirect means, 
such as promoting consumer-awareness programs, investing in 
technology development, supporting charging infrastructure 
expansion, and providing financial incentives to EV manufac-
turers (Zhang et al. 2014). Below we discuss key takeaways for 
policymakers aiming to increase EV adoption in HCMC. 
Which policies might HCMC implement 
to increase EV adoption? 
EVs have become increasingly popular in Vietnam in recent 
years, increasing from under 2,000 units in 2010 to over 1.8 mil-
lion in 2020 (2.7 percent of overall vehicle registrations in 2020), 
mostly consisting of electric two-wheelers (Vietnam Register 
2020). For EVs to become a major share of the vehicle market 
in HCMC, policymakers must consider aggressive policies to 
foster EV adoption (Vietnam Register 2020; Oh et al. 2019; 
World Bank 2022). 
Evidence from countries and cities around the globe (as seen in 
Table 2) shows that demand-side policies can promote greater 
EV adoption by making them more affordable and convenient. 
These policies address some of the main barriers that inhibit 
consumers from purchasing EVs, such as purchase price, 
dependence on a charging network, and consumer awareness 
(Broadbent et al. 2018; Higueras-Castillo et al. 2021). Assess-

China has been offering subsidies for purchasing EVs since 
2009, and purchase incentives feature strongly in China’s 
e-mobility strategy, along with actions such as promoting EV 
awareness through government purchases and exempting EVs 
from traffic controls (Zhang et al. 2014; Ma et al. 2017; He et al. 
2018). These policies have been effective, with vehicle purchase 
tax exemptions, for example, increasing EV market share by 
2.1 percent and purchase subsidies increasing EV market share 
by 1.5 percent (Table 2) in leading Chinese cities (Ma et al. 
2017). Li et al. (2020), who assessed national and municipal 
policies across 43 different Chinese cities, including Shenzhen 
and Beijing, also found that incentives like purchase subsidies 
and driving restriction exemptions are effective because they 
disincentivize ICE vehicles and help consumers overcome 
EVs’ high costs.
The United States has offered purchase incentives at the federal 
and state levels to encourage EV adoption. The federal EV 
purchase tax credit, ranging from $2,500 to $7,500 depending 
on the vehicle’s battery capacity, has been available to EV buyers 
since 2009. The Inflation Reduction Act of 2022 (IRA)—which 
includes $370 billion in climate and clean energy investments—
extends the $7,500 federal EV tax credit for 10 years from 2023 
(Silverstein 2022). The IRA also provides a $4,000 tax credit for 
the purchase of a used EV (Silverstein 2022). Jenn et al. (2018) 
find that incentives like tax credits and high-occupancy vehicle 
lane access have produced noticeable EV growth, with financial 
incentives increasing EV purchases by 2.6 percent on average 
per $1,000 incentive.
European countries have also employed a host of purchase 
incentives, such as purchase subsidies and reduced vehicle 
taxes. Norway, the Netherlands, and Sweden are among the top 
performers (Hall et al. 2017). Norway, which now has over a 50 
percent EV market share, implements several effective policies, 
including a CO2-differentiated vehicle registration tax (Norwe-
gian Electric Vehicle Association).

Vietnam’s government recently announced a new EV registra-
tion fee waiver policy in March 2022 (VietNamNet 2022; 
Apolat Legal 2022) and different sources have highlighted 
the need for strong incentives to enable the transition toward 
EVs during the early stages of EV adoption (Oh et al. 2019; 
Le and Yang 2022; World Bank 2022). As policymakers in 
Vietnam and HCMC assess various strategies to increase EV 
adoption, they can learn from actions taken by model cities and 
countries. Direct financial incentives to EV buyers, which can 
be in the form of purchase subsidies or tax credits, are some 
of the best-known incentives provided by cities and national 
governments. These types of incentives address consumers’ cost 
concerns through price reduction, partial reimbursement, or tax 
deduction, which make EVs more cost-competitive with ICE 
vehicles. Research has shown that purchase subsidies are more 
effective mechanisms than tax credits because consumers are less 
motivated by the possibility of future savings than by incentives 
they receive immediately (Narassimhan and Johnson 2018; 
DeShazo et al. 2017). 
Figure 3 presents examples of EV purchase incentives imple-
mented in different markets. The literature highlights that 
countries’ existing policies at the national and city level, particu-
larly purchase subsidies and tax exemptions, can increase EV 
adoption, but HCMC will need to tailor its e-mobility strategy 
based on its own market conditions to complement national 
policies. HCMC officials must carefully evaluate the local 
vehicle market and decide which incentives overcome the main 
barriers to EV uptake in the city. 
In addition to assessing policy effectiveness and local market 
conditions, HCMC policymakers need to ensure that the incen-
tive is within the city’s regulatory powers. The local government 
might have limited authority to introduce new regulations for 
specific incentives, like tax exemptions or purchase subsidies. 
Understanding the legal framework is therefore crucial to 
designing a set of incentives properly suited to the context. 
Nonetheless, local policy needs the support of higher govern-
ment levels, and the linkage between national and local policy 
is a key consideration. In many of the cases analyzed, national 
policy has provided financial support to subnational govern-
ments to develop demand-based incentives (Hall et al. 2019).
Supporting the transition toward EVs in Vietnam will require a 
combination of financial incentives, regulations, and public and 
private investments (World Bank 2022). In addition to evaluat-
ing the pros and cons of different EV incentives, policymakers 
must assess the level of budget required as they design EV 
incentives, plan investments, and evaluate their potential costs 
and benefits (Oh et al. 2019). 
China’s central government, for example, has spent about $45 
billion since 2009 on its EV purchase subsidy program (China 
Briefing 2022). Sheldon and Dua (2020) evaluated these 
subsidies’ cost-effectiveness to inform future policymaking and 
suggest potential improvements. Using data on new vehicle sales 
between 2016 and 2017, it calculated the subsidy program’s cost 
to be $4.3 billion, and the cost of subsidizing an additional EV 
to be about $24,500.

Over 68 percent of HCMC’s two-wheelers are nearing their

consumer EV demand. City officials can consider implementing 
one of the many forms of useful and effective purchasing incen-

or subsidies used to reduce the sale price of EVs, purchase tax 
exemptions implemented during the purchase or after to reduce 
overall taxes paid, postpurchase rebates and income tax credits, 
and emissions taxes to disincentivize the use of certain fuels 
(Hardman et al. 2019). 
Evidence from leading EV markets highlights that studying 
demand preferences is an important step in understanding how 
future consumption patterns might change. To better under-

income elasticity of demand for vehicles using available vehicle 
registration data. Elasticity of demand refers to the sensitivity of 
the quantity demanded for a certain good to a change in income

increasing disposable income may increase demand for cars, 
for two reasons:
 ▪ Demand for cars is elastic, as changes in quantity demand 
due to a change in GDP per capita are large across time. 
In contrast, the quantity of two-wheelers demanded is less 
responsive to changes in GDP per capita. 
 ▪ These findings suggest that making EVs more affordable 
to a broad range of income groups will increase the market 
diffusion of such vehicles.

To make EVs more attractive, we identified three general 
categories of costs to consider in an economic cost-benefit 
(CBA) analysis. These are related to different types of financial 
incentives, considering the current market price of fuel-pow-
ered cars and EVs. 
 ▪ Electric car purchase bonus: Cost associated with providing 
a car bonus to make the upfront cost of an EV more 
affordable. This is $1,400 (33 million VND)—the average 
price difference between an ICE car and EV sold on the 
Vietnamese market. 
 ▪ Electric car registration fee rebate: Cost associated with 
establishing a fee rebate for a car user to register the EV. 
This is $500 (12 million VND), based on Vietnam’s EV 
registration fee waiver policy announced in March 2022, 
which outlines a registration fee of 0 percent for EVs for 
three years from 2022 and a registration fee for EVs that 
is 50 percent lower than the fee for ICE vehicles (set at 
10 percent) for the next two years (VietNamNet 2022; 
Apolat Legal 2022). 
 ▪ Electric two-wheeler registration fee rebate: Cost 
associated with establishing a fee rebate for a two-wheeler 
user to register the EV. This is $30 (694,000 VND), based 
on existing registration fee shares, which range from 2 to 5 
percent for ICE two-wheelers (Le and Yang 2022). 
Charging infrastructure policies and deployment are key compo-
nents of the transition toward EVs (Boehm et al. 2021; Hall et 
al. 2019). We provide some initial estimates of charging infra-
structure costs (capital and operation cost for EV chargers) in 
Appendix A, but assessing charging infrastructure deployment 
in detail is beyond the scope of this paper. We assume the source 
of investments for the estimated charging infrastructure costs 
to be exogeneous (either the government provides these invest-
ments or the private sector covers these investments through 
regulation). Appendix A presents detailed methodology for 
the CBA analysis.

financial incentives across two 
potential scenarios
Due to data limitations, we focused on financial data, following 
the World Bank’s recommendation about the importance of 
estimating the potential public finance budget available to lead 
a sectoral transition (World Bank 2022). Specifically, we explore 
existing estimates of the HCMC budget for public transport. 
The aim was to identify a proxy for a budget considered finan-
cially and institutionally feasible for HCMC, based on actual 
financial resources used to support a transportation system in 
the city. Another motivation was to adopt a predictable country-
based budget rather than quantifying financial resources based 
on benchmarks coming from other countries.
Therefore, we adopt a budget that HCMC used to subsidize bus 
services in 2019 as a benchmark for the CBA. To provide more 
evidence, we take into account two different shares of HCMC’s 
2019 budget on an annual basis, designing two scenarios (Table 
5). Specifically, a quarter of this budget is applied to design 
the “low” scenario, and more than half (70 percent) is applied 
to design the “high” scenario, following WRI evidence from 
research in the electric mobility field (Kanuri et al. 2021). 
Through this approach, we aim to reflect how benefits and costs 
may change with the government’s financial capacity to provide 
EV incentives.

Assigning monetary value to benefits
The economic benefits of adopting EVs may vary across regions 
depending on the type of incentive and other contextual factors. 
We consider both the individual benefits and social benefits 
correlated to supportive local policies. 
 ▪ Individual benefits: Financial incentives provided by the 
government may reduce the purchase cost, generating a 
savings in upfront cost. In addition, the adoption of EVs 
may result in lower operating costs like electricity costs. This 
could free up resources that may be channeled into the local 
economy for purchasing other goods and services. 
 ▪ Social benefits:2 Reducing GHG emissions generates 
societal benefits associated with reducing the incidence 
or severity of climate change disasters and improving 
public health. 
The calculation of individual benefits takes into account the 
current costs of a fuel-powered car and the savings generated by 
available financial bonuses and the lower operating costs of an 
EV. The estimate of individual benefits is based on the difference 
between using a fuel-powered car and two-wheeler, on the one 
hand, and an electric car and two-wheeler, on the other. The cal-
culation considers both the savings in purchase costs as well as 
fuel and electricity consumption costs. To calculate the total cost 
savings, we estimate the reduction generated by the financial 
incentives and the difference in fuel and electricity costs between 
fuel-powered and electric cars and two-wheelers. To calculate 
differences in annual fuel and electricity costs, we assume that, 
on average, cars and two-wheelers travel 5,000 kilometers per 
year (km/year) and 3,600 km/year, respectively, based on 2020 
data provided by the HCMC Department of Transportation.
Due to limited data, our calculation of social benefits uses an 
international benchmark from the U.S. Environmental Protec-
tion Agency. We use the social cost of carbon as a benchmark of 
the monetized damages associated with an incremental increase 
in carbon emissions in a year. It includes, but is not limited to, 
changes in net agricultural productivity, human health, property 
damages from increased flood risk, and the value of ecosystem 
services that result from climate change. For this analysis, we 
used the U.S. government’s latest estimate of the global social 
cost of carbon, $53/tCO2 (US$ 2022) (Interagency Working 
Group on Social Cost of Greenhouse Gases 2021), following 
the CBA guidelines recommended by Gray et al. (2015).

ANALYSIS RESULTS
The CBA evaluates the incremental costs and benefits of an 
“incentivizing policy” under the two scenarios. Our analysis 
leads to the following findings:
Under the low scenario, an average annual budget of more than 
$13 million (around 275 billion VND) could subsidize 11,000 
new electric cars per year and 130,000 new electric two-wheelers 
through purchase and registration rebates. The annual budget 
estimated under the high scenario ($33 million, or around 770 
billion VND) could provide financial incentives for 30,000 new 
electric cars and 370,000 new electric two-wheelers. 
 ▪ Over eight years, $107 million could support the adoption 
of 96,000 new electric cars and more than 1 million new 
electric two-wheelers under the low scenario. Taking into 
account the same period under the high scenario, a total 
budget of around $300 million could spur the adoption of 
270,000 new electric cars and 3.6 million new electric two-
wheelers in HCMC (see Figure 5).
Subtracting present value benefits from present value of costs 
over an eight-year period using a 2 percent and 6 percent dis-
count rate, our analysis reveals the following key results:
 ▪ Discounted at 2 percent, NPV is about $98 million (low 
scenario) and more than $275 million in the high scenario 
(Figure 6). Discounted at 6 percent, the NPV is $68 million 
(low scenario) and $192 million (high scenario). To put it 
another way, the benefits-costs ratio is estimated at 1.86 
(discounted at 2 percent) and 1.69 (discounted at 6 percent). 
 ▪ Each EV may generate around $76/year in net benefits 
(1.7 million VND) and $65/year in net benefits (1.4 
million VND) using a discount rate of 2 percent and 6 
percent, respectively. 
 ▪ Individual benefits are a consistent share of the total benefits. 
This represents the savings in purchase and fuel costs for 
users adopting an EV under the assumption of incentives 
provided. Each new user adopting an EV who receives a 
purchase bonus could save $150 (3.4 million VND) per year. 
In total, the cost savings are $1,200 (28 million VND) over 
eight years. For two-wheeler users, the cost savings associated 
with individual benefits are $197/year (4.5 million VND). 
Over eight years, the savings for each electric two-wheeler 
user are approximately $1,623 (38 million VND).

CONCLUSIONS
Vietnam’s government has officially announced a new EV 
registration fee waiver policy that establishes a registration fee 
of 0 percent for EVs for three years from March 2022 and a 
registration fee for EVs that is 50 percent lower than the fees 
for ICE vehicles (which are set at 10 percent) for the next two 
years (VietNamNet 2022; Apolat Legal 2022). HCMC has the 
opportunity to evaluate how different policies can bolster EV 
adoption in the city. Through a demand-side approach, HCMC 
officials can increase EV demand by providing the necessary 
incentives to deter the use of ICE vehicles and increase the pur-
chase or use of EVs. In addition, the results from policy action 
may supply HCMC and national officials with valuable EV 
adoption data, which can be used to develop realistic scenarios 
and approximations of the benefits and costs of required infra-
structure investments to maintain long-term EV demand.
This analysis explains how different leading EV markets have 
implemented financial incentives to address existing market 
barriers for EVs and increase consumer EV demand in the early 
stages of adoption. Additionally, this analysis evaluates potential 
costs and benefits of implementing different EV incentives to 
encourage EV sales in HCMC, under assumptions that reflect 
the city’s market conditions and budget. 
Our analysis suggests that subsidizing the purchase and reg-
istration of EVs could be a successful strategy in HCMC as 
estimated benefits are greater than the costs. We find that net 
benefits are more than $98 million (for the low scenario) and 
more than $275 million (for the high scenario) over eight years. 
Social benefits generated from GHG emissions mitigation begin 
at $1.8 million and may reach $15 million by 2030 on average in 
the low scenario. In the high scenario, social benefits generated 
by emissions reductions may reach $41 million in 2030. 
Designing effective financial incentives and estimating a feasible 
public budget is the first step in increasing the adoption of EVs, 
according to evidence from international cities (ICCT 2020).

the EV market matures. 
To address these further questions, a cost-benefit analysis could 
be revisited with operational inputs from pilot and testing 
projects across the city to help make more suitable financing 
decisions. This next step will need strong collaboration and 
partnership between transport, energy, and urban planning 
authorities; research institutions; and the private sector. Future 
cost-benefit assessments should also evaluate the distribution 
of costs and benefits among different socioeconomic groups 
to inform decision-making and ensure equitable access to the 
benefits EVs can offer.

Gas emissions 
As the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change recommends 
in its 2006 IPCC Guidelines for National Greenhouse Gas Inventories  
(IPCC 2006), “if distance travelled data are available, it is good 
practice to estimate fuel use from the distance travelled data.”
Based on the approach suggested by Song’s (2017) Transport 
Emissions and Social Cost Assessment: Methodology Guide , we use 
the following equation: 
Energy demand = ∑i,j [Vehiclei,j × Activityi,j × FEi,j]  
Emissions = Energy demandi,j × EFi,j,k  
ESC = ∑i,kEmissionsi,j,k×SCk 
Where: 
ESC (US$) = emissions social cost (of transport);   
Emissions (ton) = emissions amount by weight (tons of CO 2);  
SC (US$/ton) = social cost factor of energy use estimated from 
the activity data;  
EF (g/km) = emissions factor;  
Vehicle = number of vehicles of type i  on fuel j;   
Activity = annual activity performed (distance traveled) per vehicle of 
type i on fuel j;  
FE (l/100km) = fuel efficiency: average fuel consumption per unit of 
activity performed by vehicles of type i  on fuel j;  
i = vehicle or vessel type (cars and two-wheelers);   
j = fuel type (Petro 95-III and diesel); and  
k = emissions type (CO 2).

Sensitivity analysis 
Due to the complexity of the research question and data-collection 
constraints, a range of cost and benefit data was collected, where 
possible, to create lower-bound, average, and upper-bound estimates 
to cover the range of uncertainties associated with the data and 
valuation methods. 
 ▪ A lower bound assumes low benefits and high costs.
 ▪ An upper bound assumes high benefits and low costs.
These assumptions have been compared with the results provided in 
the main body of this working paper (Table A5). 
The lower bound takes into account benchmarks from case studies 
(Hall et al. 2019). Specifically, we consider higher cost for providing 
car purchase bonus (following the examples in Chinese cities, we 
used a purchase bonus of $5,100, or 118 million VND) and 100 percent 
exemption from registration fees for the other vehicle, as evident from 
practices in China, Europe, and Japan (Hall et al. 2019). 
For the upper-bound estimate, a higher social cost of carbon value of 
$159/tCO2 is used, based on the 95th percentile upper-bound social 
cost of carbon value recommended by the U.S. Interagency study.
The sensitivity analysis underscores two important points: 
1. There are no standard rules to support the growth of the EV 
market. This means that if a policy has been a good practice in 
one country or city, this might be not applicable in other contexts, 
such as Vietnam. Replication of practices from leading countries 
may be not the right solution. This is amply shown by the lower-
bound scenario, where we used a benchmark from Chinese cities. 
Therefore, policymakers should shape EV development policy on 
consideration of financial feasibility. 
2. Social benefits from gas emissions mitigation may vary widely. 
This depends on the parameter used ($/tCO 2). Nonetheless, it is 
clear that EV adoption may generate multiple benefits, which may 
exceed the costs in the long term.

ABOUT WRI 
World Resources Institute is a global research organization that 
turns big ideas into action at the nexus of environment, economic 
opportunity, and human well-being. 
Our challenge
Natural resources are at the foundation of economic opportunity and 
human well-being. But today, we are depleting Earth’s resources at 
rates that are not sustainable, endangering economies and people’s 
lives. People depend on clean water, fertile land, healthy forests, 
and a stable climate. Livable cities and clean energy are essential 
for a sustainable planet. We must address these urgent, global 
challenges this decade.
Our vision
We envision an equitable and prosperous planet driven by the wise 
management of natural resources. We aspire to create a world where 
the actions of government, business, and communities combine to 
eliminate poverty and sustain the natural environment for all people.
Our approach
COUNT IT
We start with data. We conduct independent research and draw on 
the latest technology to develop new insights and recommendations. 
Our rigorous analysis identifies risks, unveils opportunities, and 
informs smart strategies. We focus our efforts on influential 
and emerging economies where the future of sustainability 
will be determined.
CHANGE IT
We use our research to influence government policies, business 
strategies, and civil society action. We test projects with communities, 
companies, and government agencies to build a strong evidence 
base. Then, we work with partners to deliver change on the ground 
that alleviates poverty and strengthens society. We hold ourselves 
accountable to ensure our outcomes will be bold and enduring.
SCALE IT
We don’t think small. Once tested, we work with partners to adopt 
and expand our efforts regionally and globally. We engage with 
decision-makers to carry out our ideas and elevate our impact. We 
measure success through government and business actions that 
improve people’s lives and sustain a healthy environment.
