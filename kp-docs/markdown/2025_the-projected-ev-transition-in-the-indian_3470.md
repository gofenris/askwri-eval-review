Highlights
▪ India’s electric vehicle (EV) uptake accelerated from 0.6 percent of
total vehicles in 2019 to 7.6 percent in 2024, presenting opportunities 
for India’s automotive industry to diversify production as both internal 
combustion engine vehicles (ICEV) and EV markets continue to grow.
▪ India targets 30 percent EV uptake by 2030, and the International Energy
Agency’s projections estimate about 50 percent EV uptake by 2035. This
projected transition could create longer-term adaptational challenges
for India’s automotive sector due to significant differences between ICEV
and EV component manufacturing.
▪ This paper uses 58 key informant interviews to investigate the transition
perceptions of, and strategies used by, Indian automotive enterprises.
▪ Perceptions of the projected transition vary. Automotive component
manufacturers (ACMs) making ICEV-specific components, especially
micro, small, and medium enterprises (MSMEs), foresee losses, whereas
original equipment manufacturers (OEMs) and ACMs manufacturing
technology-agnostic or EV components perceive gains.
▪ Enterprise-level strategies include product diversification, research and
development, capacity-building, changes in manufacturing processes,
and workforce training through industry–academia partnerships and
certification programs.
▪ Whereas larger enterprises such as OEMs are equipped to handle the
projected transition, MSMEs lack the required capital and expertise.
▪ An equitable long-term EV transition requires technological support,
access to transition finance, and targeted workforce development.

About this paper 
To better understand the implications of a projected EV 
transition for enterprises of differing sizes and at different 
tiers of the supply chain in India’s automotive industry, this 
paper asks the following questions:
 ▪ How do Indian automotive manufacturing enterprises 
(OEMs and ACMs) perceive the challenges and 
opportunities emerging from the projected EV transition?
 ▪ What strategies are these enterprises formulating to 
navigate the projected transition?
It aims to answer these questions through a series of 58 
semi-structured key informant interviews (KIIs), conducted 
across the automotive manufacturing value chain, with OEMs 
and ACMs of different sizes (large and medium-sized Tier 1 
enterprises as well as Tier 2 and Tier 3 enterprises, which are 
mostly MSMEs) that cater to different vehicle segments and 
component categories (Figure ES-1). 
Executive summary 
Context
EV adoption has increased considerably in India: 0.6 
percent of vehicles sold in 2019 were EVs; by 2024, this 
increased to approximately 7.6 percent (Vahan Dashboard 
2025). India targets a 30 percent share for EVs in 2030 (PIB 
2023), and EV sales across all modes are expected to reach 
about 50 percent in the Stated Policies Scenario (STEPS) and 
exceed 65 percent in the Announced Pledges Scenarios (APS) 
by 2035 (IEA 2024).
Should these projections materialize, major changes will 
be required across India’s automotive industry given the 
differences in manufacturing processes for ICEVs and 
EVs, especially the powertrain (i.e., the set of components 
that generate power to thrust the vehicle into motion). 
Moreover, EVs only require a seventh of the components 
required in ICEVs, which may pose a challenge for Indian 
ICEV component manufacturers, many of whom are MSMEs 
(Gupta et al. 2023b).
India is the third-largest automotive market in the world, 
and the automotive sector contributes approximately 6 
percent of the country’s gross domestic product (GDP) 
(PIB 2025a). This sector is also unique in that a large 
proportion of its automotive component manufacturing 
is carried out by MSMEs, with around 35,000 to 45,000 
MSMEs producing over 2,000 components for ICEVs, 
accounting for 75 percent of enterprises in the ACM industry. 
These MSMEs tend to have limited access to formal sources 
of financing or credit, restricting their capacity to execute 
longer-term plans for their business and invest in technology 
upgrades or workforce skilling. This limits their ability to 
adapt to dynamic and rapidly evolving external transitions 
(Gupta et al. 2023b).
Currently, both ICEV and EV sales continue to grow in 
India (Vahan Dashboard 2025). However, if India’s EV 
transition accelerates as projected, the automotive industry 
will need to also adapt to EV manufacturing, which presents 
opportunities as well as challenges. India’s automotive

▪ Perceptions of the projected EV transition vary based
on the ability of enterprises to diversify into EV (and
EV component) manufacturing, with well-capitalized
enterprises viewing the transition more positively. ICEV
and EV OEMs perceive the transition as an opportunity to
expand their markets, especially given their rich in-house
research and development (R&D) capabilities. However, 
MSMEs involved in manufacturing ICEV powertrain
components view the transition negatively due to their
lack of access to financial and technical resources and/or
market awareness to revamp their existing manufacturing
processes. However, not all MSMEs share this view: those
manufacturing powertrain-agnostic components, such as
suspension and steering systems, electricals and electronics, 
and EV powertrain components, think that the projected
transition will likely impact their businesses positively. 
▪ A projected transition to EVs will see the demand
for certain ICEV manufacturing processes and skills
decline, and others may continue to remain relevant. The
demand for some ICEV-related manufacturing processes
is likely to persist, whereas the demand for forging and
die-casting processes will likely decrease. EV-specific
testing and manufacturing processes such as battery
pack assembly, electric motor manufacturing, electronics
manufacturing, soldering, and automated optical inspection
will likely increase the demand for highly skilled technical
workers and decrease the demand for skillsets related to
ICEV powertrain assembly, testing, and design. 
▪ Thus, strategies by enterprises (OEMs and ACMs)
to navigate the projected transition include specific
measures to address manufacturing and skills changes
along with broader strategies. These enterprises are
diversifying their product mix to take advantage of the
current demand for both ICEVs and EVs, which also
allows them to remain relevant when a transition to EVs
occurs. Enterprises such as EV start-ups and new entrants
from other sectors are also building in-house research, 
design, and development (RD&D) capacities. Changes in
manufacturing processes are also being employed, as in the
case of gear finishing or stator winding. 
▪ Multiple strategies are being used to enhance the
skills of automotive workers to help them adapt to EV
manufacturing. To cater to the demand for emerging
skillsets due to a potential transition from high-labor–low-
skills to low-labor–high-skills, legacy automotive OEMs
have set up in-house skilling institutes to train their
workforce. Automotive MSMEs are also partnering with
skilling institutes and academia to skill their workers via
mandatory on-the-job training (OJT), enrolling them in
Dojo centers and skilling institutes such as the Automotive
Skill Development Corporation (ASDC) and Sector
Skill Councils (SSCs) for EV-specific skill certifications. 
Although the responses from our KIIs did not reveal 
perceptions of imminent job losses due to a potential 
transition to low-labor–high-skills manufacturing, this 
aspect requires further study but is outside the scope 
of this paper. 
Key recommendations 
Based on our research, the following measures may be 
considered by policymakers, industry associations, and 
enterprises to facilitate an equitable projected EV transition 
among automotive enterprises: 
▪ Support market development for MSMEs in EV
component manufacturing by helping them access
potential market opportunities and transition pathways.
▪ Promote business opportunities in manufacturing of EVs
and EV components through tailored financing support
for different kinds of enterprises, such as:
▪ Dedicated funds to help ICEV MSMEs upgrade their
technology and infrastructure
▪ R&D funds for EV start-ups and enterprises
transitioning from non-automotive sectors
▪ Upskill and reskill workers, especially workers of
traditional automotive MSMEs, on broad and basic as well
as specialized transition-oriented skillsets to enable them
to transition to EV manufacturing. 
Introduction
Background
India’s transport sector is the country’s third-largest energy 
consumer (Paladugula et al. 2018), with demand forecasted to 
increase exponentially (Kamboj et al. 2022). To reduce India’s 
dependence on oil imports as well as emissions from the 
sector—road transport contributes approximately 90 percent 
of Indian transport emissions (Kamboj et al. 2022)—the 
union government has set an ambitious rapid road transport 
electrification target (PIB 2021): achieving a 30 percent sales 
share for EVs by 2030. These targets have been supported 
by considerable investments: multiple national-level EV 
promotion schemes
1 have received consolidated outlays of 
approximately Rs. 67,333 crores (approximately US$7.8 
billion). Twenty-eight Indian states have supplemented 
these EV promotion measures with their own policies, 
schemes, and subsidies.
These measures have resulted in significant increases in EV 
uptake across the country, especially since the pandemic, from 
0.6 percent of total vehicle sales in 2019 to 7.6 percent in 
2024 (Vahan Dashboard 2025) (Figure 1). Sales are predicted

Although this projected EV transition, when accompanied 
by grid decarbonization, can effectively reduce transport 
sector emissions (IPCC 2022), these major differences in 
manufacturing can disproportionately impact certain types 
of enterprises in the industry. For example, micro, small, and 
medium enterprises (MSMEs), which comprise 75 percent 
of enterprises in the automotive component manufacturing 
(ACM) industry, often lack the capital and technology 
to transition to newer manufacturing processes, making 
this transition potentially inequitable for them. Thus, it 
becomes essential to not leave major segments of the existing 
automotive (manufacturing) industry, such as MSMEs 
behind, especially given the significant labor implications. 
As the following subsection shows, India’s automotive 
manufacturing industry encompasses firms of varying scale, 
complexity, technology, and access to capital. Their abilities to 
pivot to EV manufacturing are likely to vary as well.
Thus, this paper provides early-stage research on the 
preparedness of India’s automotive manufacturing industry 
for the projected EV transition. We aim to understand how 
the potential transition is perceived by different segments 
(and sizes) of this sector, how it is perceived as impacting 
manufacturing and allied processes, and the strategies being 
deployed or considered to mitigate (or take advantage 
of ) its impacts. Based on these findings, we provide a few 
recommendations on how the projected EV transition can be 
made more inclusive for the existing automotive value chain.
The Indian automotive industry
India’s automotive industry is the world’s third largest 
(Saxena 2024). The industry is a major contributor to India’s 
economy, accounting for about 35 percent of the country’s 
manufacturing GDP (Invest India n.d.). The total turnover 
of the industry from sales of vehicles and automotive 
components is about 20 lakh crore (~$240 billion) (PIB 
2025a), of which exports account for 2.9 lakh crore (~$35 
billion). The industry is diverse, comprising large OEMs and 
ACMs of different sizes. 
The Indian automotive value chain is complex and multi-
tiered (Riat 2019), as described in Figure 3. The industry is 
classified into different tiers, where Tier 3 suppliers, which 
constitute the “earliest” stage of manufacturing, are small 
or micro enterprises providing raw materials, child parts, 
or process-based
2 services (such as surface coating, heat 
treatment, etc.) to Tier 2 suppliers or sometimes directly to 
Tier 1 suppliers and OEMs. Tier 2 suppliers are next in the 
supply chain. They represent an early-intermediate stage of 
small component assemblies and are MSMEs mostly involved 
in the manufacturing of child parts and small assemblies that 
are supplied mostly to Tier 1 suppliers. Tier 1 suppliers are 
medium-size or large enterprises that usually prepare the final 
component assemblies, which they supply to OEMs, which 
assemble the vehicle. Thus, Tier 3 represents the “earliest” 
stage of manufacturing (sourcing raw materials or child 
parts), Tier 2 represents an early-intermediate stage of small 
component assemblies, Tier 1 represents the final component 
assemblies, and OEMs represent the final vehicle assembly.

A unique aspect of India’s automotive industry is the ubiquity 
of MSMEs, which constitute approximately 75 percent of 
enterprises in the ACM industry. This industry generated 
a turnover of Rs. 6.14 lakh crore (~$74.1 billion) from auto 
component sales to OEMs, the export market, and the 
aftermarket in FY2024 (PIB 2025a). Approximately 35,000 
to 45,000 MSMEs currently manufacture over 2,000 ICEV 
parts that are supplied both domestically and abroad. These 
MSMEs tend to have limited access to formal sources of 
financing or credit, restricting their capacity to execute 
longer-term plans for their business and invest in technology 
upgrades or workforce skilling. This limits their ability to 
adapt to dynamic and rapidly evolving external transitions 
(Gupta et al. 2023b). Because India’s automotive MSMEs 
generate significant employment in the automotive sector, 
studying the challenges they may face, especially those 
related to transition-related know-how, workforce skilling, 
and technological and financial capabilities, assumes great 
importance in any research on transitions in the industry 
(Gupta et al. 2023a; Gupta et al. 2023b; CRISIL 2024; 
iFOREST 2024). 
The projected EV transition: Challenges 
and opportunities for the Indian ACM 
segment
The Indian automotive market is poised for sustained 
expansion over the coming decades. India has at least 300 
million vehicles on the road, and another 200 million are 
forecasted to be added in the next two decades (IEA 2022). 
Under the IEA’s STEPS model, half the vehicles sold are 
expected to be EVs by 2035, and under its APS model, EV 
sales in India are expected to scale up to over 65 percent 
by 2035 (IEA 2024). Although ICEVs will constitute the 
majority of the vehicles on the roads in the short run, the 
growth in EV sales is expected to gradually overtake the 
growth of ICEV sales.
Structurally, this projected growth in EV sales in India differs 
from that in mature vehicle markets such as the EU and 
the United States, where the overall market size is stagnant. 
In contrast, in India, both the ICEV and EV segments are 
growing simultaneously. The US and EU markets are seeing a 
rapid growth in EV penetration. By 2035, EV sales across all 
modes (excluding two- and three-wheelers) are expected to 
cross 70 percent in the United States and 85–90 percent in the 
EU (IEA 2024). 
This projected growth in domestic and foreign EV markets 
signals a need to create ecosystem-level planned transition 
pathways for OEMs and ACMs to help them adapt to 
changing market demands and leverage them for net gains. 
The higher growth rates of EV sales in India will also require 
localizing the automotive supply chain to sustain India’s 
position and presence in the global market (Dash 2023). 
Currently, the ICEV supply chain in India is mainly domestic, 
with over 70 percent of auto components sourced locally for 
all vehicle segments (Dhawan et al. 2018). In contrast, about 
70 percent of EV components are currently imported from 
other countries, including China and Taiwan. The projected 
transition presents challenges associated with the heavy 
reliance on imports such as price disparities or tariff changes, 
making it difficult to source materials reliably at stable 
prices while also increasing the vulnerability of the domestic 
automotive market to geopolitical risks (Moneycontrol 
2021). Hence, the projected EV transition presents a business 
opportunity for the Indian ACM industry to design and 
develop high-quality EV components for the domestic market 
(Balu et al. 2022). This opportunity can be fully utilized only if 
the localization potential of the Indian automotive value chain 
is realized through necessary infrastructure upgrades and 
skills development. 
Although certain chassis components and some control units 
are largely manufactured domestically, key high-tech EV 
components such as battery cells, electronic control units, and 
battery management systems (BMS) are still predominantly 
sourced from international markets. However, there is a 
constant push from the government toward building local 
capacities. By FY2028, the Indian auto industry aims to raise 
investments worth Rs. 5.97 lakh crore (~$7 billion) to boost 
the localization of advanced components such as electric 
motors and automatic transmissions by reducing imports and 
leveraging the “China Plus One” trend (PIB 2025a). Thus, if 
India aims to maintain its strong position in the ACM market 
globally, localizing EV component manufacturing would help 
meet not only domestic but also export market demand and 
ensure a smooth EV transition for automotive enterprises 
of all sizes, catering to the domestic, export, and aftermarket 
supplies (Chaturvedi et al. 2022). 
Research questions
Research is available on EV transitions and their market and 
job impacts, both globally and in India. These publications 
explore the implications of the transition in terms of regional 
or cluster-level disparities (Agora Verkehrswende 2022; 
iFOREST 2024), segments of the supply chain that are most 
likely to be disrupted (Saha et al. 2024; Bansal et al. 2024; 
iFOREST 2024), and the type and quantity of jobs that 
might be lost or gained (BlueGreen Alliance 2018; Strategy& 
and CLEPA 2021; Agora Verkehrswende 2022; KPMG and 
ASDC 2022; Celasun et al. 2023; Saha et al. 2023). However, 
given the unique nature of the Indian automotive industry—
that is, its significant dependence on MSMEs, which 
constitute a large share of the ACMs in the industry—a 
focused assessment of the transition perceptions and response 
strategies of automotive enterprises is needed. This assessment

is essential to understand how the Indian automotive industry 
can exploit the opportunities from the projected EV transition 
and ensure that the most vulnerable stakeholders among 
them—the MSMEs—are not left behind in the process. To 
this end, this working paper is a qualitative exploratory study 
aimed at answering the following research questions:
▪ How do Indian automotive manufacturing enterprises
perceive the challenges and opportunities emerging from
the projected EV transition?
▪ What strategies are these enterprises formulating to
navigate the projected transition?
This paper examines the projected EV transition at the level 
of the enterprise, not individual workers. We do this because 
existing research in the Indian context (iFOREST 2024) 
has already examined the labor implications of the projected 
transition; that is, the potential increases or decreases in 
labor requirements due to the projected transition to EV 
manufacturing. However, enterprise-level impacts are less well 
understood. Moreover, whereas other research focuses on the 
growth of the EV ecosystem and its overarching effects from 
a consumer standpoint (Diouf 2024), this paper examines 
supply-side perspectives (of automotive enterprises) to 
understand the adaptational challenges and opportunities in 
the expanding EV market.
The paper is structured as follows. First, it describes the 
research context, followed by a description of the research 
methodology. Subsequent sections present our findings on 
the perceived challenges and opportunities associated with 
the projected EV transition as well as the enterprise-level 
strategies being formulated to navigate it. The paper concludes 
by offering specific suggestions for enabling an equitable and 
inclusive transition for EV and EV component manufacturing 
within the Indian automotive industry. 
Research methodology
To answer this paper’s research questions, we use primary 
data collected through 58 semi-structured key informant 
interview (KIIs) with owners, C-suite executives, and senior 
leaders of OEMs and ACMs of different sizes, from the major 
automotive clusters in India. 
Study location
To capture a representative sample of perspectives, this 
working paper adopts a pan-India approach that covers India’s 
major auto industry clusters. The Indian automotive industry 
is spread across four primary clusters: Delhi–Gurgaon–
Faridabad in the north, Mumbai–Pune–Nashik–Aurangabad 
in the west, Chennai–Bengaluru–Hosur in the south, and 
Jamshedpur in the east (KPMG and Ministry of MSME 
2020). Because enterprises are concentrated in northern, 
southern, and western clusters,
3 the KIIs covered these three 
clusters. Specifically, in this study, KIIs were conducted 
with enterprises in Delhi NCR–Haryana–Uttar Pradesh 
(Cluster 1– North), Rajasthan and Maharashtra (Cluster 
2–West), and Karnataka–Tamil Nadu (Cluster 3–South), as 
shown in Figure 4. 
Sampling strategy and sample distribution
To identify respondents, we used a purposive sampling 
strategy, which relies on researchers’ judgment in choosing 
participants based on a set of characteristics (Etikan et al. 
2016). Specifically, we used a maximum variation sampling 
strategy (Suri 2011) to ensure sample diversity in terms 
of location, size of operations, and vehicle and component 
categories. We also employed snowball sampling through

Data collection and analysis
The study uses qualitative data collected through KIIs with 
58 respondents from across the above-mentioned enterprise 
categories, all of whom are C-suite executives, proprietors, or 
senior staff members of their enterprises. Figure 8 highlights 
the thematic focus of the KIIs. 
A combination of 50 in-person and 8 virtual KIIs were 
conducted over a period of six months ( June–November 
2023). These qualitative data were analyzed using thematic 
coding (Saldana 2015) to identify common patterns of 
enterprises’ transition perceptions and response strategies. 
Broad themes were identified from the KIIs’ focus areas, and 
the responses pertaining to these themes were compared to 
derive trends and inferences. Details on enterprise size and 
the type of product manufactured were also used to develop 
enterprise-category-wise transition narratives.
Limitations
Because the study uses a non-probabilistic purposive 
sampling strategy and is based on KIIs conducted in specific 
geographical clusters, the findings cannot be generalized 
across all enterprises and automotive clusters in India. The 
findings are also not applicable to the perceived transition 
opportunities and challenges of downstream stakeholders. 
Further, because we received responses from only around 
50 percent of the respondents we reached out to (n = 116), 
participation bias may have impacted our findings. Some 
large enterprises were unable to participate in this study due 
to multiple internal protocols and lengthy approval processes 
for data sharing. Similarly, a few start-ups did not participate

Enterprise-level strategies 
to adapt to the projected EV 
transition: Evolving manufacturing 
technology and changing

The accelerating sales of EVs in India is leading to the 
evolution of automotive manufacturing technologies 
and processes (Bansal et al. 2024; Saha et al. 2024). As 
discussed above, automotive enterprises perceive a shift in 
the in-demand automotive manufacturing processes and 
workforce skillsets. This section presents the findings along 
two lines: the strategies being deployed by enterprises to 
adapt to the evolving manufacturing technology landscape 
and the evolving workforce development needs in light of the 
projected EV transition. 
Evolving manufacturing technology and its 
implications
Broadly, the following trends are evident with respect to 
changing automotive manufacturing processes:
 ▪ Some existing ICEV-related manufacturing processes 
may continue to remain relevant with suitable adaptations, 
depending on the new requirements of EV-related 
manufacturing processes. 
 ▪ Certain mechanical processes used to produce ICEV 
powertrain parts are expected to be less relevant over time 
owing to the projected rise in the overall EV market share.
 ▪ The emergence of new manufacturing processes unique 
to the EV powertrain are anticipated to compel EV 
OEMs and ACMs to invest in new technologies and 
infrastructure. 
Thus, automotive enterprises are strategizing to better adapt 
to these demand shifts in the industry. The following sections 
expand on these three identified trends.
Some ICEV manufacturing processes may 
continue to remain relevant, with suitable 
adaptations
ICEV manufacturing processes that are likely to maintain 
or increase their relevance in EV powertrain manufacturing 
include motor winding, coating, and machining. Although 
these processes have been used traditionally, manufacturers 
would have to make minimal to moderate changes to align 
them with new technological requirements. For instance, 
two of the seven respondents from Tier 1 ICEV powertrain 
ACMs in the sample alluded to differences in the winding 
process used in ICEV starter motors and EV motors. 
Although the core winding principle remains unchanged, 
EV motors use a different wound stator mechanism.5 Hence, 
industry experts suggest that such enterprises could build a 
manufacturing line for EV motors and motor parts by making 
only minimal changes to their existing processes. 
The transmission systems of EVs and ICEVs differ, with 
gears having a limited functional role in the former. In EVs, 
fewer gears are required, but they must have higher precision 
and quality than ICEV gears, as highlighted by 5 out of 12 
manufacturers of ICEV transmission components. In terms 
of gear-manufacturing processes, the gear-shaving process 
(required for gear finishing) will need to be replaced by a more 
complex grinding process to achieve higher precision in the 
finished product for EVs. This change will require significant 
investments in infrastructure upgrades, which large Tier 1 
enterprises could make but would pose challenges for MSMEs 
(from Tier I, Tier II, and Tier III). According to respondents 
from three MSMEs and one large Tier 1 enterprise, a shift 
toward manufacturing gears for EV transmission systems 
would require the purchase of unaffordably expensive new 
machinery. Moreover, because EVs require fewer gears, the 
shift could lower business volumes even though it could 
increase the value of transactions. 
Certain ICEV processes are likely to see 
reduced demand
The reduced need for several mechanical components in 
EV powertrains is expected to reduce the use of certain 
mechanical manufacturing processes used for ICEV 
component manufacturing. For instance, respondents from 
ICEV ACMs pointed out that for forging and die-casting—
processes that are widely applied in multiple ICEV powertrain 
components—production capacities are likely to fall because 
EVs require fewer forged and die-casted parts. Moreover, EVs 
use 25–27 percent more aluminum than ICEVs due to vehicle 
lightweighting requirements; therefore, the die-casting process 
may need to be significantly improved (Mining Technology 
2025). This may also have significant implications for raw 
material suppliers, which have largely relied on ferrous metals 
(high-strength steel) for ICEVs and may face challenges in 
addressing the growing need for aluminum, especially given 
India’s heavy reliance on aluminum imports (Satpathy and 
Mohan; Kukreja 2018; ET EnergyWorld 2024). 
These mechanical processes are usually implemented by 
“process-based” MSMEs providing machining, forging, and 
die-casting services to multiple companies across sectors. The

requirements and the associated technology upgrades, as well 
as hand-holding them on design, dimension, and quality

projected transition seamless for the entire supply chain. They 
are also building in-house capacities to manage the complexity 
and quality requirements of the new manufacturing processes 
being employed by them.

and in-house R&D capabilities as key strategies
Two types of new players are entering the EV ACM 
ecosystem. First, some born-EV start-ups present in this 
segment are exclusively manufacturing EV powertrain 
components, such as motors and battery packs. Second, 
manufacturers in other industries that have products

a transition into the automotive industry by building on 
their existing expertise (Table 1). For instance, electronics 
manufacturing services companies see opportunities in the 
extensive use of electronics components in EVs. Similarly, 
pump and motor manufacturers catering to other sectors 
(such as agriculture) are focusing on EV motors and/or their 
components. The increasing need for thermal management 
systems in EVs—to maintain key powertrain components 
at an optimal temperature and efficiency—has also opened 
a business avenue for manufacturers supplying thermal 
management system components to other sectors.

Our interviews show how automotive enterprises are adapting 
to the projected EV transition in the Indian automotive 
industry (Table 2). Key strategies include developing 
in-house R&D capacities, product diversification, market 
expansion, adapting existing product design expertise to 
the EV markets, and developing new suppliers. Whereas 
born-EV start-ups and larger enterprises perceive R&D as an 
organic part of their operations, smaller enterprises are taking 
proactive initiatives to integrate these functions into their 
core operations in preparation for the projected transition. 
Respondents pointed out that this was due to the nascency 
of EV-related technology, lower entry barriers, and rising 
competition. This is a positive response to the projected EV 
transition, given the traditionally limited scope for R&D and 
technology ownership in Indian automotive supply chains. 
Although R&D has increased in ICEV OEMs over the years, 
driven by global competition, ICEV ACMs have mostly been 
dependent on their OEM customers for designs (Saripalle 
2016). However, as shown in this study, the projected EV 
transition is changing that trend by spurring innovation 
across the value chain and across enterprise categories. For 
instance, one of the MSME transmission manufacturers for 
the ICEV segment (as well as the off-highway segment) 
invested over Rs. 10 crores (~$100 million) in infrastructure 
innovation for EV gears.
However, adapting to transitional needs poses different 
challenges for MSMEs and EV start-ups than for OEMs and 
Tier 1 enterprises. This is largely due to the former’s limited 
resources for new infrastructure and technology upgrades. 
To mitigate the risk of losing business, MSMEs that are 
significantly invested in ICEV powertrain components will 
need to proactively adapt to transition-related changes in 
manufacturing processes. For entirely new manufacturing 
processes for EVs, both legacy automotive MSMEs and 
emerging start-ups may find it difficult to establish their 
position in a continuously evolving market. In such a scenario, 
larger industries—that is, OEMs and large Tier I ACMs— 
are expected to pitch in to ensure that investments made by 
MSMEs are protected from the potential risks caused by 
technological evolution in the EV space. 
Changing workforce development needs 
Along with technological changes, the projected EV transition 
is likely to herald a shift from high-labor–low-skill operations 
to low-labor–high-skills operations. Figure 16 shows this 
projected shift in skillset demand. According to the National 
Skills Qualification Framework (NSQF), job levels from 2 
to 4 (see Figure 16) comprise semiskilled roles that primarily 
involve performing repetitive tasks under supervision 
(e.g., operators, technicians, mechanics). Job levels 5 to 6 
comprise jobs requiring a skilled workforce with specialized 
knowledge and the ability to perform complex tasks with 
minimal supervision (e.g., data analysts, master technicians). 
Although job roles expected to become less relevant over 
time are distributed across skill levels, new roles due to the 
EV transition are more concentrated at higher skill levels 
(Ramaswamy et al. 2022).
The respondent EV start-ups and ICEV and EV ACMs 
covered in our study pointed to the same trend of shifting 
skillsets expected across the automotive industry due to 
the projected transition. They prefer a mix of skilled and 
semiskilled workers on the production floor, unlike traditional 
MSMEs, which primarily hire semiskilled workers for 
these roles. Further, unlike their ICEV counterparts, large 
and small-scale EV powertrain manufacturers, including 
battery pack manufacturers, prefer ITI or diploma certificate 
holders for production associate roles. In ICEV enterprises, 
semiskilled workers mainly work as helpers on the production 
floor or are involved in minor low-skill and labor-intensive 
assembly-related functions.

Conclusion and the way forward
This study highlights that different types of automotive 
enterprises in India are perceiving and responding differently 
to the projected EV transition. Figure 21 presents our 
main findings. 
The perception of enterprises regarding the impacts of the 
projected EV transition on their business depends on their 
access to new manufacturing processes and infrastructure, 
their ability to adapt, and their strategies to upskill their 
workforce and invest in newer, more complex technologies. 
Similarly, whether enterprises have the necessary resources 
and funds to impart new EV-specific skills or upgrade 
the ICEV-specific skills of their workforce dictates their 
workforce development strategies.
Lastly, we find that ICEV powertrain ACMs, most of whom 
are MSMEs, perceive the projected transition negatively 
because of their inability to diversify into EV markets. On 
the other hand, due to their rich in-house R&D capabilities, 
ICEV and EV OEMs perceive the potential transition 
positively, because they see it as an opportunity to expand 
their markets.

Based on our analysis of the perceptions of different kinds 
of enterprises toward the projected EV transition and the 
strategies being employed by them, we provide the following 
specific considerations for policymakers, industry associations, 
and enterprises from the automotive manufacturing 
ecosystem to ensure an equitable transition among 
automotive enterprises:
 ▪ Facilitate access to potential market opportunities and 
transition pathways: Targeted interventions are needed 
to help MSMEs adapt to the potential transition and 
give them access to viable diversification pathways. The 
industry stakeholders we interviewed mentioned that 
one way to facilitate this was through existing OEM–
ACM meets for different categories of ACMs based on 
their component specialization, enterprise size, and the 
distribution of their business share between the automotive 
and other sectors. For instance, seller–buyer meets could 
be organized between ICEV powertrain MSMEs and EV 
OEMs or start-ups to give the former an understanding 
of potential EV customers’ new powertrain requirements 
that they could tap into in the future. Similar meets could 
be organized for manufacturers from non-automotive 
sectors that are interested in leveraging their existing 
capabilities for EV component manufacturing. Such 
meets could also be organized between ICEV powertrain 
MSMEs and manufacturers from non-automotive sectors; 
the latter could give the MSMEs an idea about potential 
opportunities in the non-automotive sector, which could 
help them tackle short-term transition risks. These kinds of 
convenings could be facilitated by industry associations.
 ▪ Promote business opportunities in EV and EV 
component manufacturing through tailored financing 
support for different kinds of enterprises: Dedicated 
funds tailored to fulfill the transition needs of different 
stakeholder groups are required. For ICEV MSMEs 
diversifying to EV component manufacturing, a fund for 
upgrading their businesses’ technology and infrastructure 
could enable them to adapt to the new EV powertrain 
requirements of their customers. Similarly, for EV start-
ups and enterprises transitioning from non-automotive 
sectors to e-mobility, a fund could be created specifically 
to foster R&D and technological innovation by helping 
them develop expertise in high-value EV powertrain 
components such as batteries, motors and controllers, 
and power electronics. These funds could be mobilized 
through government schemes, financial institutions, and 
private investment. 
 ▪ Provide transition-oriented upskilling and reskilling 
for inclusive workforce development: Training could be 
provided on broad and basic as well as specialized skillsets 
to upskill and transition the workforce at the bottom of 
the pyramid. This suggestion aligns with findings from 
global studies and may be particularly helpful in reducing 
the vulnerability of ICEV powertrain MSMEs (mostly 
Tier 2 and Tier 3 enterprises), which mostly engage 
semiskilled workers. To that end, academic and skilling 
institutions could work together with industry and 
government on joint capacity-building initiatives to ensure 
the development of courses tailored to industry needs and 
to facilitate provision of training at the manufacturing 
facilities for employed workers. 
This working paper lays the groundwork for understanding 
Indian automotive manufacturing enterprises in order to 
better prepare them for the projected EV transition, but there 
is scope for further study with respect to the mapping of 
cluster-specific transition needs, the socioeconomic challenges 
faced by enterprises and workers, and the need for supply 
chain financing mechanisms among automotive MSMEs.

Glossary
Born-EV: This refers to new companies being launched or new 
products being made to specifically cater to electric vehicles.
Bus Bar Assembling: Bus bars are metal-based flat conductors 
used for grounding and conducting electric current through various 
systems and subsystems in an electric vehicle.
Cell Grouping: It refers to the process of grouping cells into 
modules based on similar properties to improve capacity. 
Cell Sorting: It refers to the process of categorizing individual 
battery cells based on their properties and performance 
characteristics to optimize their efficiency. 
Chassis: It refers to the horizontal frame (skeleton or structure) 
supporting the entire body of the vehicle and onto which all other 
components are mounted. 
Child Parts: It is a term used for smaller parts that are assembled 
to create a bigger essential part of the vehicle. 
Cluster: A cluster is a region where automotive industry 
enterprises are colocated and where inter-enterprise relations exist. 
The colocation of enterprises in the automotive industry generates 
benefits, including sharing of sector-specific inputs, skilled labor 
and knowledge, intra-industry linkages, and opportunities for 
efficient subcontracting.
Die-Casting: It is used to describe processes that utilize metal 
dies, or molds, to produce parts from various metals. 
End-of-Line Testing:  It refers to quality-control tasks executed at 
the end of a production line. The tests usually focus on evaluating 
the correct functioning of the device under test (sometimes under 
varying environmental conditions). 
Forging: It is a process involving the shaping of metal using 
localized compressive forces. The blows are delivered with a 
hammer or a die.
Gear Finishing: It is the process of removing burrs and nicks, 
refining teeth surfaces, and achieving geometric tolerance in gears. 
Gear Shaving:  It is a free-cutting gear-finishing operation that 
removes small amounts of metal from the working surfaces 
of gear teeth. 
Harness Crimping:  It is the process through which electric 
cables or an assembly of wires are connected to electrical and 
electronic components. 
Heat Treatment: It is used to enhance and maintain the structural 
integrity of cars and improve the durability of critical components. 
Highly Skilled Workers: Workers with specific levels of 
proficiency and competence acquired through intensive 
technical or professional training or practical experience for a 
considerable period. 
Laser Welding: It is a form of welding used to join together metals 
or thermoplastics using a laser beam to form a weld. 
Lightweighting:  It is a concept in the automotive industry that 
refers to building less-heavy vehicles in order to achieve better fuel 
efficiency, battery range, acceleration, braking, and handling. 
Machining: It is a process whereby a desired shape or part is 
achieved by the controlled removal of material from a larger piece 
of raw material by cutting. 
Metal Forming:  It is a process where materials are subjected 
to plastic deformation to obtain the required size, shape, and/or 
change in physical and chemical properties. 
Metal Treatment: It is designed to interact with metal surfaces in 
a molecular and chemical process to create a protective buffer on 
the surface of the metal.
Module Compressing: It refers to the process of creating tight, 
compressed, and secure connections in the battery module using 
high temperature and pressure. 
Module Stacking:  It refers to the process of linking or stacking 
batteries in parallel or series to improve the capacity or voltage of 
the battery system. 
PCB Potting: It is a method used to protect circuit boards by filling 
a printed circuit board (PCB) enclosure with a liquid material called 
a potting compound or encapsulation resin. 
Powertrain: It the set of components that generate power to thrust 
the vehicle into motion.
Production Floor:  It refers to the production area in a 
manufacturing unit and includes all the production processes, 
machinery, equipment, and raw materials. 
Resistance Welding:  It is a cost-effective method of joining 
metals by conducting a strong current and applying pressure to 
melt the metals at the localized points. 
Semiskilled Workers:  These are workers with skills gained 
through experience on the job and capable of applying it under 
the supervision or guidance of a skilled employee. They may also 
supervise unskilled workers.

About WRI India
WRI India, an independent charity legally registered as the India 
Resources Trust, provides objective information and practical 
proposals to foster environmentally sound and socially equitable 
development. Our work focuses on building sustainable and liveable 
cities and working towards a low carbon economy. Through research, 
analysis, and recommendations, WRI India puts ideas into action to 
build transformative solutions to protect the earth, promote livelihoods, 
and enhance human well-being. We are inspired by and associated 
with World Resources Institute (WRI), a global research organization. 
Know more: www.wri-india.org 
Our challenge  
Natural resources are at the foundation of economic opportunity and 
human well-being. But today, we are depleting Earth’s resources at 
rates that are not sustainable, endangering economies and people’s 
lives. People depend on clean water, fertile land, healthy forests, 
and a stable climate. Livable cities and clean energy are essential 
for a sustainable planet. We must address these urgent, global 
challenges this decade.
Our vision  
We envision an equitable and prosperous planet driven by the wise 
management of natural resources. We aspire to create a world 
where the actions of government, business, and communities 
combine to eliminate poverty and sustain the natural environment 
for all people. 
Our approach  
COUNT IT  
We start with data. We conduct independent research and draw on 
the latest technology to develop new insights and recommendations. 
Our rigorous analysis identifies risks, unveils opportunities, and informs 
smart strategies. We focus our efforts on influential and emerging 
economies where the future of sustainability will be determined. 
CHANGE IT  
We use our research to inform government policies, business 
strategies, and civil society action. We test projects with communities, 
companies, and government agencies to build a strong evidence 
base. Then, we work with partners to deliver change on the ground 
that alleviates poverty and strengthens society. We hold ourselves 
accountable to ensure our outcomes will be bold and enduring.
SCALE IT  
We don’t think small. Once tested, we work with partners to adopt and 
expand our efforts regionally and globally. We engage with decision-
makers to carry out our ideas and elevate our impact. We measure 
success through government and business actions that improve 
people’s lives and sustain a healthy environment.
