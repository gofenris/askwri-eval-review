Highlights 
 ▪ Transit-Oriented Development (TOD) strategies 
can improve connectivity between housing and 
employment centers, green spaces, and community 
facilities by supporting both local environmental 
sustainability objectives, through more efficient use 
of land, and “complete” streets, by creating safer 
infrastructure for pedestrians and bicyclists, leading to 
potential modal shifts. 
 ▪ Enabling conditions for TOD may include changes in 
land-use regulation and other statutory provisions 
to create an integrated regulatory framework.

institutional coordination during planning and 
implementation.

include Land Value Capture, a strategy based on 
expected increases in land values due to infrastructure 
investments. These mechanisms have been used to 
generate revenues such as Tax Increment Financing 
(TIF) and a wide range of taxes and fees.
 ▪ Cities should be aware of the potential negative 
impacts of TOD, such as displacement and 
gentrification. Effective and inclusive TOD strategies 
should be accompanied by land management 
strategies that mitigate these impacts, including 
having policies in place to ensure the preservation and 
expansion of affordable housing.

TOD. It is neither designed to be a comprehensive guide to TOD imple -

information to help city officials navigate some of the major barriers often 
encountered with TOD.

Context
Over the last several decades, cities have increasingly 
prioritized Single Occupancy Vehicles (SOVs), which has 
contributed toward greater sprawl. A sample of spatial 
data from 200 cities across the world showed that between 
1990 and 2015, the buildup of urban areas increased at a 
rate faster than population growth. In the Global South, 
where urban populations have increased the fastest, urban 
areas increased by a factor of 3.5 (Angel et al. 2016). 
This trend shows that growth in many cities has gener-
ated sprawl, rather than “smart growth” that encourages 
transit, walkability, and multimodal connectivity. At the 
same time, it is estimated that between 2005 and 2050, an 
additional 2.3 billion vehicles will be added in emerging 
markets (Suzuki et al. 2013).   
City sprawl is directly linked to automobile usage and, 
by extension, energy consumption and greenhouse gas 
(GHG) emissions. In many cities in developing coun-
tries, the rising rates of motorization and poor existing 
infrastructure creates traffic congestion that is reducing 
economic growth (Rajé et al. 2018; Lall et al. 2017). As 
cities grow, additional infrastructure, such as sewage and 
water systems, must be expanded to serve new develop-
ments. A 2003 study conducted in the United States 
concluded that the cost per capita of the provision of 
public services declines with density and increases with 
sprawl (Carruthers and Ulfarsson 2003), although density 
should be assessed on a case-by-case basis in relation to 
infrastructure support capacity. TOD strategies are an 
opportunity for cities to achieve economies of scale and 
manage growth by promoting walkability and greater 
mixed-use development.

Question 2: What are the planning principles and steps 
for TOD implementation?
Spatial planning strategies conceptualize how transit lines 
can connect with station areas to encourage TOD. There 
are different types of spatial models: urban core refers to 
high densities and more mixed land uses, urban residen-
tial refers to higher population densities than suburban 
nodes but fewer land uses than urban core, and, finally, 
suburban residential refers to areas with low population 
densities but designed to improve accessibility to urban 
centers. The planning process often includes a stock take 
or amendment of enabling policies and institutional coor-
dination, market analysis, stakeholder (public and private) 
engagement, site selection, detailed project design, identi-
fication of financing, land assembly, and construction and 
operation. There are several factors that should be consid-
ered when selecting and designing a TOD, such as infra-
structure characteristics, ease of implementation, land use 
constraints, cost of site preparation, political constraints, 
social equity, and technical and environmental risks. 
Question 3: What are common design principles and 
strategies for TOD?
Common design principles of TOD projects focus on 
accessibility, livability, safety, and connectivity. TOD proj-
ects are designed with an emphasis on residents who walk 
and bike, and should also be integrated with other modes 
of transport. In addition, planners consider zoning tools 
that encourage mixed-use and compact development. For 
instance, housing is to be built up, rather than out, and 
the ground floor zoned to attract commercial businesses in 
order to encourage economic activity and vibrancy within 
the TOD corridor. Planning for public spaces can increase 
social capital around TOD corridors by providing univer-
sally accessible spaces that offer green spaces and promote 
social exchange.
Question 4: What are some of the ways to assemble 
land for TOD?
Some land assembly tools include land pooling, Com-
munity Land Trusts, public land grants or write-downs, 
and eminent domain. These strategies use various legal 
instruments to amalgamate land parcels for TOD corri-
dors. Utilizing these financial and legal tools to encourage 
TOD, such as tax incentives, subsidies, or land grants, 
can promote public services such as green spaces and 
street furniture.
Question 5: What are possible funding and financing 
mechanisms for TOD?
As TOD investments are often complex, involving a range 
of different types of infrastructure and service compo-
nents, public funding typically cannot cover the entire 
expense of the project. They have often been funded and 
financed through a combination of sources, including 
betterment levies, property taxes, Tax Increment Financ-
ing (TIF), air development rights, and special assessment 
districts. In some cases, financing for a transit investment 
underlying the TOD district can come from fare box rev-
enues and other user fees; however, this amount generally 
only covers the cost of operation and maintenance. 
Question 6: What are some Land Value Capture tools 
used to finance TOD?
Land Value Capture (LVC) can potentially support financ-
ing TOD projects. The value of land can be affected by 
its use, building potential, and development in the sur-
rounding area. Government entities can utilize LVC tools 
through changes in regulation, public investments, or joint 
developments. Regulatory tools range from changes in 
zoning to selling rights to increase the building capacity of 
a land parcel. Using tax measures or charging fees through 
land banking are common ways government entities can 
raise funds using LVC.
Question 7: What incentives exist to encourage private 
sector development? 
Throughout the investment life cycle of a TOD, both local 
and national governments use incentives and subsidies to 
encourage private investments that meet the public goals 
set out at the start of the project. Incentives can come 
in many forms, including grants, zoning changes, fiscal 
incentives, regulatory measures, programs, and collabora-
tive activities. 
Question 8: What are possible legal and institutional 
structures for TOD?
A range of delivery models and contractual tools have 
been utilized globally in the implementation of TOD 
investments. Projects may be wholly or partly owned and 
operated by the public sector, the private sector, a non-
profit organization, or a hybrid combination. Real estate is 
an inherently political process and requires coordination 
and communication between developers, funders, stake-

Question 9: How can cities better manage effects  
of gentrification? 
Gentrification is the result of new development that 
increases income inequality and displaces people of lower 
socioeconomic status as property values rise. The main 
strategies to mitigate the negative effects of gentrification 
are related to maintaining the existing housing supply 
along with increasing the stock of affordable units. Some 
of the ways to do this include inclusionary zoning, which 
requires a certain percentage of new units to be tied to 
income levels, as well as a range of flexible financing 
mechanisms, such as structured funds for affordable hous-
ing land acquisition, subsidies to affordable housing, and 
subsidies to borrowers.  
Question 10: How can cities measure expected 
outcomes and impacts of TOD?
Available metrics focus on how well the TOD has achieved 
a walkable, high-density, mixed-use, and mixed-income 
development with high transport connectivity with the 
rest of the city. The framework and performance metrics 
related to the overall impact of TOD projects are also 
linked to indicators connected to financial, social, environ-
mental, and economic impacts. Surveys and assessments, 
to better understand community opinion, are some of the 
tools that can be utilized to measure impacts of the TOD. 
The TOD Standard and TOD Index are two examples of 
monitoring and evaluation tools cities have used to mea-
sure the impact of developments. 
INTRODUCTION
Although the concept of Transit-Oriented Development 
(TOD) has no universally accepted definition, it can be 
thought of as a process of leveraging mass transit to coor-
dinate public and private investment in reasonable prox-
imity to a transit facility, to provide “moderate and high 
density housing, along with complementary public uses, 
jobs, retail and services” (Calthorpe 1993). In this man-
ner, TOD is a planning strategy intended to encourage a 
more mixed-use, high-density, mixed-income, and pedes-
trian and bike-friendly urban development organized 
around transit stations (Sarmiento et al. 2014; Higgins 
and Kanaroglou 2017). It optimizes land use and trans-
portation linkages to create more economically dynamic 
neighborhoods that connect people to core services, 
employment, and cultural opportunities. TOD’s potential 
beneficial impacts include improved quality of life and 
economically thriving, inclusive, and equitable1 neighbor-
hoods that produce lower carbon emissions (Carlton and 
Fleissig 2014; Lane 2017; Dittmar et al. 2004). 
There is a social dimension to TOD that helps create more 
livable, inclusive, and mixed-income neighborhoods, in 
turn creating better cities. Promoting mixed-use develop-
ment with higher density zoning can create opportuni-
ties for developers to build mixed-income housing. An 
inclusive TOD approach proposes that TOD should 
provide benefits to the entire community, independent 
of income level (Sarmiento et al. 2014). Mixed land use 
enhances economic activity by diversifying the types of 
goods and services readily accessible within a community. 
When added to mixed-income development, or a hous-
ing development with differing levels of affordability 
for various income groups, it can help promote diverse 
labor pools, inclusion, social integration, and innovation 
(Greenberg and Belzer 2008). TOD can be used by cities 
as demonstration projects to improve their image, sharpen 
their identity, and advance urban redevelopment efforts 
(Amirtahmasebi et al. 2016).
METHODOLOGY
To comprehend current trends in TOD, a literature review 
was conducted that focused on previously applied TOD 
frameworks and financing mechanisms, national and local 
master plan documents, the on-the-ground experience 
of the authors, and real-world examples. Additionally, 
26 case studies from more than 50 cataloged examples 
around the world were selected and analyzed. The selec-
tion was based on geographic distribution and an in-depth 
review in consultation with experts. The following steps 
were taken to select and develop the case studies: 
 ▪ An exploratory mapping exercise of the universe 
of prominent TOD projects on the ground, not just 
master plans, was conducted. 
 ▪ A subset of city examples based on expert consultation 
and multi-criteria weighting, including geographical 
representation, financing mechanisms, institutional 
arrangements, and technical components, 
was shortlisted. 
 ▪ An assessment was conducted using a common 
research framework developed by World Resources 
Institute (WRI) (“business model framework”). It 
focused on four dimensions:

Q1: WHAT ARE THE BENEFITS AND COSTS OF 
TOD INVESTMENTS?
Q1.1 Benefits
The benefits of TOD include creating a range of economic 
opportunities and transport options; preserving pedes-
trian, green, and open spaces; creating complete streets; 
providing community facilities; improving quality of life; 
and encouraging sustainable economic development. 
Overall, TOD can create a sense of permanence through 
visual recognition and visitor appeal that spurs develop-
ment (Salat and Ollivier 2017). More specifically, TOD 
can lead to several direct and indirect benefits, such as 
the following: 
 ▪ Promoting increased density around transit 
stations, which can reduce automotive traffic and 
carbon emissions. This also increases ridership 
and revenues for transit providers, decreasing the 
need for public subsidies for transit (Sung and Oh 
2011; Alberti 1999; Ingvardson and Nielsen 2018; 
Kamruzzaman et al. 2015). 
 ▪ Directing new development to infill sites, thereby 
limiting urban expansion and conserving open public 
spaces (Cervero and Sullivan 2011). 
 ▪ Providing the impetus for urban renewal, particularly 
in areas with unplanned high densities, which enables 
incremental augmentation of essential infrastructure, 
community facilities, and open spaces. 
 ▪ Providing pedestrian-friendly conditions and bicycling 
infrastructure, resulting in a decrease in traffic 
fatalities (Ewing et al. 2003). 
 ▪ Generating overall positive health outcomes 
through cleaner air, decreased congestion, and 
increased physical activity (Durand et al. 2011; 
Brown et al. 2015). 
 ▪ Providing a financing mechanism through increased 
property tax and sales tax revenues over time as well 
as through the establishment of development fees 
(Bartholomew and Ewing 2011).
The level of investments and public benefits achieved will 
vary depending on project objectives. However, there 
are several qualities that are consistent among the TOD 
projects considered as best practices. TOD aims to create 
higher-density and mixed-use development, and concen-
trate infrastructure needs in smaller spaces, leading to 
economies of scale for the provision and management of

basic infrastructure and services, such as energy, water, 
and waste, compared to suburban sprawl (Thompson

Although TOD can increase land values, which benefits 
municipal governments by creating an additional revenue

societal cost related to gentrification. 
Q1.2 Capital, Operational, and Social Costs
The three main capital costs associated with TOD are 
transit infrastructure, street infrastructure and public 
space, and real estate construction and housing (World 
Resources Institute and World Bank Group 2018). Transit 
infrastructure can include costs related to a new metro 
rail, light rail, or bus rapid transit (BRT) line. Typically, 
this is the largest investment needed and is usually 
covered by the public sector. Street infrastructure and 
public space includes changes in the local street networks 
and associated sidewalks, bicycle lanes, and other public 
spaces. These capital investments are either covered by the 
public sector or by developers in cases where the market 
is strong and the government can place more demands on 
private companies. In addition, costs related to real estate 
construction and housing refer to new buildings which 
form the core of a TOD area (Adams 2003). These costs 
are typically borne by the private sector. The government 
may decide to subsidize these costs through tax incen-
tives, low-interest loans, and grants if developer interest 
and market conditions are not strong enough to catalyze 
private investment. Other costs also arise, such as expen-
ditures to cover operation and maintenance of transit 
facilities and surrounding infrastructure. 
Cities can work in partnership with the private sector 
to create the appropriate framework so that part of the 
capital cost of the TOD investment can potentially be 
recouped. Although partnerships with real estate devel-
opers and infrastructure concessionaires can provide an 
alternative source of financing, one of the criticisms of 
TOD has been that the public investment and related zon-
ing changes have granted private parties excessive returns. 
A related negative impact has been that TOD investments 
result in higher land value, which effectively reduces the 
stock of affordable housing resulting in the displacement 
of lower to middle-income groups near the transit invest-
ment (Wilson 2018). If not mitigated, this may lead to a 
pattern of unaffordability, gentrification, displacement, 
and worsened inequalities.
Finally, TOD has not always generated the promised ben-
efits. In this manner, there can be opportunity costs from 
not having invested in other infrastructure projects that 
have equal or greater social and environmental impact.

Box 1 | Case Study: Rosslyn-Ballston Corridor, Arlington County, Virginia 
The Rosslyn-Ballston Corridor is in Arlington County, Virginia, across the 
Potomac River from Washington, DC. The area’s proximity to the capital of 
the United States contributed to the high market demand for office space in 
the corridor. This area experienced considerable growth during the 1960s 
when regional planning for a Washington Metropolitan Transit System began 
and Arlington was focused on reviving the existing retail corridor (Arlington 
County Government n.d.). 
County planners and officials saw this growth as an opportunity to reimagine 
communities, reduce car dependency, and revitalize struggling commercial 
areas. Arlington’s new development occurred with limited public subsidies 
beyond the construction of Metro, and despite relatively low-density zoning. 
County officials and residents lobbied for the Metro line to run along an 
existing, although deteriorating, commercial corridor with stations in Rosslyn, 
Clarendon, Courthouse, Virginia Square, and Ballston (Alves 2017; EPA 2014). 
Although in comparison to the originally proposed aboveground Metro line, 
this new underground Metro line was more expensive to build, the county 
wanted to protect single-family neighborhoods, where possible, while also 
boosting the area’s existing retail corridor. The Arlington General Land Use 
Plan outlined the development around these Metro stations, later comple -
mented by site-specific plans, to guide further development adjacent to each 
of the five Metro station areas.
Arlington’s high-density transit corridor is considered both a model of TOD as 
well as an illustration of missed opportunities. On the one hand, the corridor 
has added commercial office space, several federal agencies as well as 
housing for a growing population of workers who commute via public transit 
(Greenberg and Belzer 2008). The overall results include increased land 
values around the transport corridor through the repurposing of previously 
underused industrial areas. In addition, it resulted in an increased tax base, 
increased walkability, more green spaces, and improved livability. On the 
other hand, several apartment complexes along the corridor have gradually 
been converted into high-end apartments or condominiums, increasing the 
cost of living and reducing the overall stock of affordable housing. In addition, 
some historic areas have been converted into modern buildings, reducing the 
previous character of the area.

Q2: WHAT ARE THE PLANNING PRINCIPLES 
AND STEPS FOR TOD IMPLEMENTATION?
Q2.1 Spatial Models 
Spatial planning strategies lay out how transit lines can 
connect with station areas to encourage TOD. In a 2018 
article, Huang et al. identified three main types of TOD 
nodes—urban core, urban residential, and suburban 
residential—each of which serve different roles within the 
overall transit network (Huang et al. 2018):
 ▪ Urban core: High densities and more mixed land 
uses. These nodes will tend to have the highest transit 
and pedestrian traffic.  
 ▪ Urban residential: Higher population densities 
than suburban nodes, but fewer land uses 
than urban cores.
 ▪ Suburban residential: Low population densities 
but designed to improve accessibility to urban cores, 
especially during peak periods. 
Many cities around the world have tried different combi-
nations of TOD strategies. For example, Portland, Oregon, 
has used a mix of streetcars, heavy and light rail, and 
buses to increase densification in the urban core. Simi-
larly, the Mass Transit Railway (MTR) in Hong Kong has 
developed the “Rail & Property” model that focuses on 
development around MTR-owned property within a 500-
meter catchment area to the main station, which is also 
known as a “TOD zone.” This strategy encompasses both 
the urban core and urban residential models. There are 
five types of mixed-use typologies used by MTR within the 
urban core (Nguyen 2020): 
 ▪ High-rise residential with no commercial, typified by 
Tsing Yi Station 
 ▪ Large-scale residential with limited office and 
commercial (Tai Koo Station) 
 ▪ Large-scale mixed use, roughly divided among various 
land uses, including hotels (Kowloon Station)
 ▪ High-rise commercial with some residential and 
retail (Central)
 ▪ Mixed with a range of other uses 
In contrast, Arlington, Virginia, a suburb of Washington, 
DC, has focused on creating urban residential areas that 
effectively serve as new urban cores along with developing 
suburban residential nodes designed to improve access to 
the large commercial and employment areas in the U.S. 
capital (Straatemeier 2013; Arlington County Govern-
ment n.d.). A hybrid model has involved the development 
of “polycentric” nodes, which essentially seek to provide 
system-wide connectivity that leads to economies of scale, 
creates feedback among nodes, and implicitly assumes 
that lower-density suburban areas can grow to become 
urban residential areas or a new urban core over time.
Given varying traffic levels, it is more likely that urban 
core nodes, and possibly urban residential areas with 
high-volume attraction centers, will generate enough reve-
nue to be self-sustaining over time. In contrast, other node 
types, such as suburban residential, are more likely to 
require public subsidies or cross-subsidies to cover capital 
costs for implementation, as well as costs related to opera-
tions and maintenance. These suburban areas also provide 
the chance to create affordable housing opportunities due 
to the availability of cheaper land while providing connec-
tions to the main mass transit corridors and labor markets 
within the urban core. Potential drivers of pedestrian 
traffic and economic activity include the following:
 ▪ Commercial and retail activity. Making TOD 
corridors more vibrant and attractive, for example, 
through the inclusion of cafes, restaurants, and other 
commercial businesses, can enhance the sense of 
community in a neighborhood. Closely connected 
to the idea of active ground floors is the design of 
transition areas between public and private spaces 
which can help set the tone for a neighborhood. Retail 
or other uses that activate the streets or sidewalks are 
usually prioritized in TOD. 
 ▪ Green public spaces. Green public spaces 
encourage mixed-used development by providing 
places for meeting and cultural exchange for all types 
of residents, regardless of social or economic status. 
These open spaces are universally accessible and 
programmed for different income and age groups, 
and activity types. Such spaces can increase the social 
capital of a community and increase the quality 
of life of its residents (Ijla 2012). Safety of public 
spaces can be improved through active facades and 
public lighting.

 ▪ Historic and cultural spaces. This approach 
focuses on preserving and incorporating places of 
historic and cultural importance within the TOD area. 
While it may not always be the most cost-effective 
or highest density use of a space, in many cases, 
governments have incentivized historic preservation 
of buildings within TOD corridors to maintain a 
neighborhood’s character. The preservation of historic 
buildings can often increase property values and 
make an area more attractive for residential land use 
(Renne et al. 2015).
Q2.2 Enabling Factors
To ensure comprehensive implementation of TOD, plan-
ning must be coordinated and integrated at all levels. 
Planning and implementation of TOD will involve various 
levels of governmental authority: from regional agencies 
to city agencies and local urban entities. It is observed that 
despite a fair understanding of the TOD approach, cities 
find on-the-ground implementation challenging due to a 
weak enabling environment. Addressing this situation will 
require integration between tiers of government as well 
as an exhaustive review of the existing national, state, and 
city-level regulatory frameworks. It is important for cities 
to look at regulations for any necessary amendments that 
may be required to ensure that state legislations, develop-
ment plans, zonal plans, local area plans, development 
control regulations, and budgetary allocations enable the 
implementation of TOD. The existing regulations may 
require revisions to incorporate the TOD concept and 
strategies, and might necessitate the formulation of new 
frameworks or policies to facilitate implementation of 
TOD. This will also ensure a coalition across all tiers of 
government entities required to cater to all scales of TOD 
application; that is, at the city/region level, corridor level, 
station area level, and at site level.  
Q2.3 Planning 
Ideally, TOD projects are part of a larger master plan that 
considers the whole transportation and land use network. 
However, TODs often start with smaller plans, and cities 
often debate what is the most appropriate scale to begin a 
TOD (Hale 2014). One model is to develop multiple transit 
corridors with TOD nodes concurrently, while another 
approach is to focus primarily on the development of a 
key station area of an existing or planned transit line. 
Although there is no simple rule of thumb, a key part of 
the planning process is robust due diligence, including 
stakeholder engagement, particularly with the communi-
ties that will be most affected by the development (see 
Figure 1). Planning for TOD can occur at different scales: 
city or regional, corridor, and station area.
 ▪ City-level or regional plans focus on large areas 
that are likely to cross into adjoining districts and 
peri-urban areas. These plans often involve several 
TOD areas, making it possible to consider social and 
economic impacts across the metropolitan area. They 
also facilitate the implementation of urban policies 
needed to bring TOD to the other scales. At the same 
time, planning at a larger scale implies institutional 
coordination, especially if multiple jurisdictions 
are involved, which adds complexity to budgeting, 
planning, financing, and implementation.
 ▪ Corridor plans define land use along a mass 
transit corridor around transit stations. These types 
of plans consider connecting and complementing 
different types of employment drivers and land uses. 
As with regional plans, there may be institutional 
coordination and budgeting issues when corridors cut 
across municipalities. These budgeting issues tend to 
be present in a phased approach with TOD corridor 
planning, and, if not planned meticulously, may risk 
the development opportunity and Land Value Capture 
(LVC) potential of later stage phases. In general, much 
of the investment will likely come from the public 
sector though there may be more consultation and 
input from the private sector. The corridor scale also 
allows for area-based fees and taxes over a wider area 
that can provide significant financing streams.  
 ▪ Station-area plans focus on areas within a 5 to 
10-minute walk (or 500 meters to 1,000 meters) 
from a transit station. At this scale, detailed designs 
should address station accessibility, mixed use, 
multimodal integration, public spaces, and increasing 
density to create a high-quality livable and walkable 
neighborhood. Investment and implementation plans 
are easier to determine during upfront negotiations 
with private developers and existing landowners. 
Publicly owned land within a limited area can be 
used as a pilot to ignite redevelopment and entice 
private investment.

Q2.5 Stakeholder Engagement 
A well-conceived civic engagement strategy can highlight 
potential opportunities and concerns, making it easier to 
gain political buy-in for the project from local communi-
ties early in project development. Failing to communicate 
with key stakeholders and the public can greatly under-
mine a project’s viability by risking a negative reaction to 
development that may cause excessive delays or project 
cancellation. Increased taxes and fees as financing mecha-
nisms for TOD projects can also hinder stakeholder buy-in 
and adoption. Misunderstandings and misconceptions can 
be quite common at the outset of a project, including con-
cerns about impacting cultural areas, Not in My Back Yard 
(NIMBY)-type behavior, and gentrification, especially in 
cities with  affordable housing shortages (Devine-Wright 
2009; Schrenk et al. 2012). 
Q2.6 Site Design and Selection
The next stage in the TOD life cycle is the selection and 
design of intervention sites. Investment locations are 
usually selected based on a range of factors, including 
infrastructure characteristics, ease of implementation, 
land tenure, land use constraints, site preparation costs, 
political considerations, and social equity. Intervention 
sites have different benefits and costs based on the type of 
land use development in question. The main land uses are:
 ▪ Greenfield development, the easiest to deliver, 
converts unused land to urban use by constructing all 
trunk infrastructure, including roads and utilities. Site 
design is open-ended, and selection of a greenfield 
site relies heavily on future development patterns and 
growth projections. Major considerations are the cost 
of adding water, sewage, gas, and electricity lines; how 
these new lines will be financed and operated; and 
whether the opportunity exists to shift these costs to

the private sector. Greenfield development not only 
has the greatest demand risk but must also account for 
the cost of mitigating potential environmental risks to 
existing natural areas and biodiversity.
 ▪ Brownfield or infill development involves 
repurposing existing urban land often from industrial 
to residential mixed use. While brownfield sites 
are considered less risky as development patterns 
may already exist, providing the baseline data for 
financial projections and land tenure negotiation 
can be complex. Brownfield sites often have 
existing infrastructure lines, but there is a cost to 
moving and upgrading existing water, electricity, 
and telecommunication lines without disrupting 
service. Although brownfield sites generally have 
fewer environmental risks, there might be additional 
costs related to cleaning up contaminated areas and 
demolition. Cities are often required to assume these 
costs as the private sector developers are extremely 
reluctant to incur them or take on associated risks. 
Brownfield sites often have great potential to 
transform the economic and social dynamics of station 
areas and a larger submarket. Infill development uses 
land that is either vacant or has a building that can 
be demolished. There may be limited infrastructure 
costs associated with infill projects, but parcels are 
often small and require long permitting processes and 
more complex construction operations to fit within 
the city. Infill is more likely when property values are 
rising, and developers see that they can add value by 
increasing density. 
Q3: WHAT ARE COMMON DESIGN PRINCIPLES 
AND STRATEGIES FOR TOD?  
Approaches to TOD design vary widely. Every case will 
require a slightly different and unique design configu-
ration. Although the designations have changed over 
time within the literature, traditional TOD has focused 
on the “6Ds”— density, diversity, design, destination 
accessibility, distance to transit facilities, and demand 
management (Hebert 2012; Ewing and Cervero 2010). 
These principles tend to drive the complementarity that 
creates greater synergies within a network and urban area. 
Embedded within these principles is the concept of multi-
modal integration, which brings together seamless physi-
cal connectivity and operational integration among modes 
of travel. It not only ensures safe and easy access but also 
reduces travel time and costs while providing multiple 
options. Transit stations should be designed to prioritize 
and support facilities like bicycle parking, intermediate 
public transport parking spaces, feeder buses, and drop-
off spaces, while ensuring barrier-free access (Carrigan 
et al. 2013). While this approach offers many advantages, 
it has sometimes undervalued the benefits of improved 
walkability and connectivity, while often ignoring the 
potentially negative impacts on existing populations who 
are priced out of the TOD impact area. 
Similarly, the “5Ps” approach, which focuses on people 
(number of residents and workers), places (retail and 
commercial areas), physical form (average block size), 
performance (frequency of transit service), and pedes-
trian/bicycle areas (access to sidewalks and low-stress 
bikeways), has also been introduced in some cases. 
Both frameworks include the development of “employ-
ment centers” and “attraction centers,” which can include 
retail districts, social service providers (such as hospitals 
or education facilities), cultural facilities, recreational 
centers, or sporting facilities to drive transit and passen-
ger traffic, albeit at diverse and infrequent time periods 
(Thorne-Lyman et al. 2016). These approaches have the 
common objectives of supporting the creation or intensi-
fication of employment and destination zones, increasing 
urban and transit concentration, increasing mixed-use 
development, and promoting transport multimodality 
while reducing automotive traffic.  
These types of design principles, including how to use 
public spaces and streets, how to manage parking, and 
how to combine different land uses in a TOD neighbor-
hood, are central to the success of TOD. Fundamentally, 
an investment in TOD relies largely on the building blocks 
of a good neighborhood: safe streets, accessible transit, 
green and open spaces, diversified housing choices to 
cater to different income groups, and employment oppor-
tunities. Below is a summary of the main TOD design 
principles, drawing from work developed by the Institute 
of Transportation & Development Policy (ITDP) and WRI 
(ITDP 2017; Sarmiento et al. 2014):
 ▪ Walkability, or neighborhoods that accommodate 
and encourage walking. The pedestrian area is safe, 
complete,3 accessible, active, and vibrant. High-
quality pedestrian access, a key feature of TOD, 
can be designed through factors such as directness 
and connectivity, ease of movement, sign legibility, 
safety, and security.

 ▪ Bikeability, or neighborhoods that accommodate 
and encourage cycling. The cycling area and the 
adjoining network is safe and there is ample space 
for bicycle storage and parking. In particular, secure 
bicycle parking facilities at stations give customers the 
confidence to leave their bicycle at the station during 
the day (Shah et al. 2015). 
 ▪ Connectivity, or a dense network of streets and 
paths. Walking and cycling routes are short, direct, 
varied, and create the right space to compete with 
automobile usage.
 ▪ Accessibility to transit. Developments are located 
near high-quality public transport that is accessible by 
foot; for example, within 0.5 kilometer (km).
 ▪ Fare and operational integration, which 
facilitates switching within and among transit modes 
so that the first and last-mile connectivity and 
payment do not become a bottleneck in the use of 
public transit systems (Hidalgo and Carrigan 2010).
 ▪ Mixed-use development with a range of 
demographic and income groups among residents. 
Mixed-use development within TOD areas can be 
achieved either by horizontal mixing (separating 
activities in separate buildings) or vertical mixing 
(combining uses within the same building) 
(Coupland 1997).
 ▪ Densification, or the optimization of residential and 
employment density which can support high-quality 
transit as well as public services and spaces.
 ▪ Compactness, or the creation of regions with short 
transit commutes with development areas next to or 
near an existing urban area.
 ▪ Parking and circulation restrictions to 
encourage increased nonmotorized mobility 
by regulating parking and automobile use in 
core urban areas.

Q4: WHAT ARE SOME OF THE WAYS TO

Land is the main building block of TOD. It is required for 
undertaking urban expansion, infill development, or rede-
velopment of TOD areas. The availability and size of land 
determines the cost and scale of a project.4 Situations vary 
among cities regarding scale of project, size, ownership, 
type of existing development (i.e., planned or unplanned), 
and environmental conditions of the parcels, presenting 
different kinds of challenges. If there are large parcels, a 
key strategy is consultation with the landowners with large 
holdings in the corridor, especially next to or near station 
areas (Guy and Henneberry 2002). If one of these TODs 
is developed successfully, then it can transform a multi-
block area and spark additional economic growth. Inten-
sive cooperation and coordination among the developer, 
government, local community, and other stakeholders 
helps to ensure a high-quality project with a design that 
matches the overall vision for the area. In cases where the 
public sector is the largest single landowner, it can poten-
tially act as a “master developer” by investing in public 
infrastructure while managing the timing and phasing of 
implementation. The key factors that impact land assem-
bly for TOD include the following: 
 ▪ Development or redevelopment potential, 
which impacts the quantity and size of parcels needed.

Q4.1 Land Pooling or Land Readjustment
Land pooling, also known as land readjustment, assembles 
land parcels from different owners for unified planning 
and servicing. It can be considered an LVC mechanism 
(see Question 6). The idea is to consolidate parcels owned 
by different owners or groups of owners under a legal 
entity that transfers ownership to a land pooling agency, 
which later transfers part of the land back to the original 
owners (Mathews et al. 2018; Jain 2007). The develop-
ment authority prepares a master plan for the given area, 
lays out the infrastructure, and distributes the remain-
ing land back to the landowners while charging them 
a onetime betterment fee for deployed services. Unlike 
eminent domain, there is no land acquisition during the 
land pooling process (Ballaney 2008; Yomralioglu 1993). 
Land pooling facilitates planned development without 
complicated compensation negotiations that require 
large capital investment from the public sector. While 
it reduces the cost of land assembly, it is constrained by 
individual landowners’ willingness to participate. Addi-
tionally, the authority in charge of land pooling has the 
right to subdivide the land, as needed. Land assembly is 
widely practiced around the world in the implementa-
tion of TOD projects. Prominent land pooling projects 
include Town Planning Schemes in Ahmedabad, India 
(see Box 2), land readjustment in Colombia (Vitta 2012), 
land pooling in Australia (Lozano-Gracia et al. 2013), 
the Futako-Tamagawa Station redevelopment project in 
Tokyo (PPIAF 2017), and the Land Readjustment Project 
at Toyama Station in Toyama, Japan (Kidokoro 2019).
Q4.2 Community Land Trusts 
Community Land Trusts (CLTs) provide collective own-
ership of land to ensure its ongoing use for community 
purposes. They are often set up to promote the long-term 
affordability of rental buildings and ownership housing. 
CLTs promote housing affordability by retaining owner-
ship of the land beneath homes and multifamily buildings, 
even after those buildings are sold to income-qualifying 
households or other nonprofits. By retaining land owner-
ship, CLTs permanently remove the price of land from 
the total cost of home ownership, partially mitigating the 
impact of rising land prices (Center for Transit-Oriented 
Development 2009a). CLTs can require that homes are 
sold at affordable prices based on agreed-upon resale 
formulas or rented at affordable levels (Greenstein and 
Sungu-Eryilmaz 2005). There are several prominent mod-
els of CLTs (Democracy Collaborative n.d.), including the 
Champlain Housing Trust in Vermont (formerly the Bur-
lington Community Land Trust), which is the largest CLT

Q4.3 Public Land Dedication or Write-Downs
Local jurisdictions can make public property available to 
developers at reduced prices, particularly if the developer 
provides affordable housing or other public benefits. For 
the developer to benefit from the reduced price of the 
land, it must ensure that a portion of or all the land is 
used for public benefit. Dedicating specific parcels of land, 
or land write-downs, for private developers can be par-
ticularly helpful where rents could otherwise not support 
new development or where the cost of land is otherwise 
prohibitively expensive. Public land dedication or write-
downs can incentivize development to start around a 
specific TOD site (Reconnecting America n.d). However, 
a strong contract that includes the option of penalties is 
critical to ensure that the developer fulfills its obligations.
Q4.4 Eminent Domain 
An example of a public sector land acquisition approach, 
eminent domain grants regulatory power to governments 
or public agencies that can compulsorily take over private 
property for public projects or interests, with compensa-
tion to original landowners, but not necessarily with their 
consent. Eminent domain can help accelerate land assem-
bly, although if not carefully applied, it can also create 
long-lasting social mistrust toward the public sector. The 
proposed project can be paused or delayed if it does not 
meet the requirements for public necessity or interest. 
Contentious eminent domain processes can potentially 
result in judicial reviews, which can delay project develop-
ment and increase costs. Eminent domain authority was 
applied in Nanchang, China, where it was used in com-
bination with upzoning to fund the public transit infra-
structure outlined in the city’s master plan and land use 
plan, highlighting the need for mixed mode integration of 
five new metro lines and integrated bus services (Suzuki 
et al. 2015). Although relevant due to its extensive use in 
the past, eminent domain as a method of land acquisi-
tion is gradually becoming inactive, with fewer use cases 
in recent times.

Q6: WHAT ARE SOME LAND VALUE CAPTURE 
TOOLS USED TO FINANCE TOD?  
LVC mechanisms enable communities to partially or fully 
recover capital investments by capturing part of the value 
increase created by the initial public investment. TOD is 
an appropriate context in which to utilize LVC mecha-
nisms to partially recover the cost of transport and other 
trunk infrastructure investments by capturing the value 
created by subsequent real estate development (Suzuki et 
al. 2013). Table 2 outlines some LVC tools that can be used 
to invest in TOD that are organized by how the revenues 
are captured and created. 
1. LVC based on changes in regulation: 
Instruments that aim to capture land value increases 
generated by changes in urban regulations and 
policies, such as additional or special building rights, 
zoning, and project authorizations.
2. LVC based on public investments: Instruments 
that aim to capture the appreciation generated by past 
or future public investments on infrastructure such 
as transit corridors, sanitation infrastructure, public 
spaces, or other public goods.
3. LVC based on joint developments: Instruments 
that allow public and private sectors to coordinate 
on projects using the land and its value as financial 
components of the arrangement.
Before implementing LVC mechanisms, cities should ana-
lyze and assess the value of land that will be utilized. The 
square-meter price of land is influenced by three factors: 
current use, potential use, and its current and future sur-
roundings, especially economic and employment centers 
and connecting infrastructure (Smolka 2014). These 
factors can be negatively or positively impacted by zon-
ing regulations, such as use limitations, FAR, and build-
ing height restrictions. Changes in regulation and new 
investments in surrounding infrastructure or in economic 
activity can generate positive changes in land value. This 
increase in land value can potentially be captured by the 
public sector to be reinvested in the city.

Q6.2 Betterment Levies and Taxes
Special assessment taxes, a type of betterment levy, are a 
targeted form of taxation applied in a specific and defined 
urban area that will benefit from the infrastructure 
development that supports the construction of new homes 
and commercial space. This mechanism is also sometimes 
called a Business Improvement District (BID) or Local 
Improvement District (LID), depending on local context 
(Howard and Bryant 2015). In this case, existing property 
owners must agree, usually through a public vote, to addi-
tional self-assessment as the public authority is generally 
unable to impose the special assessment unilaterally. The 
tax is applied on the full value of the property and is added 
to existing taxes. A special assessment tax can be used to 
back bonds issued by the public authority.
A successful special assessment tax depends on the fol-
lowing factors: the legal ability of the city to raise property 
taxes and convene private land developers and owners, 
the legal and regulatory frameworks that allow changes 
in property tax rates, a robust fiscal base, and, finally, the 
technical capacity to assess increases in property values 
and ensure adequate updates of land registry. This mecha-
nism has been used widely in the United States and a 
version of it has also been applied in Colombia (see Box 4).

Q7: WHAT INCENTIVES EXIST TO ENCOURAGE 
PRIVATE SECTOR DEVELOPMENT? 
Q7.1 Planning Instruments
Zoning exemptions or requirements are some of the most 
common incentives provided in exchange for meeting 
certain public objectives (Burnier 1992). Zoning incentives 
include greater height or floor-area allowances, such as 
density bonuses; and parking space reductions. Some-
times these incentives are packaged into overlay zones, 
where specific areas qualify in hopes of spurring develop-
ment (Arrington 2005; Atkinson-Palombo 2010). These 
zones also give cities the opportunity to clearly outline 
their public benefit requirements.
Density bonus
Density bonuses are ways for local governments to encour-
age developers to provide public amenities by adopting a 
density incentive zoning program in the TOD area. When 
White Flint, Maryland, upgraded its rail transit station in 
2010, the planning authority adopted a combination of 
value capture mechanisms, including upzoning5 the TOD 
area with a density incentive program, where developers 
could opt to either limit their projects to the standard 
density maximums—0.5 FAR or 10,000 square feet (sq. 
ft) (whichever is greater) and 40 feet (ft) in height—or be 
allowed to build at higher density (Cervero et al. 2004). 
Depending on the area and the level of benefits provided, 
density incentives could go up to 3.5 FAR and 300 ft in 
height (Morris 2002). Projects can receive density incen-
tives for providing public facilities like schools, libraries, 
recreation centers, parks,  public parking, or affordable 
housing beyond the required minimum (Sloan 2010). In 
a booming real estate market, where there is little room 
for affordable developments, linking affordable housing 
production to market-rate development using density 
incentive zoning can be an effective tool if the profit from 
additional density exceeds the extra cost of building the 
affordable housing. 
Reducing parking requirements
The public sector can ease rigid parking requirements, a 
site-specific obstacle, especially when those requirements 
are set inappropriately high within a transit-oriented area. 
In urban areas in the United States, research shows that 
the cost of constructing one parking space is on average 
around $31,000, depending on geographic locations and 
construction type (Shoup 2014). Although parking can 
provide additional private sector revenues, lower parking 
requirements for housing near transit stations provides 
more space for commercial development and increases the 
financial feasibility of affordable housing. 
Q7.2 Fiscal Instruments
Other than investment in public facilities and infrastruc-
ture and through public-private partnerships (PPPs), 
municipalities often have limited financial capacity 
to initiate urban development. However, there are 
several proven fiscal instruments that can attract pri-
vate investment. 
Tax-based incentives
Local governments can facilitate development by reduc-
ing tax liens on projects that provide public benefits. For 
example, local governments can forgive tax liens for devel-
opers that agree on certain site redevelopment require-
ments. This type of forgone revenue can be considered as a 
public investment in the future TOD. Credits, deductions, 
abatements, and exemptions are all different types of 
tax-based incentives for development (Hess and Lombardi 
2004; Cervero et al. 2004; Dittmar et al. 2004).
The Low-Income Housing Tax Credit (LIHTC), a program 
of the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Develop-
ment (HUD), is the dominant funding mechanism for the 
development and rehabilitation of affordable housing in 
the United States. The LIHTC is administered by state and 
local governments, and issues tax credits for the acquisi-
tion, rehabilitation, or construction of rental housing that 
targets lower-income households. It is a dollar-for-dollar 
tax credit for affordable housing investments and provides 
incentives for the utilization of private equity in the devel-
opment of affordable housing for low-income families. 
LIHTC accounts for much of the affordable rental housing 
created in the United States.
Non-tax financial incentives
Local governments can utilize non-tax incentives such 
as loans, tax-exempt financing, and public investment 
programs to encourage private sector investments in 
TOD. Financial assistance may also help mitigate some of 
the risks by having the public sector lead the investment.

Q8: WHAT ARE POSSIBLE LEGAL AND 
INSTITUTIONAL STRUCTURES FOR TOD? 
While all the benefits detailed in Question 1 are desirable, 
they are not always feasible. Thus, TOD projects require 
that the public sector has clearly determined priorities. 
Inevitably, there will be tough choices in the final site 
design and negotiation. International experience has 
shown that prioritizing transit-oriented investments is 
more robust when it involves all stakeholders (Center for 
Transit-Oriented Development 2009b; Hale and Charles 
2007). Among government stakeholders, there needs to be 
representation from all the relevant departments in charge 
of the different aspects of TOD, such as housing, envi-
ronment, transport, and economic development. In the 
best-case scenario, representatives of the private sector, 
civil society, and local resident groups will also be active 
members during project development. Design changes 
and continuous negotiations are common in TOD projects. 
Thus, making a specific public agency accountable for 
meeting a city’s TOD priorities and vision helps to avoid 
undesirable outcomes (Lane 2017).

Successful implementation of TOD strategies requires 
necessary physical infrastructure investments, but also an 
administrative structure that encourages land develop-
ment and investment that is transit-oriented. Planning for 
real estate can be an inherently political process, requir-
ing coordination and communication among developers, 
funders, and residents. These are critical responsibilities 
that a municipal government can fulfill during acquisition, 
design, financing, and public review (Freemark 2019). 
Another important role for the public sector is to properly 
design and enforce regulatory frameworks to encourage 
investment in TOD.

area. Among the benefits of the BID structure is that it 
creates a reliable funding source for supplemental services 
and programs, such as public safety, or marketing of a 
neighborhood brand (U.S. Department of Transportation 
2011). As they are self-managed and funded by the own-

PPPs are long-term contracts between a public sector 
entity and a private sector corporation whereby the private 
sector often finances, constructs, and operates infrastruc-

for payment or the right to collect associated revenues 
for a certain period (NCSL 2017; Suzuki et al. 2015). The 
idea is to transfer part of the commercial and operational 
risks to the private sector, which is better suited to man-

obligations, even if the parent company goes bankrupt. An 
SPV can be created for the sole purpose of overseeing and 
managing the investment.

has the potential for displacement and other impacts

California, was instrumental in first opposing a top-down

incomes rise, which may lead to the displacement of 
lower-income households by middle- and high-income 
households (World Resources Institute and World Bank 
Group 2018). Simply investing in TOD does not automati-
cally mean improved quality of life and livability for all 
residents; in fact, TOD can potentially increase inequality. 
While there is no simple solution, cities have employed the 
following general strategies to address the problem:
 ▪ Maintaining affordability of existing housing 
supply. Maintaining the affordability of existing 
multifamily housing is crucial for keeping housing 
costs affordable for residents and maintaining the 
traditional culture of the neighborhood impacted 
by TOD. As transit improvements are reflected by 
increases in property values, market-rate rentals that 
are currently affordable to low-income households 
may soon become unaffordable if not protected. 
 ▪ Increasing affordable housing supply. When 
new TOD strategies are implemented, simply 
preserving the existing affordable housing will 
not be enough to balance the income mix of the 
new neighborhood. Thus, boosting the inventory 
and pipeline for more affordable housing is 
equally important. 
Some concrete ways to implement these strategies, at both 
the planning and development stages, are outlined in the 
following subsections.

Q10: HOW CAN CITIES MEASURE EXPECTED 
OUTCOMES AND IMPACTS OF TOD? 
The investment cycle does not end with construction but 
continues as buildings lease-up and economic activities 
take hold in the TOD area. It often takes years for a TOD 
site to fully lease, which results in additional admin-
istrative costs. 
There are currently no universally accepted methodolo-
gies for measuring the impacts of TOD. Monitoring and 
evaluation (M&E) systems should be designed to account 
for the three different scales of TOD planning because 
the performance indicators will vary by level. There are 
several performance indicators that can be used as proxies 
to assess the impact of TODs (see Table 5). These perfor-
mance indicators will provide a framework on which to 
base the project’s progress. Possible impact indicators are 
listed in four categories: mobility, social, environmental, 
and economic. Assessing the impact of these indicators 
requires the collection of data from a variety of sources. 
For instance, project success can be measured through 
the collection of community opinions, user surveys, 
and assessments. Feedback on neighborhood projects 
is important at all steps of a project, from planning to 
implementation.

CONCLUSION 
Considering local context, cities around the world should 
be looking at TOD as an urban strategy to provide a 
range of benefits to residents, such as increased and more 
affordable supply of housing, more accessible public 
transport, green and open spaces, streets that cater to 
users of all types, and higher-quality community facili-
ties. Despite potential negative impacts, TOD presents a 
powerful tool to build more compact, mixed-use neighbor-
hoods with high-quality public transport options which 
improve not only accessibility and quality of life in cities, 
but also equity for lower-income groups that may be 
marginalized in traditional models of urban development. 
This publication brought to light the pertinent questions 
and considerations that decision-makers should assess 
before, during, and after a TOD project is put in place, as 
well as during its operation and maintenance. An aware-
ness of these aspects will contribute to successful develop-
ments through informed decisions that relate to planning, 
design, land assembly, finance, private sector incentives, 
and administrative structures. 
Depending on the design and form of implementation, 
TOD can potentially be exclusive, leading to many nega-
tive outcomes, such as the displacement of lower-income 
residents as a consequence of the gentrification of neigh-
borhoods. Inclusive TOD strategies imply thinking about 
these issues upfront, with clear policies and community 
participation and engagement, to ensure that long-term 
residents of neighborhoods are served by the decisions 
made by city leaders, not treated as an afterthought. 
From both the planning and development perspectives, 
there are ways for cities to mitigate negative impacts 
such as displacement. The supply of affordable hous-
ing can be increased through the creation of structured 
funds for land, as well as zoning reforms such as inclu-
sionary zoning. 
As TOD is complex in nature, the financial and owner-
ship structures can take many different forms. Bonds, Tax 
Increment Financing, and special assessment taxes, along 
with the sale of air development rights are some financial 
mechanisms available to cities. In terms of ownership, 
TOD requires close coordination and communication 
among a range of different stakeholders. Public sector 
actors, such as planning and transport authorities, should 
work closely with private developers and service provid-
ers, along with nonprofit actors and community organiza-
tions. This type of participatory planning is crucial in TOD 
to reduce the risks brought on by gentrification. 
This paper is helpful for cities to identify the building 
blocks and bottlenecks of TOD. As the landscape contin-
ues to evolve, there are a range of questions and consid-
erations that would be interesting to explore with further 
research, with the overall objective of digging deeper into 
some of the more pressing and complex challenges that 
cities that have already initiated efforts to implement 
TOD may be facing. Some of these challenges include 
the interaction of potentially contentious strategies such 
as increased density and open space placed against the 
need to reduce parking requirements, the socio-economic 
impacts of land readjustment schemes which may 
threaten to worsen gentrification and inequalities, and the 
importance of ensuring cities are utilizing the standard 
methodologies available to better assess not only the 
economic benefits, but also the social and environmental 
benefits of strategies such as TOD.

WRI Ross Center for Sustainable Cities works to make urban sustainability a 
reality. Global research and on-the-ground experience in Brazil, China, India, 
Mexico, Turkey, and the United States combine to spur action that improves 
the lives of millions of people. 
Based on longstanding global and local experience in urban planning 
and mobility, WRI Sustainable Cities uses proven solutions and action-
oriented tools to increase building and energy efficiency, manage water 
risk, encourage effective governance, and make the fast-growing urban 
environment more resilient to new challenges. 
Aiming to influence 200 cities with unique research and tools, WRI 
Sustainable Cities focuses on a deep cross-sector approach in four 
megacities on two continents, and targeted assistance to 30 more urban 
areas, bringing economic, environmental, and social benefits to people in 
cities around the globe.

ABOUT WRI 
World Resources Institute is a global research organization that turns big 
ideas into action at the nexus of environment, economic opportunity, and 
human well-being. 
Our Challenge
Natural resources are at the foundation of economic opportunity and human 
well-being. But today, we are depleting Earth’s resources at rates that are 
not sustainable, endangering economies and people’s lives. People depend 
on clean water, fertile land, healthy forests, and a stable climate. Livable 
cities and clean energy are essential for a sustainable planet. We must 
address these urgent, global challenges this decade.
Our Vision
We envision an equitable and prosperous planet driven by the wise 
management of natural resources. We aspire to create a world where the 
actions of government, business, and communities combine to eliminate 
poverty and sustain the natural environment for all people.

Our Approach
COUNT IT
We start with data. We conduct independent research and draw on the 
latest technology to develop new insights and recommendations. Our 
rigorous analysis identifies risks, unveils opportunities, and informs smart 
strategies. We focus our efforts on influential and emerging economies 
where the future of sustainability will be determined.
CHANGE IT
We use our research to influence government policies, business strategies, 
and civil society action. We test projects with communities, companies, 
and government agencies to build a strong evidence base. Then, we work 
with partners to deliver change on the ground that alleviates poverty and 
strengthens society. We hold ourselves accountable to ensure our outcomes 
will be bold and enduring.
SCALE IT
We don’t think small. Once tested, we work with partners to adopt and 
expand our efforts regionally and globally. We engage with decision-makers 
to carry out our ideas and elevate our impact. We measure success through 
government and business actions that improve people’s lives and sustain a 
healthy environment.
