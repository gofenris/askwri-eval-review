Executive summary
Introduction and context
School buses carry more than 20 million children over 3 
billion miles annually. Yet nearly 90 percent run on fossil fuels, 
primarily diesel, exposing students—especially those in low-
income communities and communities of color—to harmful 
air pollution and contributing significantly to greenhouse gas 
(GHG) emissions (Lazer et al. 2024; SBF 2024).
ESBs provide numerous benefits: they improve health 
outcomes, reduce climate impacts, lower operating costs over 
time, create good jobs, and even strengthen the electric grid. 
They also support the broader development of a decarbonized 
transportation sector. Driven by federal investments like the 
United States Environmental Protection Agency (US EPA) 
Clean School Bus Program, state-level policies, and strong 
advocacy efforts, ESB adoption has accelerated in recent years. 
Over 14,000 ESBs are committed nationwide, with about 5,000 
already in service. But reaching full fleet electrification will 
require overcoming persistent barriers.
This roadmap offers a stakeholder-informed pathway to 
surmount challenges and achieve 100 percent ESB adoption. 
The intended audience includes policymakers, utilities, school 
districts, operators, original equipment manufacturers (OEMs), 
financing entities, and community advocates who can act 
collectively to accelerate adoption.
About this working paper
This working paper draws on expert knowledge from across 
the school bus ecosystem to define the challenges of and pose 
solutions to reaching full fleet electrification for all school buses 
across the United States. The roadmap draws on insights from
 ▪ the July 2024 “Roadmap to 100” School Bus Electrification 
Summit, hosted by WRI and the Bezos Earth Fund, which 
convened over 200 stakeholders from school districts, 
operators, manufacturers, utilities, financing entities, 
and advocacy groups, along with federal, state, and local 
government agencies;
 ▪ in-depth follow-up interviews with representatives from 
each stakeholder group, conducted by the Center for 
Transportation and the Environment (CTE), capturing real-
world barriers, solutions, and equity considerations; and
 ▪ WRI’s synthesis and peer review process, which analyzed 
common themes, distilled actionable recommendations, 
and ensured the report reflects practical, stakeholder-
informed solutions.
The research is qualitative—grounded in expert experience 
rather than comprehensive empirical data—reflecting current 
market realities and emerging best practices. Where relevant, 
this is complemented by ESB market data and research.
Key findings
The challenges of reaching full fleet electrification
While ESB benefits are clear, current adoption remains 
far from a nationwide transition. Some district fleets 
have, however, made the transition. Primary barriers 
include the following:
 ▪ Higher total cost of ownership (TCO) compared to 
diesel buses, driven by high purchase prices. ESBs cost 
about $270,000 more up front than diesel models, with 
savings partially recouped only over time (Curran 2023). 
Other factors driving up costs include limited competition, 
insufficient incentives, high battery prices, and lack of school 
bus standardization.
 ▪ Complex and costly charging infrastructure deployment, 
often slowed by utility coordination and grid capacity 
constraints. Charging station procurement is often 
fragmented and misaligned with bus delivery timelines. Grid 
upgrades—especially in rural and tribal areas—can be slow 
or costlier than for other districts. Interoperability issues 
between chargers and buses add complexity.
 ▪ Deployment complexities and misperceptions that strain 
already limited capacities and complicate decision-
making for school districts, especially underserved ones 
struggling to navigate financing resources. Technician and 
driver shortages, coupled with inconsistent and inaccessible 
training, hinder fleet readiness. Without standardized 
curricula and inclusive access, districts risk dependency 
on external entities for maintenance. Misconceptions 
about ESB safety, range, cold-weather performance, and 
environmental impacts can further erode community and 
policymaker buy-in.
Systems-level solutions for full fleet electrification
Achieving full electrification of US school bus fleets demands 
system-wide collaboration across manufacturers, utilities, 
policymakers, financiers, advocates, school districts, and 
operators. While each stakeholder plays a distinct role, the 
solutions can be grouped into three interdependent areas:
 ▪ Achieving TCO parity means aligning up-front costs 
with long-term savings from lower fuel and maintenance 
expenses. To accelerate this shift, stakeholders must combine

 ▪ Enabling successful deployment and operations calls for

development. Districts need time to pilot buses, integrate

and high-pollution communities would support equity.

districts on managing energy consumption and maximizing

raise public awareness, dispel misinformation, and ensure 
that environmental justice remains central, and financiers 
should include workforce training in project funding to build 
local capacity. Policymakers should streamline procurement 
rules, fund transition planning resources, and invest in 
training centers to create a strong ESB workforce with 
competitive pay and career pathways.
Recommendations
The next one to three years are pivotal for full fleet 
electrification. The current stage of early adoption, largely 
dependent on incentives, must transition into a more sustainable 
phase in which scale, financing, and operational readiness drive 
progress. Ecosystem actors should focus on the following:
 ▪ Accelerating progress toward TCO parity
 ▪ Policymakers should continue providing strong federal 
and state funding while also shaping market conditions 
that drive down prices through scale, aggregated 
procurement, and standardized specifications.
 ▪ Utilities and regulators must help unlock revenue 
streams, such as vehicle-to-grid (V2G) services, demand 
response, and stationary storage while safeguarding 
battery performance.
 ▪ Financing entities should design ESB-focused products 
that blend public and private capital to bridge up-front 
costs, capitalizing on long-term operating savings.
 ▪ Ensuring infrastructure and utility readiness
 ▪ Utilities and bus operators should prioritize proactive grid 
planning, flexible interconnection programs, and rural 
and tribal equity plans.
 ▪ School districts should optimize charging through 
managed charging strategies and standardize charger 
compatibility to reduce costs and delays.
 ▪ Policymakers and regulators should streamline 
permitting, align program rules, and expand support 
for planning services. Investing in mobile or modular 
charging solutions could also help districts deploy quickly 
while awaiting permanent upgrades.
 ▪ Supporting districts, communities, and workforces 
beyond bus purchase
 ▪ Manufacturers and dealers must provide reliable 
after-sales support, workforce training, and 
procurement assistance.
 ▪ Policymakers should expand training opportunities, 
particularly in underserved and rural areas. Advocates 
and school leaders must share data and stories to build 
public support.

Introduction
This Roadmap to 100 addresses challenges, solutions, and 
recommendations relevant to achieving a fully electric 
fleet of school buses nationwide, with a focus on clarifying 
who within the ecosystem should be responsible for each 
recommended activity.
Methodology
This document is informed by expert opinion gathered at 
the July 2024 “Roadmap to 100” School Bus Electrification 
Summit—hosted by WRI and the Bezos Earth Fund—which 
brought together over 200 private and public industry leaders 
engaged in the transition to electric school buses (ESBs). The 
summit discussions provided a foundational understanding 
of the complex ecosystem surrounding school bus fleet 
electrification and highlighted a variety of challenges and 
potential solutions, as well as equity considerations.
Summit participants included over 200 
representatives, including
 ▪ school districts;
 ▪ school bus contractors;
 ▪ bus, battery, and charger original equipment manufacturers 
(OEMs) and dealers;
 ▪ federal, state, and local officials;
 ▪ utilities;
 ▪ teachers and students;
 ▪ public and private financing institutions;
 ▪ advocacy nonprofits; and
 ▪ charge management companies.
Following the summit, the Center for Transportation and the 
Environment (CTE) conducted a series of in-depth interviews 
with representatives from each stakeholder group to gain further 
insights. These perspectives provided critical qualitative data that 
reflected real-world experiences.
The insights gathered from both the summit discussions and the 
stakeholder interviews (see Appendix A) were interwoven to 
form the foundation of this roadmap, allowing for a synthesis of 
perspectives that revealed common challenges, recurring themes, 
and strategies for school bus fleet electrification. Through 
analysis of the expert opinion data, this document is grounded 
in practical, stakeholder-informed knowledge, ensuring that 
the roadmap reflects both the complexity of the transition 
and opportunities for success with broad buy-in. Not all ideas 
that came up during the summit or follow-up interviews are 
included, as ideas were analyzed and synthesized as part of the 
writing. This document was further reviewed by a select group of 
representatives from across the ecosystem as part of WRI’s peer 
review process for all publications.
Motivation and context
Why electrify
Electrifying the US school bus fleet is a crucial step toward 
improving public health, reducing greenhouse gas (GHG) emis-
sions, reducing school transportation costs, creating high-quality 
jobs, and improving resiliency (Curran 2023). Transitioning to 
ESBs offers a unique opportunity to address existing inequi-
ties while ensuring a cleaner, more sustainable future for all. 
The adoption of ESBs normalizes transportation electrifica-
tion and supports broader electrification across medium- and 
heavy-duty vehicles.
Eliminate health and climate impacts of diesel 
exhaust, especially for children
Medium- and heavy-duty vehicles, such as school buses, 
represent over 10 percent of the on-road fleet but generate 
over half of nitrogen oxides (NOx) and fine particulate matter 
transportation-based air pollution in the United States (BTS 
2023; US EPA 2024a). Long-term exposure to diesel exhaust 
has been linked to respiratory diseases, cardiovascular issues, 
and cognitive impairments, particularly among children, whose 
developing lungs and immune systems make them more 
vulnerable (Beatty and Shimshack 2011; Liu and Grigg 2018). 
Research has shown that children who ride diesel school buses 
experience increased rates of asthma attacks and decreased lung 
function, which can negatively impact school attendance and

academic performance (Austin et al. 2019; Pedde et al. 2023). 
School bus electrification contributes to long-term climate 
goals by reducing GHG emissions from the transportation 
sector, which currently contributes a quarter of GHG emissions 
nationally—the largest direct source of GHG emissions (US 
EPA 2022). On a lifecycle basis, ESBs produce less than half 
the GHG emissions of both diesel- and propane-burning buses 
(Todd and Zepka 2023). The widespread adoption of ESBs, 
coupled with clean electricity sources, can significantly cut 
carbon emissions while ensuring safer, healthier environments 
for students and communities.
Reduce operating and maintenance costs
Transitioning to a 100 percent ESB fleet offers potential for 
long-term economic benefits, though the current up-front costs 
of ESBs may present a more difficult short-term financial case 
for total cost of ownership (TCO). Over time, an ESB can 
deliver over $100,000 in savings on operating and maintenance 
expenses (Curran 2023). With fewer mechanical components, 
no need for oil changes, and reduced brake wear due to 
regenerative braking, ESBs require less maintenance. As the 
technology matures and up-front costs decrease, these savings 
are expected to become even more pronounced, making ESBs a 
viable and cost-effective option in the years ahead.
Drive job growth
Demand for ESBs has spurred new industry growth. As of 
early 2025, there are 21 manufacturing facilities in 13 states 
for ESBs and components, fueling economic growth from the 
“Battery Belt” in Georgia and North Carolina to midwestern 
manufacturing hubs like Michigan and Indiana (Wang et al. 
2024). This industry growth contributes to the growing clean 
energy economy, offering workers a cleaner and safer work 
environment with fewer fossil fuels and no exhaust exposure. 
The battery manufacturing and recycling industry has also 
seen major job growth in the United States. As of February 
2024, one analysis showed that more than 98,000 jobs had 
been announced across the sector, representing $130 billion in 
investments, with the largest investments in Georgia, Michigan, 
and North Carolina (EDF 2024).
Mitigate disproportionate harms of air pollution
Electrifying the school bus fleet provides a critical opportunity 
for mitigating harms of air pollution in communities that most 
need relief. Low-income students, Black students, and children 
with disabilities, who disproportionately rely on diesel-burning 
school buses, are among those most impacted (Wheeler 2009; 
BTS 2021; FHWA 2022). Studies show that communities 
of color experience 61–75 percent more exposure to 
transportation-related air pollution, leading to disproportionate 
health burdens (Pinto de Moura and Reichmuth 2019). A focus 
on equity in the transition ensures that benefits are delivered 
first to the communities most harmed by diesel pollution.
Strengthen grid resiliency
ESBs offer a variety of benefits to vehicle owners and grid 
operators through vehicle-to-grid (V2G) technologies. V2G 
is an energy technology concept in which an electric vehicle 
(EV) is used as a mobile battery and stored energy in the 
vehicle can be discharged. V2G services provide grid operators 
with a flexible, on-demand source of power that can be utilized 
during periods of high demand. Vehicle owners, such as school 
districts and other operators, are then financially compensated 
for the services they provide to the electric grid. In times of 
emergency, there may be power outages, and ESBs can supply 
backup power to schools, shelters, homes, emergency response 
command centers, and other locations as part of a local 
microgrid. V2G technologies also enable more efficient use of 
energy from renewable resources by storing excess renewable 
energy when it is abundant and releasing that energy when it is 
not (WRI 2022).
Background and state of ESBs
With about half a million school buses nationwide driving more 
than 20 million students over 3.16 billion miles per year, school 
buses are among the most visible vehicles in the United States 
(NCES 2024; SBF 2024). Nearly 90 percent of the school buses 
on the road today burn fossil fuels—primarily diesel (Lazer et 
al. 2022). The most polluting buses in the US fleet—those that 
are older and lack modern emissions reduction technology—
are disproportionately concentrated in school districts with 
higher shares of low-income households and residents of color 
(Lazer et al. 2024).
As of December 2024, there are over 14,000 committed ESBs 
in the United States. An ESB is considered committed when 
a school district or fleet operator has been awarded funding to 
purchase it or makes a formal agreement to purchase it from a 
manufacturer. Of those, approximately 5,000 ESBs are already 
transporting nearly 265,000 students across 49 states, Wash-
ington, DC, Puerto Rico, and American Samoa, as well as 21 
American Indian tribes. According to WRI estimations and 
analysis, 7.5 percent of school bus sales in 2024 were electric—
very near the battery electric market share for light-duty vehicles 
of 8 percent (Alliance for Automotive Innovation 2024).

Today, school districts and cities nationwide are driving 
demand, fueled by policy commitments, funding, and grow-
ing manufacturer investment. Seven states have enacted school 
bus electrification transition requirements, with several others 
setting targets through transportation and climate plans, includ-
ing the Advanced Clean Trucks (ACT) rule (see Appendix 
B). At the same time, major federal investments—$5 billion 
overall through the Clean School Bus Program and $381 mil-
lion awarded to school buses through the Clean Heavy-Duty 
Vehicles Program—along with state voucher and pilot programs 
are helping districts offset high up-front bus costs. Demand 
for these programs has been high, with application numbers 
outpacing funding.
Though current state and federal policies face uncertain futures, 
assessing policy impacts is an essential part of planning for full 
fleet electrification. WRI projected annual ESB sales using three 
scenarios: state transition targets only (as shown in the green 
portion of Figure 1); the additional impact of the Advanced 
Clean Trucks (ACT) rule (as shown in the gold portion of

standards. Results show that state policies alone could secure a 
22 percent sales share by 2035. Combining all supportive poli-
cies could increase that to 70 percent (US EPA 2024b, 2024c).

BARRIERS TO ACCESSING FINANCE AND PUBLIC FUNDS
Despite the potential benefits of financing and available funding, 
school districts face the following challenges in accessing the 
necessary resources:
 ▪ Fragmented funding and financing landscape. Districts 
and contractors must navigate a fractured funding landscape. 
Timelines, processes, eligibility requirements, reporting 
requirements, and allowable costs are among the categories 
across which programs can differ. The unpredictability of 
securing funds can make it difficult to plan for long-term 
fleet electrification, budget for up-front costs, or align 
infrastructure upgrades, ultimately discouraging some 
districts from committing to ESB transitions or reducing 
the likelihood that the effort will be prioritized over other 
competing financial obligations. Perceived and real financing 
risks still impede mainstream financial entities from 
providing financial solutions.
 ▪ Limited exposure and access to financing options. There 
is a significant need for more information for districts to 
leverage grant and finance stacking opportunities. Many low-
income and underserved communities are unaware of these 
opportunities or lack the time to navigate the complexities of 
financing arrangements.

A higher total cost of ownership for electric 
school buses
ESBs currently cost about $270,000 more up front than diesel 
models, exceeding their estimated lifetime operational savings of 
approximately $150,000 (Curran 2023). As a result, the TCO is 
roughly $120,000 more without funding support. This makes the 
transition financially challenging for school districts, especially 
those with limited resources.
DRIVERS OF HIGH UP-FRONT COSTS
High up-front costs are hypothesized to stem from limited 
market competition, incentives insufficient for scale and 
resulting price declines, high battery costs, and a lack of 
standardization that limits manufacturing efficiency, as 
detailed below:
 ▪ Limited competition in a complex procurement market. 
Although nearly a dozen manufacturers produce ESBs, 
limited competition was hypothesized to drive up prices. 
Stakeholders emphasized that this issue is compounded 
by the dominant role dealers play in both sales and 
maintenance. Strong OEM-dealer relationships may 
discourage fleets from exploring alternatives or comparing 
prices. The school bus procurement process is highly 
fragmented, with districts buying independently due to 
differing state and local requirements.
 ▪ Insufficient incentives to support scaling manufacturing.

UNDERLEVERAGED SOURCES OF POTENTIAL REVENUE OR 
SAVINGS
Beyond high up-front costs, the TCO gap is also about missed 
opportunities for ESB operators. Below are some of the 
challenges to realizing potential revenue or savings:
 ▪ Battery second life. When ESBs reach the end of their 
operational lives, the accumulated batteries pose an 
environmental challenge. Both battery manufacturers and 
bus operators emphasize the need to develop robust plans for 
battery recycling or repurposing to ensure that the transition 
to ESBs does not create additional environmental burdens. 
EVs are typically expected to have 70- to 80-percent 
battery capacity at the end of their lives, which points to 
the opportunity for significant second-life use (Neubauer 
et al. 2015). By some estimates, this can be up to 7 to 10 
more years. The responsibility for managing these batteries, 
determining second-life applications and valuation, or 
recycling them in an environmentally responsible manner has 
yet to be fully addressed. There are limited data to support 
specific valuations, impeding the flow of finance to address 
this portion of up-front costs.
 ▪ Unmanaged charging and unrealized V2G revenues. Lack 
of knowledge and use of technology solutions to stagger the 
timing of ESB charging has also resulted in large peaks in 
power demand, which can incur high-demand charges from 
the utility. Unmanaged charging can also result in energy 
consumption during peak system demand, when energy is 
the most expensive and often the most polluting and carbon 
intensive. Despite some notable examples, most districts do 
not have the software, hardware, or access to an enabling 
compensation scheme required to earn revenue by providing 
services to the electricity grid. 
The cost and time needed for installing charging 
infrastructure
Transitioning from diesel buses to ESBs requires school districts 
to invest in on-site charging infrastructure. Utilities sometimes 
need to make upgrades to transformers and power lines. These 
two systems are interdependent, demanding close coordination 
among school districts, utilities, and infrastructure providers that 
is time-intensive and adds cost.
UNFAMILIARITY WITH POWER AND CHARGING 
INFRASTRUCTURE 
For school districts and bus operators, the planning, technical 
selection, procurement, and deployment of charging 
infrastructure are often new processes, resulting in the 
following obstacles:
 ▪ Fragmented procurement logistics. School districts are 
largely responsible for procuring charging infrastructure, 
which requires understanding timelines, pricing, long-term 
maintenance, and delineation of roles and responsibilities. 
While school finance departments may be equipped to 
manage grants and funding, most districts have limited 
experience with the technical and logistical complexities 
of the infrastructure required for ESB deployment. 
This leaves school districts susceptible to suboptimal 
procurement agreements. Additionally, delays in service 
upgrades and switchgear delivery are common sources of 
project slowdowns.
 ▪ Oversizing charging infrastructure. State and utility 
electrification planning support can be inconsistent, 
underfunded, and overly technical. One common pitfall is 
that operators select more powerful electric vehicle supply 
equipment (EVSE) than needed, adding cost and requiring 
additional maintenance.
 ▪ Charger incompatibility. There is a persistent challenge with 
interoperability of EVSE and different makes and models 
of ESBs and software. Uncertainty causes planning delays, 
affects operations, and complicates purchasing decisions.
BUILDING OUT DISTRIBUTION INFRASTRUCTURE 
For ESBs to be viable, utilities must ensure that local 
distribution infrastructure can accommodate the increased 
electricity demand. The expansion of grid infrastructure presents 
the following challenges:
 ▪ Time of service installation versus bus arrival. Utilities are 
structured to respond to service requests and meet demand 
as requested. However, bus yards are often not in a utility’s 
existing planning processes. As a result, responding to new 
fleet electrification plans can be time consuming.
 ▪ Costs in tribal, rural, and urban areas. Building additional 
grid infrastructure can be expensive in rural and tribal areas, 
where the existing grid may be underdeveloped. Meanwhile, 
urban areas may have grid systems that are at or near 
capacity, making it difficult to integrate additional loads 
without significant investment.

The transition to ESBs changes how school districts plan, oper-
ate, and support student transportation. ESBs introduce new 
technologies and require new external partnerships. This shift 
can strain already limited capacity, particularly when transporta-

LIMITED TRANSITION PLANNING RESOURCES AND TIME 
Short funding windows and long implementation lead 
times present a challenge to completing long-term strategic 
transition planning:
 ▪ Prioritizing deployment versus planning. Most funding 
programs prioritize vehicle deployment over strategic 
transition planning, infrastructure development, and long-
term support. As a result, many districts move forward 
without comprehensive assessments of their fleet, facilities, 
charging needs, service schedules, or costs. Districts may 
encounter unexpected technical, operational, or financial 
hurdles that could have been avoided with more thorough 
preparation. Especially in small or rural districts with limited 
staff, this pressure can lead to rushed decisions around 
vehicle procurement, charging infrastructure, and operational 
changes, increasing the risk of future deployment issues.
CAUTION AROUND NEW TECHNOLOGIES
When considering the transition to ESBs, districts also 
consider the following costs and risks of transitioning to a 
new technology:
 ▪ Transition apprehension. Implementing any new 
transportation technology requires staff time, training, and 
operational changes—resources that many districts already 
find stretched thin. Without long-term data on ESB 
performance, some districts delay procurement decisions, 
return to more familiar technologies, or hesitate to deploy 
ESBs with vulnerable populations, such as students 
with disabilities, who would otherwise benefit greatly 
from using ESBs.
 ▪ Bus performance and downtime concern. If early ESB 
deployments encounter performance issues, districts risk 
service disruptions if they don’t already have a backup 
bus. This can be an acute problem for specialized buses 
serving more vulnerable populations, such as students with 
disabilities. New maintenance demands and the need for 
specialized training on electric drivetrains or reliance on 
dealers for repairs and charging systems further complicate 
daily operations, especially for rural districts with longer 
service wait times.
 ▪ Volatility in the ESB manufacturing and charging 
industry. Some bus and charger manufacturers have exited 
the ESB market, leading to some concerns among districts 
around the viability of the industry overall. Moreover, 
districts that have already purchased buses or equipment 
from businesses no longer operating now need to address the 
need for service and warranty protection.
 ▪ Shortage of trained ESB technicians and drivers. The 
number of automotive technicians and school bus drivers 
employed in the United States has fallen by 5 percent and 
15 percent, respectively, since 2020; this equates to 40,000 
technicians and 34,000 drivers (TechForce Foundation 2023; 
Martinez Hickey et al. 2024). This is especially pronounced 
in rural areas, where the shortage of technicians leads to 
delays in maintenance. While this affects the entire school 
bus workforce, shortages of qualified technicians could 
pose particular challenges for school districts looking to 
transition to ESBs.
 ▪ Hiring and retention challenges. Low pay, atypical 
schedules, limited career advancement opportunities, and 
mental strain from managing students have contributed to 
a lack of skilled workers in the school transportation sector. 
In many states, schools have lost funding due to declines in 
enrollment and state budget shortfalls. School districts also 
face competition for talent from companies that operate 
electric heavy-duty trucks and offer higher wages and better 
overall job quality.
 ▪ Barriers to comprehensive and consistent training. 
Training offered to school districts by OEMs, dealers, and 
third-party providers varies in quality and consistency. 
OEMs or bus dealers often require districts to rely on them 
for ESB maintenance. Without a train-the-trainer model, 
transportation teams lose the in-house expertise they had 
with diesel buses. Moreover, young technicians, particularly 
from disadvantaged communities, may face significant 
financial and logistical barriers to entering the field. Training 
programs can be expensive, with some requiring participants 
to travel long distances and take time off work.
ESB MISPERCEPTIONS
A lack of accurate information about ESB operations can limit 
adoption. Many communities, school boards, and transportation 
staff have misconceptions and knowledge gaps about ESBs, 
ranging from concerns about battery safety and cold-weather 
performance to doubts about operational efficiency and the 
overall environmental benefits of electrification. Misinformation 
poses a significant threat to community support, especially 
when it comes to local votes to approve budgetary allocations 
for ESBs. Misperceptions may also fuel politicization of 
transportation electrification.

Achieving TCO parity
The following list outlines key approaches that each stakeholder 
group should take to drive price reductions and achieve a favor-
able TCO for ESBs:
BUS OWNERS AND OPERATORS
 ▪ Consider alternative procurement models or products. 
Implementing alternative procurement processes, such as 
aggregated procurement that covers multiple years and is 
coordinated by a central entity, may allow school districts 
to negotiate better purchase prices, reduce administrative 
burdens, signal high-volume demand for manufacturers, and 
lower the local capacity required for the transition. Many 
districts have long-standing relationships with existing 
dealers, but consideration of the full range of available 
models and expansion of the approved vendors list could 
encourage competition on price among manufacturers. 
Lower prices, paired with price transparency, will increase 
demand for ESBs beyond those included in aggregated 
procurement agreements.
 ▪ Enable price data transparency. Bus owners and operators 
should participate in efforts to make pricing data transparent. 
Increased transparency would make for a more efficient 
market, help other purchasers negotiate better deals, and 
resolve information asymmetry. Sharing pricing data can 
create competitive pressure that benefits all districts and, in 
turn, helps the industry grow overall.
 ▪ Explore tax credits and financing. All school districts 
should learn about available tax credits—45W, the most 
significant such credit, expired in 2025, but tax credits may 
be options in the future—and finance options, including 
federal and state incentives, to support their transition to 
ESBs. These tools can help reduce up-front cost burdens. 
Advocating for these tools is also key to their continued role 
in filling the purchase price gap until TCO parity is reached.
 ▪ Manage charging. Implementing smart charging 
technologies can help manage peak electricity load demands 
and reduce costs for school districts. These systems allow 
charging to be optimized based on real-time grid conditions, 
minimizing the impact on the broader electricity grid and 
reducing the risk of overbuilding infrastructure.
BUS AND CHARGER OEMS AND DEALERS
 ▪ Pursue public-private partnerships. Advancing public-
private partnerships between OEMs or dealers and school 
districts, state agencies, or private financing organizations 
will secure more predictable demand, demonstrate 
proficiency, and support long-term planning. These 
partnerships could include co-funded infrastructure pilots 
or multiyear procurement agreements that reduce risk 
for OEMs, districts, and financing entities, with a goal of 
lowering production costs.
 ▪ Support the repower market. Bus operators and OEMs 
should support the repower market, allowing older diesel 
buses to be retrofitted with electric drivetrains. This can be a 
cost-effective interim solution, especially in markets where 
new buses are not immediately affordable.
 ▪ Ensure that manufacturing is equipped to scale. By 
investing in the scale-up of production capabilities, OEMs 
will be ready to meet the growing demand for ESBs, 
lowering their costs as they learn by doing, and identifying 
and addressing potential barriers that could slow down 
production or deployment.
UTILITIES
 ▪ Serve as project sponsors in aggregated procurements. 
Utilities can serve as project sponsors or central procuring 
entities in aggregated procurements to help streamline 
procurement and financing processes for school districts.
 ▪ Develop compensation for grid services. Offer programs 
for services provided by V2G to create demand for grid-
supportive loads and bidirectional energy. This should 
include participation in virtual power plants (VPPs), energy 
export tariffs, and other demand response programs.
 ▪ Explore battery second life. Research the potential for 
battery second-life use, supporting data collection and 
coordination among siloed actors, including policymakers. 
Grid support is one potential use.
ADVOCATES AND COMMUNITY REPRESENTATIVES
 ▪ Promote data transparency. Amplifying the call for price 
transparency can ensure that all school districts access 
fair and competitive pricing. By pushing for open data 
policies and public reporting of cost information, advocates 
can help correct power imbalances in the market, make 
aggregated procurement efforts more effective, and ensure 
that the benefits of electrification are shared equitably 
across communities.

 ▪ Connect districts with financing expertise. School districts, 
especially those in underserved communities, need more 
education on how to leverage tax incentives, rebates, and 
finance stacking opportunities. Accessible, simple resources 
could assist in overcoming the complexity of these financial 
tools. Organizations such as WRI, the National Renewable

free resources to assist with financing considerations.
FINANCING ENTITIES
 ▪ Educate, engage, and originate offerings. Financial 
institutions should conduct outreach and provide education 
to school districts and other potential project partners as 
part of their deal origination efforts. Nevada Clean Energy 
Fund has demonstrated how a state energy finance authority 
can bridge the roles of technical assistance provider, project 
developer, and financial partner (NCEF 2025).
 ▪ Integrate funding and financing solutions. By engaging 
policymakers and agencies managing public grant dollars, 
financing entities can combine green bank capital, tax credits, 
and public funds. This could enable school districts to access 
a wider range of funding options and reduce the up-front 
financial burden of purchasing ESBs.
 ▪ Serve as project sponsors in aggregated procurements. 
Financing entities can serve as project sponsors or central 
procuring entities in aggregated procurements. This role can 
help streamline procurement and financing processes for 
school districts.
 ▪ Develop tailored financing products. Integrating 
concessionary elements such as flexible terms and 
preferential rates can help ensure that districts can 
participate without jeopardizing their financial stability, 
especially for low-income and rural school districts. These 
lower-cost financing options can help bring ESBs to areas 
that might otherwise be unable to afford the transition due 
to budget or capacity.
 ▪ Strengthen battery recycling and sustainability. 
Investments in battery recycling infrastructure and the 
development of second-life battery applications are critical to 
unlocking second-life battery use as a revenue stream while 
mitigating the environmental impacts of ESB deployment.
POLICYMAKERS, PUBLIC UTILITY COMMISSIONS, AND 
REGULATORS
 ▪ Dedicate strategic state and federal funding. Policymakers 
should expand funding programs to support ESBs until 
TCO parity is achieved. Award levels should phase down 
over time and be targeted at either the incremental TCO 
gap or the bottom-up manufacturing cost differential. 
Underserved communities that would not be able to address 
the up-front cost barrier even with TCO parity may need 
higher levels of funding. Funding programs can also use 
funds to strategically target specific cost drivers—for 
example, by linking funding levels to market-wide battery 
prices or price trends. Additionally, in order to drive 
down costs, policymakers should link incentives to the 
use of managed charging. Policymakers could support the 
development of financial products by using public funds 
to guarantee a minimum end-of-life value for vehicles or 
batteries (Chacon et al. 2024).
 ▪ Encourage new competitors. New entrants, including 
new OEMs, repower manufacturers and nontraditional 
transportation service providers such as Highland Fleets, 
could bring competition on price point, service quality, or 
other factors to the industry.
 ▪ Coordinate state, federal, and local support. Create a single 
entry point for school districts to access all available funding 
programs. This would help simplify the application process, 
minimize administrative burdens, and align eligibility 
requirements and deadlines across funding programs 
to prevent districts from missing opportunities due to 
conflicting schedules.
 ▪ Standardize ESB specifications. State regulators across 
multiple states should move toward universal standards for 
ESBs, potentially reducing the complexity of different school 
districts managing various types of buses and chargers. This 
could simplify the market and help scale adoption. Federal 
funders can also create federal standards as a stipulation 
for receiving funding, aligning with universal standards at 
the state level.
 ▪ Align program rules for price reduction. Review and 
adjust program rules to ensure a step down in incentive 
amounts over time and price caps for awarded vehicles to 
drive down market prices. Complement this with increased 
access to finance. State contracts, for example, should be 
flexible enough to accommodate price reductions over time 
or implement phased funding reductions to send a signal 
to the market. Agencies can take an active role in demand 
aggregation on behalf of program beneficiaries to secure bulk 
discounts. Allowing funding to support repowers could help 
increase supply and manufacturing capacity while allowing 
for cost-effective transitions.
 ▪ Support public access to pricing data. Encourage 
data transparency and provide public access to pricing 
information for buses funded with public dollars, helping 
school districts to negotiate better prices.

 ▪ Reduce barriers to market entry. By lowering entry barriers 
for new market participants, policymakers can foster

Reducing charging infrastructure cost and time
The following outlines key actions that stakeholders can take to 
build the robust and reliable charging infrastructure needed for 
successful ESB deployment:
BUS OWNERS AND OPERATORS
 ▪ Understand fleet dynamics for electrification planning. 
Conduct thorough assessments of fleet operations, including 
current bus routes and daily usage patterns as data for 
planning. This understanding helps manage charging needs 
more efficiently by right-sizing infrastructure and reducing 
power demand; it also allows for longer-term planning for 
full fleet electrification.
 ▪ Require interoperability. By incorporating clear 
requirements for interoperability between chargers and 
buses in procurement, bus owners can ensure that different 
makes and models of chargers and buses can work together 
seamlessly, reducing complications during procurement 
and deployment.
 ▪ Collaborate with utilities. Engage with utilities before 
ordering vehicles and chargers to assess grid capacity and 
identify areas where infrastructure upgrades are needed. 
This will ensure that any necessary upgrades are planned 
for in advance and can be developed appropriately 
before ESB delivery.
BUS AND CHARGER OEMS AND DEALERS
 ▪ Increase support through dealerships. Work with 
dealerships to ensure support for ESBs. This support should 
include initial communication about compatible EVSE, the 
role of managed charging, and clear communication about 
available resources.
 ▪ Ensure interoperability. Compatibility between different 
ESBs and EVSE will reduce confusion and simplify the 
purchasing process for school districts while enabling 
economies of scale. Interoperability testing or in-use results 
should be published by OEMs.
UTILITIES
 ▪ Support programs to facilitate grid connection. By 
including the development of fleet advisory services, 
proactive planning, make-ready programs, flexible 
interconnections, the use of on-site generation, stationary 
storage, and charge management systems, utilities can help 
ease the burden of connecting ESBs to the grid.
 ▪ Communicate existing capacity at school bus fleet sites. 
Utilities must provide clear and transparent communication 
about the existing grid capacity at locations where school bus 
fleets are or will be stationed. This will enable school districts 
and operators to plan effectively and avoid unnecessary costs 
or delays related to grid upgrades.
 ▪ Prioritize ESB upgrades. Engage actively with school 
districts, publicize utility points of contact, and prioritize site 
upgrades for ESB fleets, where possible, to realize benefits 
for kids and communities.
 ▪ Plan proactively for grid infrastructure upgrades. Utilities 
should conduct longer-term planning for future grid 
infrastructure upgrades, ensuring that there is adequate 
capacity for the increased load from ESB charging. This 
planning should limit the impact on ratepayers while 
facilitating growth.
 ▪ Support grid access in rural and tribal areas. Coordinate 
with tribal governments, rural districts, and local partners to 
understand priorities and identify technology solutions to 
support areas that are resource-constrained or only served by 
single-phase power.
ADVOCATES AND COMMUNITY REPRESENTATIVES
 ▪ Publicize sources for best practices. Share available 
resources that describe best practices in infrastructure 
investment with bus operators, OEMs, utilities, financing 
entities, and policymakers. Effective communication about 
successful deployments, along with local priorities and 
challenges, can ensure that infrastructure investments

Enabling successful deployment and operations
The following outlines key actions stakeholders can take to cre-
ate an enabling environment that supports equitable outcomes, 
effective operations, workforce development, and system-
wide collaboration:
BUS OWNERS AND OPERATORS
 ▪ Engage with a third-party entity for procurement 
management. Engaging, for instance, a national green bank 
or school procurement alliance to oversee the procurement 
process for ESBs would ensure that school districts have 
their interests represented and would keep costs reasonable. 
This would, in turn, help school districts make informed 
decisions and foster information symmetry during the 
procurement process.
 ▪ Prioritize accessibility in early deployment. Prioritize the 
use of ESBs on routes that serve students with disabilities, 
who often experience compounded transportation 
barriers. Equitable transitions require that clean, safe, 
and accessible buses reach these students without delay 
(Shorter et al. 2024).
 ▪ Incorporate piloting and testing. School districts need more 
time to pilot ESBs, familiarize themselves with new systems, 
and identify performance issues before fully integrating 
ESBs into their fleets. Testing periods should be factored 
into deployment timelines and designed for rapid learning 
and advancement to balance the need for scaling.
 ▪ Share best practices. Publish data on school districts’ 
websites, participate in case studies, or share at conferences 
and webinars. By fostering a collaborative environment, 
districts can learn from one another’s experiences, 
reducing mistakes and increasing the overall success of 
ESB deployments.
 ▪ Use telematics systems. By using telematics systems to 
collect real-time data on vehicle performance and sharing 
these data with relevant stakeholders, owners and operators 
can improve route optimization, fleet management, and 
overall efficiency.
 ▪ Include workers in transition planning. Involve workers 
from the outset in ESB transition planning. This ensures that 
workers are well-informed about, trained on, and integrated 
into the ESB adoption process, helping to ease the transition 
and maximize operational efficiency.

Recommendations for near-term 
actions
While the road to full fleet electrification will be a long process, 
requiring time for fleet turnover and scaled manufacturing, 
it is vital to step up efforts now. This is a pivotal moment to 
move beyond early adoption reliant on public incentives toward 
wider deployment supported by a sustainable market. To build 
on current momentum, the next one to three years must focus 
on several priority actions. Drawing upon the menu of afore-
mentioned options, Figure 4 details recommendations for high 
priority near-term actions by specific actors, with additional 
guidance on best practices and lessons learned.
Accelerate progress toward TCO parity
The single largest barrier to full fleet electrification is the 
persistent cost gap between electric and diesel buses. Public 
investment must continue until this gap is closed and be 
structured to promote a sustainable market that relies on 
financing over funding solutions. This calls for public incentives 
that help boost demand and support scale in manufacturing 
to drive down prices, along with shaping market conditions to 
support bus-related revenue streams. Once TCO parity has been 
achieved, there should be a transition from funding to financing 
programs. The following are recommended “must-do” steps for 
accelerating progress toward TCO parity:
 ▪ Policymakers should dedicate new state and federal 
funding. Policymakers, with input from the ecosystem, 
should expand and refine programs that provide funding 
support for full fleet electrification, with incentives tied 
to realistic cost assumptions that encourage the market 
to move toward TCO parity and consider the needs of 
underserved communities. Policymakers should also work 
to develop greater scale through the creation of aggregated 
procurement programs, with support and buy-in from bus 
owners, advocates, utilities, and financing entities, using 
multiyear timelines and promoting greater standardization 
of bus specifications. India has demonstrated how this can 
be done: the national government’s coordinated demand 
aggregation effort, supported by WRI experts, secured 
electric bus services at 23-to-27 percent lower cost than 
diesel/compressed natural gas (CNG) buses, without 
subsidies (Dubedi 2025).

 ▪ Utilities should support the development of bus-related

coordination with manufacturers, bus owners and operators, 
and utilities, need to move the industry from pilots to scale 
for revenue-generating opportunities tied to grid resiliency.
 ▪ Utilities should focus on ESB deployment as an 
asset by developing compensation programs for grid 
services. There is great potential here, as demonstrated 
in Oakland, California, where Zum is deploying a fleet 
of 74 ESBs that will use bidirectional chargers to send an 
estimated 2.1 gigawatt hours of energy back to the grid

districts need to work together to build a stronger 
and more sustainable V2G ecosystem. There may not 
be a robust V2G revenue opportunity for every school

alongside other vehicle-to-building and vehicle-to-
load options for uses such as emergency response and 
cooling centers.

closely with utilities to advance the development of 
compensation mechanisms for energy provided to said 
utilities. Examples of successful programs have included 
participation in demand response programs, daily 
dynamic rates for energy dispatch, and compensation for 
purchasing ESBs in exchange for utility control of energy 
discharge over the summer.

manufacturers, and private sector companies focused 
on energy storage should support thoughtful design

V2G services. Redwood Materials in Nevada provides 
a promising example of EV battery second-life uses 
(Kothari 2023). Utilizing depleted ESB batteries as 
stationary storage may provide a key end-of-life revenue 
opportunity to reduce the TCO gap while enhancing 
the sustainability of the battery supply chain (Kothari 
et al. 2023). This market holds a lot of potential as the 
number of ESBs grows.

develop ESB-focused financing products. In anticipation 
of reaching TCO parity and transitioning from funding to 
financing programs, federal and state policymakers should 
work with the private sector to develop ESB-focused 
financing mechanisms that can help address higher up-front 
costs by capitalizing future operating savings. This can 
include a blending of public financing programs through, for 
example, state infrastructure banks, as well as private sector 
investments. For example, Connecticut provides low-interest, 
long-term financing for ESBs, charging equipment, and 
electric system upgrades, with additional opportunities for 
projects in underserved communities (Pers. Comm. 2025). 
Such programs will play a key role in transitioning the ESB 
market from being incentive-dependent to truly sustainable.
Ensure infrastructure and utility readiness
Once there is funding or financing available to secure an 
ESB and the necessary charging hardware and software, the 
next greatest hurdle is deploying and operating the charging 
to minimize cost and maximize efficiency. Tackling these 
challenges can involve complex planning around the use of 
the depot, the timing of refueling, and the adoption of new 
approaches to routes, often on a site-specific basis. Constrained 
grids or physical space can add another layer of complexity. 
There are, however, several well-developed and emerging 
solutions that school districts can explore and deploy now. To 
do this, school districts must work with their infrastructure 
providers and utilities, with support from utility regulators and 
engagement with the community (Stafford 2024). Essential to 
these efforts is smarter coordination and proactive planning by 
utilities. An often-offered recommendation for school districts 
is to “call your utility” as a very first step to planning for ESB 
transition. This remains an important initial action. Below are 
recommended approaches for what to ask for and explore in 
partnership once a utility is involved, including “must-do” steps:

 ▪ As a critical first step, bus owners and operators should

managed charging, in which all ESB charging is managed 
for time of use and minimal peak power consumption 
to avoid preventable costs to operators and ratepayers. 
Multiple commercial product vendors offer services to do 
so (e.g., The Mobility House and EnergyHub). A second 
approach is to standardize charging requirements. This 
means aligning charger compatibility requirements and 
procurement specs in requests for proposals to improve 
interoperability.
 ▪ Utilities must engage in proactive planning by 
prioritizing the development of sites that will 
likely host large loads, including ESB depots. New 
York City’s Zerega Avenue Project is an example of 
this (NYDPS 2025).
 ▪ Utilities and bus owners and operators should plan for 
flexible connections. This means developing programs 
that allow for dynamic loads, like ESBs, to connect to 
areas that might otherwise be grid constrained. Utilities 
are already engaged in testing out these efforts. Under the 
PG&E Flex Connect Pilot, for example, customers with 
controllable loads can connect to the grid without waiting 
for a capacity upgrade.
 ▪ Bus owners and operators should incorporate mobile 
infrastructure options into their planning. Mobile 
infrastructure means deploying temporary or modular 
charging infrastructure that can decrease construction 
requirements for EVSE (First Student 2025; Kempower

in Pennsylvania and Westville School District in Illinois 
have integrated a trenchless, aboveground charging 
platform that saved them time and money (First Student 
2024; District Administration 2024).
 ▪ Utilities must plan for meeting the grid access needs 
of rural and tribal areas by developing rural and tribal 
grid equity plans with clear timelines, community 
consultation, and funding strategies (Compton 
and Curran 2025).
Support districts, communities, and 
workforce beyond bus purchase
Once buses and charging equipment are secured and deployed 
efficiently, school districts need sustained planning and opera-
tional support that encompasses the workforce and the broader 
community. Developing the workforce is a critical piece of 
scaling ESB deployment equitably and reliably. Buy-in from 
the people directly impacted by the buses, including operators 
and maintenance technicians; school bus riders and parents; 
and residents in the broader community, including advocates, is 
essential to building momentum and supporting the transition. 
The following are recommended “must-do” steps for supporting 
districts, communities, and the workforce beyond bus purchase:
 ▪ Bus and charger OEMS and dealers need to play a leading 
role in supporting schools with the transition, both before 
and well after ESBs are delivered. Others must also play 
a supporting role, including technical assistance providers 
in the public, private, and nonprofit sectors, and utilities. 
Leading areas of need are assisting with procurement, 
including mechanisms for aggregated procurement, and 
providing planning support around infrastructure.
 ▪ Bus and charger OEMS, in cooperation with 
policymakers, should standardize ESB workforce 
training. Develop and implement national training and 
certification standards for ESB drivers and maintenance 
personnel, in partnership with OEMs, labor unions, and 
technical experts, and with the support of advocates. 
There are national or highly accepted training standards 
in the EV charging infrastructure space (e.g., the 
Electric Vehicle Infrastructure Training Program) 
and the electric transit bus sector (e.g., the American 
Public Transportation Association Zero Emission Bus 
Maintenance Training Standards).
 ▪ Policymakers must include access to training 
programs through increased investment in hands-on, 
in-person training opportunities, especially in rural 
and underserved areas where virtual training is not 
sufficient. Partner with community colleges and technical 
education providers to deliver accessible programs that 
build local workforce pipelines.
 ▪ Advocates and community representatives, along with 
bus owners and operators using ESBs, should share clear 
messaging and data on ESB health, cost, and operational 
and climate benefits. The Electric School Bus Initiative 
Ambassador program is one example of school district 
leaders who are sharing their experiences with electrification

decision-makers who need to approve plans and funding,

this transition, their voices and perspectives must be included

important to include ratepayers—local utility customers—in

Conclusion
The road to full fleet electrification of US school buses presents 
a historic opportunity to transform how we transport our kids 
to and from school using modern technology that is cleaner, 
quieter, and more efficient. While there are several challenges 
to this transition, we already have many solutions, as presented 
in this roadmap. Full fleet electrification will not be an easy 
endeavor, but through collaborative efforts across federal, state, 
local, and private sectors, stakeholders can leverage their unique 
positions to address barriers to ESB adoption—affordability, 
infrastructure deployment, and workforce readiness—and ensure 
that the transition to ESBs not only reduces emissions and 
improves public health but also strengthens communities and 
provides economic opportunities for those who need them most. 
Moreover, this transition will help support the transformation 
of the transportation sector overall, demonstrating a pathway for 
the medium- and heavy-duty sector and helping to normalize 
electric transportation for a new generation. Now is the time 
to act to create a cleaner, more equitable future for all students, 
families, and communities.

Appendix C. School bus ecosystem actors 
The electrification of the US school bus fleet involves a broad range 
of stakeholders—described in more detail in this appendix—with 
each playing a critical role in ensuring an equitable, effective, and 
accessible transition. At the center of the ecosystem are student 
school bus riders and drivers, who experience the impacts of 
every player’s actions in their daily lives. Approaching school bus 
electrification as an ecosystem calls for engaging actors as part of an 
interconnected system.
Bus owners and operators
Bus owners and operators are the primary actors responsible for 
purchasing, maintaining, and operating school buses. These include 
school districts; transportation staff, including drivers, private 
contractors, and other transportation service providers; and tribal 
communities. Responsibilities include making decisions about bus 
routing and serving individual student needs, with the top priority 
being the safe and reliable delivery of students daily. While private 
contractors account for over one-third of the existing fleet, only a few 
fleets are large enough to leverage economies of scale for purchasing 
power and technical expertise (NSTA 2025). This means that 
hundreds of smaller contractors and thousands of individual school 
districts will be navigating this process.
Bus and charger OEMs and dealers
OEMs and dealers are responsible for designing, building, and 
distributing both vehicles and charging infrastructure. Manufacturers 
produce the core technologies needed for electrification, including 
buses and the hardware and software systems that manage charging 
and energy usage. Each OEM produces discrete components with 
little overlap between the bus and charging systems. Dealers serve 
as intermediaries between OEMs and end users, providing sales, 
delivery, technical support, and ongoing maintenance services. 
These actors are responsible for offering training and support 
to bus operators to ensure that new electric fleets are managed 
effectively and safely.
Utilities
Utilities play a pivotal role in providing the necessary energy 
infrastructure to support electric school bus fleets. They ensure 
that power grids are adequately equipped to handle the increased 
demand from charging stations. Utilities may also provide additional 
services related to vehicle electrification, including advisory 
services, funding for vehicles, electrical infrastructure and EVSE, 
and participation in, for example, managed charging and V2G 
programs and pilots.
Advocates and community representatives
Advocates and community representatives serve as critical voices in 
promoting an equitable transition to ESBs. These groups often work 
to ensure that marginalized communities, including in low-income 
and rural areas, as well as students with disabilities, are prioritized in 
electrification programs. They often collaborate with policymakers, 
bus operators, and OEMs to ensure that programs and technologies 
are designed with the needs of vulnerable populations in mind. 
Community representatives can include parents and students who, 
as direct constituents of the school district, can regularly encourage 
school districts on electrification. Even in the face of competing 
priorities, family member advocates can play a key role through local 
campaigns and school board outreach.
Financing entities
Beyond local and state bonding capacity to support school 
transportation costs, financial institutions, including federal and state 
agencies and private banks, can provide capital and/or development 
support for school bus electrification. These entities can offer 
capital—in the form of equity or debt at concessionary or market 
rates—that makes the transition to ESBs financially viable for school 
districts and operators, including in underserved and resource-
constrained communities.
Policymakers, public utility commissions, and regulators
Policymakers and regulators are responsible for setting the legislative 
and regulatory frameworks that support and drive the transition 
to ESBs. They do this at the federal, state, and sometimes local 
level. They design programs and policies that govern the funding, 
incentives, rates, and regulations related to general vehicle emissions 
(e.g., CAFÉ standards and NOx rules) and specific to medium- and 
heavy-duty, including school bus, electrification (e.g, ACT). Regulators 
set policies that can affect the financial feasibility of electrification 
and the investment rates in ESB infrastructure, which can impact the 
distribution of access to clean transportation options. Policymakers 
and regulators are responsible for crafting policies that ensure 
funding and infrastructure are distributed fairly and that programs are 
inclusive and accessible.

About the Electric School Bus Initiative
Founded in partnership with the Bezos Earth Fund in 2020 and in 
collaboration with partners and communities, WRI’s Electric School 
Bus Initiative aims to build unstoppable momentum toward an 
equitable transition of the US school bus fleet to electric, bringing 
health, climate, and economic benefits to children and families across 
the country and normalizing electric mobility for an entire generation.
