HIGHLIGHTS
 ▪ The World Resources Institute (WRI) Ross Center for 
Sustainable Cities has had a long-standing presence 
in Mexico City and has been a key change agent for 
sustainable development. WRI Ross Center’s long-term 
engagement across multiple sectors in Mexico City 
makes it one of the organization’s “deep dive” urban 
geographies. The main objective of this document is 
to understand the results of this “deep dive” approach 
in Mexico City by documenting and evaluating lessons 
learned over the years.
 ▪ Learning from past successes and failures and 
documenting the experiences and outcomes of projects 
undertaken in cities is essential for catalyzing the kind 
of city-wide change that WRI Ross Center aspires to. 
To this end, we used a mixed-methods approach to 
analyze WRI Ross Center’s influence in Mexico City 
and the effectiveness of its deep dive approach there 
(starting with its origins as the Center for Sustainable 
Transportation–CTS).
 ▪ We found that WRI Mexico’s technical expertise 
combined with understanding of the political 
landscape was integral to the success of Mexico City’s 
implementation of its Bus Rapid Transit (BRT) system, 
Metrobús, in 2006. This paper outlines WRI Ross Center’s 
role in this project and finds that the organization 
helped contribute to transformative change in the city’s 
transportation network.
 ▪ Global projects such as the Building Efficiency 
Accelerator (which has a focus on Mexico City) that 
have involved deep collaboration across WRI’s matrix 
(international offices, programs, and centers) and other 
institutions have allowed for WRI Ross Center to broaden 
and build its technical expertise in sectors such as 
building efficiency. 
 ▪ Three success factors for sparking the kind of 
change WRI Ross Center has seen in Mexico City’s 
transportation sector and beyond include: a relationship 
of trust with city officials, a deep understanding of 
the political and institutional ecosystem of the city, 
and flexibility of funding and institutional structure to 
encourage innovation.

SUMMARY
As the largest program at the World Resources 
Institute (WRI)—an international NGO working 
at the nexus of the environment and international 
development—the WRI Ross Center for Sustainable 
Cities believes that “diving deep” in a particular 
geography can help the organization effectively 
achieve its goals, and those of the cities it works in, 
at scale. Working across the network of international 
offices in Brazil, Mexico, India, Turkey, China, and 
Ethiopia, WRI aims to help cities achieve greater 
economic productivity, better environmental quality, 
and a higher quality of life for all citizens.  The 
“deep dive” approach focuses on long-term and 
multisectoral engagement across the different systems 
in a city—transport, water, energy and land use, 
among others—and transcends individual project 
funding and political turnover to make city-wide 
transformative change possible. In several cities, 
WRI has maintained a presence for more than a 
decade, in which time the institution has developed 
strong relationships built on trust with city officials 
and urban organizations. WRI Ross Center’s time in 
these cities has allowed for the buildup of capacity 
and localized knowledge, which it has accessed 
thanks to flexible funding that has allowed the 
organization to respond to emerging opportunities 
while pursuing a long-term vision of sustainable 
urban development within a broader global strategy.  
The WRI Ross Center’s work in Mexico City 
exemplifies its deep dive strategy. Originally 
established in 2002 as the Center for Sustainable 
Transportation (Centro de Transporte Sustentable; 
CTS), the organization evolved into CTS-EMBARQ 
Mexico in 2011, and into WRI Mexico in 2016 (WRI 
Mexico has since broadened its scope of work to 
include climate, energy, and forests, but for the 
purposes of this paper we will be focusing on WRI 
Mexico’s city work, (“WRI Ross Center”)). The 
organization’s growth is partly attributable to a 
partnership with the Mexico City government on its 
first bus rapid transit (BRT)1 system, Metrobús, which 
launched in 2006. Mexico City and CTS collaborated

WRI Ross Center’s deep dive experience in Mexico

To what extent has the “deep dive” approach 
contributed to transformative change, to which 
WRI Ross Center has been aspiring–broad, cross-

all those participating in the city (Beard et al. 2016)? 
To answer this, WRI Ross Center must review what 
the institution did, and how the city has changed as 
a result of its work in collaboration with partners. 
This paper aims to answer these questions regarding 
WRI Ross Center’s work in Mexico City and will 
contribute to a growing field of research around 
measuring and monitoring the impact and influence 
on local decision making of organizations working 
in urban development around the world. The team 
in WRI Mexico, based in Mexico City, contributed 
the bulk of the research and rich historical detail 
included in this paper, with methodological support 
from the WRI Global office based in Washington, DC.  
THE DEEP DIVE ENGAGEMENT 
APPROACH 
This paper is a part of a pilot effort to build a 
methodology that provides a comprehensive way 
to document, understand and evaluate WRI Ross 
Center’s work and impact in the deep dive cities. It 
uses a mixed methods approach to a) capture and 
learn from the organization’s experiences; b) analyze 
if and how WRI Mexico’s deep dive work and strategy 
has contributed to a city-wide transformation in 
Mexico City; and c) summarize lessons learned from 
years of experience doing this work. The approach 
we use to address these goals is grounded in action 
research (Huang 2010; Anderson and McLaclan 
2016) and system thinking theories (Meadows 2008; 
Tan et al. 2019), where actors affect the environment 
and attention is placed on processes, interaction of 
the elements, and the global tendency of the system, 
instead of on the isolation of variables, to provide 
specific understanding of a single component. At the 
beginning of the nineties, Mexico City was considered 
one of the most polluted cities in the world (WHO 
and UNEP 1992), and the need for improving air 
quality along with the improvement of public services 
was–and still is–evident. 4 Achieving such change is 
not easy, but it’s necessary to avoid the worst effects 
of climate change and provide basic services for a 
growing urban population. Our thesis is that the deep 
dive method allows us to achieve more results than a 
less expansive and flexible approach. Lessons learned 
will be considered for the update of WRI’s deep dive 
strategy on issues such as the need for permanent 
local staff, use of flexible funds, transparency 
in roles and funds allocation, and mechanisms 
to track and monitor impact, among others.
We analyze the enabling conditions and main results 
of five key projects: 1) Metrobús, CTS’s foundational 
project; 2) the Integrated Transport System 
(Sistema Integrado de Transporte; SIT); 3) coalition 
building around mobility–two projects we focus 
on stemming directly from the organization’s BRT 
work; 4) the Building Efficiency Accelerator (BEA); 
and 5) the Cooperation Action System Tacubaya 
(Sistema de Actuación por Cooperación; SAC), the 
latter two address energy and water, respectively, 
that contribute to WRI Mexico’s portfolio of work 
to include urban development by developing 
internal expertise and external recognition.
To assess WRI Ross Center’s possible contributions 
to relevant policy changes in Mexico City, we are 
combining quantitative accessibility analysis and 
qualitative analysis through interviews and social 
mapping tools. It is a first attempt to capture WRI 
Mexico’s experience and therefore not a complete 
impact analysis, considering methodological and data 
limitations such as information gaps and the further 
need for external validation. Detailed methodology 
for this paper is described in the corresponding 
section, with additional details in Appendix A.

FINDINGS
Our research finds that the WRI Ross Center’s deep 
dive approach in Mexico City helped support and 
catalyze change in public transportation and broader 
urban policy.5 The Center’s technical expertise–
stemming from its establishment as the Center for 
Sustainable Transport (CTS) in 2002 before being 
integrated into the broader WRI family as WRI 
Mexico–combined with political know-how was 
integral to the success of the city’s implementation of 
its BRT system, Metrobús, in 2006 (see Appendix E). 
Using our first method of measuring the institution’s 
contribution to change, the accessibility tool, we 
found that the average numbers of jobs available 
within a 30-minute commute from people’s homes 
(single or multimodal, with or without mass 
transit) was increased by 77 percent between 2004 
and 2014, before and after implementation of the 
system.6 Metrobús implementation is considered as a 
timeframe for the analysis and not the only reason for 
the increase in job accessibility (other factors might 
have been economic growth, densification, and zoning 
law changes, among others). The results can be seen in 
Figure ES-2. Given WRI Mexico’s small size compared 
to the size and complexity of the challenges faced by 
Mexico City, finding projects where the organization 
can act as a catalyst—helping to create the enabling 
conditions that generate multiplier or leveraging 
effects and thus facilitate or promote change directly 
or indirectly—is essential to city-wide transformation. 
Through our second method (qualitative analysis 
of the organization’s deep dive work in Mexico City 
through interviews of current and former staff), we 
drew several conclusions about the experience of 
WRI Ross Center’s engagements in Mexico City: WRI 
Mexico’s strategy has been appropriately flexible in 
order to align with WRI’s global strategy to respond 
to local challenges and emergent opportunities; 
WRI Mexico has been well placed in the ecosystem 
of institutions advancing sustainability in Mexico; 
and the Metrobús project, which WRI Ross Center 
had a part in implementing, has indeed sparked 
broader change in the city, reaching other sectors 
beyond urban mobility at a national level.
Analyzing detailed notes from the Board of Directors 
and versions of WRI Mexico’s strategy over the years 
revealed how it has become effectively integrated into 
the WRI Global family (especially in administrative 
processes and systems) with context sensitivity and 
adaptability around projects and strategic goals that 
respond to local needs. Perhaps one of the richest 
findings revealed through this research was that of 
the ecosystem of institutions around sustainability, 
as displayed in Figure ES-2. The vast nature of 
this ecosystem and the joint leadership that works 
to support the city’s sustainability goals has been 
key to expanding collaboration and advancing the 
issue of urban sustainability in public discourse. 
The Metrobús system has been integrated with the 
metro, suburban rail, and new nonmotorized transit 
innovations like bike sharing, along with broader 
land use and urban policy, all of which have improved 
accessibility for urban dwellers (see section ”From 
Project to City-Level Impact”). The fact that WRI 
Ross Center played a catalytic role in the original 
project and in further work on sustainable mobility 
suggests that its deep dive commitment is an effective 
strategy for influencing broader city-wide change. 
We have identified three important success 
factors for WRI’s work and strategic approach 
to transformative change in deep dive cities: 
Building Trust : Strong technical skills, combined 
with a deep understanding of the political and 
institutional context, has helped to establish CTS, 
CTS EMBARQ Mexico, and now WRI Mexico as an 
independent, knowledgeable, and trusted advisor in 
Mexico City, and at national and regional levels. 
Policy Ecosystem: WRI Ross Center’s strategic 
position in a large ecosystem of change agents 
in Mexico City has allowed the organization to 
share knowledge and learning effectively among 
partners and other stakeholders, to generate 
local capacity to address urban development 
challenges, and to empower other civil society 
actors. WRI Mexico has provided convening space 
for peers to collaborate and has used its network 
to seize windows of political opportunity.

INTRODUCTION
The World Resources Institute (WRI) is a nonpartisan 
research organization working at the nexus of 
international development and the environment. It 
has been recognized for its evidence-based research, 
policy influence, and coalition-building around key 
global challenges: food, forests, water, energy, climate, 
sustainable cities, and the ocean. The organization 
functions as both a think tank and a do tank, as 
embodied in its institutional approach: “Count it, 
change it, scale it.” WRI conducts analysis built over 
reliable data and uses research to inform government 
policies, business strategies, and civil society action. 
To scale successful policies and practices, WRI 
works with partners to implement and build upon 
recommendations, from public transport projects 
to active mobility solutions, integrated urban 
and land use planning, resource efficiency, clean 
energies, and green infrastructure. The WRI Ross 
Center for Sustainable Cities, having emerged 
from the Center for Sustainable Transport, an 
implementation-focused organization (the “do 
tank”), exemplifies this theory of change at work.
In 2002, WRI launched EMBARQ—a sustainable 
urban development initiative—in Mexico City 
through an agreement between WRI, the city 
government, and the Interdisciplinary Center of 
Biodiversity and Environment (CeIBA), a nonprofit 
civil society organization. This was the founding 
of WRI’s Center for Sustainable Transport, which 
was referred to as CTS until 2011, when it became 
CTS-EMBARQ Mexico, and later just as WRI 
Mexico. The city-specific work going on now in 
WRI Mexico falls under WRI’s umbrella program, 
WRI Ross Center for Sustainable Cities. 
Following the practices of its predecessor EMBARQ, 
the WRI Ross Center has historically analyzed project 
impact to ensure that funds are used effectively 
and that its work is consistent with institutional 
and programmatic strategies as well as funders’ 
expectations. WRI defines outcomes as positive 
actions by governments, companies, and civil society, 
informed by its work, that improve people’s lives 
and the environment. The organization has not, 
however, previously analyzed city-level change, and 
this paper represents a first attempt to do that in the 
case of Mexico City, one of the “deep dive cities.” 
The WRI Ross Center defines deep dive cities as those 
where there is an office that has a portfolio of projects 
extending beyond one sector. This presence is also 
accompanied by deep multi-stakeholder engagement 
that lasts more than five years. Enabled by a flexible 
funding model that allows the organization to build 
up capacity and localized knowledge, and to respond 
to emerging opportunities, WRI Ross Center has 
moved beyond individual projects to pursue deeper, 
lasting, and systemic change in these cities. The fact 
that WRI’s work on sustainable urban development 
originated in Mexico City makes it one of the 
pioneering “deep dive” cities for the Ross Center.7 
However, WRI Ross Center hasn’t formally 
documented the full extent of its work, knowledge 
accrued, or lessons learned over time, all of which 
seems necessary for a learning institution. Some 
knowledge is captured in knowledge products (such as 
WRI reports, working papers, and guidebooks), as well 
as donor reports and communications products (such 
as blogs and commentaries). But these are generally 
external-facing, information-sharing tools and miss 
some of the rich detail behind how key decisions were

deliberate reflection and analysis of the organization’s 
work and lessons learned over the years.

METHODOLOGY
To address this paper’s aims, we build on the five-
step approach of World Bank’s Steffan Janus, which 
was developed for institutional learning (2017) and 
is here adjusted and expanded for our purposes. 8 
The five steps of identification, capturing, validation, 
formatting, and use in learning provide a partial 
structure for our work. We have also added analytical 
steps before, during, and after these five to focus 
on how strategic decisions were made for selection 
of entry points, relationships to be forged, and 
adjustments made as WRI Ross Center progressed. 
Work for this paper was difficult because we applied 
these methods post hoc. We had to deal with a lack 
of data, staff changeover, and compiling nondigital 
documents, and so, we often relied on the memory 
of current and former staff. We also analyzed the 
institutional strategy to capture knowledge and 
extract lessons. The earlier existence of CTS and 
CTS-EMBARQ and the lasting relationships between 
current and previous staff helped overcome these 
challenges. Researchers in WRI Mexico have also 
dug deep into archives and conducted extensive 
interviews to draw the best picture of WRI Ross 
Center’s work and experiences in Mexico City over the 
years. Capturing and documenting these experiences 
fulfill steps 1 and 2 in Janus’s approach. The internal 
and external review process in combination with 
the design and layout process, standard for all WRI 
knowledge products, and the development of derivative 
communication products fulfill steps 3 and 4. Given 
our iterative interview and internal feedback on 
specific segments of the paper before the formal review 
process, our research process itself also included 
analysis and validation. Dissemination of the final 
publication along with internal learning workshops 
will be an ongoing process outlined in step 5. 
For the second aim of this deep dive research 
(understanding the impact of WRI Mexico’s city-
level role), we are combining accessibility analysis, 
interviews, and social mapping tools. Research was 
conducted in three parts: 1.) accessibility analysis: 
this uses three datasets on city-wide access to 
employment opportunities resulting from improved 
public transport in order to capture the city-level 
transformation resulting from key work streams 
such as public transport and land use; 2.) qualitative

PRACTICE NOTE   •  EXPLORING EXPERIENCE IN MEXICO CITY: A DEEP CITY-LEVEL ENGAGEMENT APPROACH      |      13
analysis of the evolution of WRI Mexico’s strategy 
and decisions made within the context of institutional 
growth and development, drawing on interviews with 
key current and former staff, and broad systems and 
sociological analysis of Mexican institutionalism along 
with perspectives on the political economy of the city; 
and 3.) mapping of the ecosystem of actors in which 
WRI Mexico has worked in Mexico City. These mixed 
methods allow us to paint a full yet evolving picture 
of WRI Mexico’s city-wide influence in Mexico City. 
There are of course limitations to what we can 
conclude about WRI Ross Center’s contribution to 
city-wide transformation. The complexity of a city 
changing over time with a shifting set of actors 
and stakeholders and of an organization growing 
from a project-focused initiative to a full-scale 
international office makes measuring WRI Ross 
Center’s city-wide impact difficult. Therefore, this 
is not a complete impact analysis, but it contributes 
to the understanding of WRI Ross Center’s role, 
and its actions and their effects at the city level. We 
hope this paper contributes to documenting WRI’s 
experiences and understanding the organization’s 
influence in the changing political economy of 
Mexico City. Further research is needed, as is careful 
documentation of ongoing projects, to better quantify 
and locate WRI’s impact. Moreover, the present 
analysis depends substantially on internal analysis 
and views, and it would ideally be accompanied by an 
independent external assessment were sufficient time 
and funds available. That remains for future work.
As an organization, WRI faces the multifaceted 
challenge of trying to shift views and behaviors 
in a changing political, cultural, and economic 
landscape. We use a simple notion of change 
here while acknowledging a vibrant literature of 
transformative change both within and outside of 
WRI (see Appendix A) (Limerick et al. 2013; Maassen 
and Galvin 2019), considering transformative change 
as broad, cross-sectoral, sustained progress towards 
improving the economy, environment, health, and 
well-being for all those living and participating in a 
city (Beard et al. 2016). This paper will explore WRI 
Mexico’s experience in enabling change, and we hope 
it will start a broader discussion of WRI’s role in 
transforming society to better protect the environment 
and the future generations that depend on it.

MEXICO CITY CONTEXT
To better understand WRI Ross Center’s impact 
in Mexico City, it is useful to recognize the 
political-economic context. In this section, we 
provide background on Mexico City focused on 
opportunities and challenges that have affected 
WRI’s work. This context will help to clarify 
lessons from past experiences and provide 
perspective on how to apply them to other cities.
As the capital city with a population of nearly 9 million 
plus a significant number of daily commuters,9 Mexico 
City serves as a center for opportunity, innovation, 
and knowledge sharing in Latin America. Previously 
known as Federal District ( Distrito Federal ), 
Mexico City was established as a federal entity in 
2016 through political reform (Rabell 2017) that 
granted the city autonomous power and authority.  
Mexico City is divided into 16 city halls whose mayors 
or heads of government are democratically elected 
every three years. These 16 joined together with 60 
municipalities from the neighboring states (States of 
Mexico and Hidalgo) to make up the Metropolitan 
Zone of the Valley of Mexico (Zona Metropolitana 
del Valle de México; MZVM). The MZVM is the 
largest metropolitan area in Mexico and one of

Phase 1
In 2002, Mexico City’s Secretary of Environment, 
the Interdisciplinary Center of Biodiversity and 
Environment (CeIBA), and WRI’s Climate, Energy 
and Pollution Program came together to sign a 
Memorandum of Understanding (MOU) around a new 
initiative dedicated to improving air quality through 
transformation of the city’s transport system. The 
result of this alliance was an independent organization 
known as Centro de Transporte Sustentable, or CTS.
The primary objective of CTS was to provide key 
technical assistance for the creation of Mexico 
City’s first BRT line, known as Metrobús. CTS 
soon moved beyond technical assistance on the 
project to promote public discourse on sustainable 
mobility. In 2005, along with El Colegio de México , 
CTS convened the first forum on transportation 
in the country—the International Congress for 
Sustainable Transport—which brought together 
experts and stakeholders towards a common 
vision of sustainable urban mobility. 
An upshot of this event was the journal Movilidad 
Amable (“‘Friendly Mobility’”), which aimed to 
promote public interest in urban mobility. Since 
2005 the Congress has grown each year, bolstered by 
support from many other organizations 10 that helped 
to create an ecosystem of institutions, government 
agencies, national and international NGOs, and 
transport users focused on sustainable mobility, 
urban development, and climate change (air quality). 
Phase 2
The second phase of CTS’s evolution began in 2006 
and is defined by the scope and scale broadening 
of the organization’s work on urban mobility. 
CTS’s ongoing involvement with the expansion of 
Metrobús since 2008 helped to solidify its reputation 
in the urban mobility realm and allowed the 
organization to strengthen its mobility expertise 
and grow its portfolio of projects in the city.
Following the arrival of a new CEO and a new 
head of government in Mexico City in 2005, CTS 
transitioned from a MOU to a full-fledged civil society 
organization in 2006. Along with other changes in the 
organization’s internal structure, a board of directors 
was established. Internal guidelines such as a mission 
statement and operation procedures were created. 
This led to increased support from funders, some of 
whom supported the new Institutional Development 
and Strategic Alliances programs within CTS. Among 
funders, the Shell Foundation and the William 
and Flora Hewlett Foundation played significant 
roles in restructuring the organization, providing 
not only key financing but also strategic advice on 
implementing, tracking, and overcoming challenges.
Key actors interviewed concurred that CTS’s 
early involvement in the Metrobús project helped 
bring in new funders hoping to support broader 
sustainable urban mobility work. Key partners 
included Bloomberg Philanthropies’ Global 
Initiative on Road Safety (BIGRS), FedEx, the 
Andean Development Corporation (CAF), and the 
World Bank, among others, all of whom opened 
doors for new work within CTS, including direct 
involvement in developing Mexico City’s Road 
Safety Agenda in 2009 (WRI Mexico 2016).11

and urban development, which were incorporated 
with a comprehensive strategy that determined the 
creation of products (derived from the initial strategy 
on public space and urban development approaches) 
such as a manual, a toolkit, and applications of this 
framework to low-income housing, among others. 
Further projects focused on public space allowed CTS 
to maintain an urban development agenda. In 2010, 
CTS led the project Public Space, Public Life ( Espacio 
Público, Vida Publica; EPVP); the organization’s first 
project focused on improving public space in the city 
as an adaptation of Gehl Architects’ work on public 
space (CTS EMBARQ Mexico 2016). Later projects 
in this line of work included the Pocket Parks project 
(CTS EMBARQ Mexico 2016) and the 20 de Noviembre 
and Pasos Seguros projects in 2014. Outside Mexico 
City, WRI led a participatory public space recovery 
project in La Pila (San Luis Potosí) in 2016, and in 
2018 developed proposals for a National Agenda on 
Public Space and Public Life (WRI Mexico 2018). 
Phase 4
The year 2012 was a turning point for CTS as it shifted 
its branding strategy and widened its transport 
focus. CTS publicly became CTS-EMBARQ, which 
indicated a broadening of institutional objectives 
while maintaining the reputation that CTS had 
built up over nearly a decade (WRI Mexico 2016). 
CTS-EMBARQ aimed to create integrated public 
policy solutions that improved transport system 
operations, accessibility, sustainability, and road 
safety by maintaining its participation in projects 
such as PROTRAM. CTS-EMBARQ played a key 
role in proposing and designing an Integrated 
Transport System in Mexico City after the arrival 
of the former general manager of TransMilenio 
(Bogotá’s BRT system). During this phase, internal 
changes included the institutionalization of key 
roles and areas, and defining a theory of change 
combining three interrelated approaches: to deliver 
strategic and pioneer projects in cities; to replicate 
best practices; and to shift international policy in 
order to leverage sizeable sustainable investments 
and influence national policies (WRI 2011, 18).
Phase 5
The final phase in CTS-EMBARQ’s evolution as an 
organization came in 2016 as it joined WRI as WRI 
Mexico. Because WRI Mexico was still focused only on 
urban issues, the international office acted primarily 
as a branch of the Ross Center for Sustainable Cities 
(WRI’s cities program, named in 2014). As such, WRI 
Mexico has been closely aligned with the Ross Center’s 
mission to “expand transport and urban development 
expertise of the EMBARQ network to catalyze 
innovative solutions in other sectors, including 
water, buildings, land use and energy, combining 
the research excellence of WRI with 15 years of on-
the-ground impact through a network of more than 
250 experts working from Brazil, China, Ethiopia, 
India, Mexico and Turkey” (Wrirosscities.org 2019).
The transition to WRI Mexico came with a move 
to share knowledge and expertise from across 
WRI’s programs, centers, and international offices, 
as well as to tap into funding from the Stephen 
M. Ross Philanthropies. During this time, WRI 
Mexico was restructured into five work areas, 
each with its own director: Urban Mobility, Urban 
Development, Energy and Climate Change, Forests, 
and Communications and Public Affairs. Further 
explanation of this is provided in Appendix C.

A HISTORY OF CTS  
AND METROBÚS
At the end of the 20th century, environmental 
degradation and pollution had moved to the center 
of global political discourse. Having experienced 
rapid urban growth, Mexico City was known 
for having some of the worst air pollution in the 
world (WHO and UNEP 1992). The city’s public 
transportation system was suffering from safety 
issues, coordination problems, bad fleet management, 
and high GHG-emitting vehicles (Mejía-Dugand et 
al. 2013). As more and more people bought private 
cars, air pollution worsened. Attempts to regulate 
full-size buses (the “pulp-bus” system) through a 
project called “Route 100” fell flat, and the lack of an 
integrated transportation policy hampered the city’s 
ability to provide basic transportation services to its 
growing population (Villasana and Gómez 2017). 
To address both the environmental and service 
provision problems of a failing transport system, 
Mexico City’s former secretary of environment 
began to explore options for improving mobility in 
Mexico City. One such option was a program called 
Pro Air III (ProAire III), which was introduced by 
the secretary and aimed to improve air quality in the 
greater metropolitan area between 2002 and 2010 
(Secretaría de Ambiente de la Ciudad de México 
2002). This project included improvements to 
infrastructure, bus technology, transit management, 
and an updated fare system, and it became a model 
for other transit-related projects in the city. 
It was in this context that a transport advisor to the 
Shell Foundation and former director of the Climate, 
Energy, and Pollution Program of WRI won a grant in 
2002 from the Shell Foundation to create a project on 
sustainable transport in Mexico and other developing 
countries in which WRI worked. This opportunity 
emerged partly due to personal and professional 
connections based on trust and merit—Mexico 
City’s secretary of environment was his former PhD 
student (CCC 2013)—and partly due to the growing 
focus in Mexico City on sustainable mobility. 
Under this new grant from Shell, WRI had the 
option of either creating a new organization in 
Mexico City or giving a subgrant to a preexisting 
organization. WRI decided to go with the second 
option, based on its close relationship with the 
founding director of the Interdisciplinary Center 
of Biodiversity and Environment (CeIBA), a 
Mexican nonprofit civil organization focused 
on environmental research. CTS was initially 
supported by CeIBA and WRI, and early operations 
were managed by CeIBA’s previous coordinator 
of international relations (Interviews 2018).
CTS began the project by bringing public transit 
experts into Mexico. Specialists who had planned 
and implemented BRT systems in Bogotá, Colombia, 
and Curitiba, Brazil, came to the city to share their 
experiences and knowledge (more information on 
BRT systems and Latin American experiences can 
be found in Appendix D). These initial collaborations 
led to five mobility project proposals, from which two 
BRT corridor projects emerged: Ermita-Iztapalapa 
and Insurgentes Avenue16 (Interviews 2018). 
In August 2004, Mexico City’s head of government 
officially announced the launch of a new BRT 
project called Metrobús. One corridor, Insurgentes 
Avenue, was chosen because of the large impact 
the route would have on transit in the city and 
the possibilities it created for expansions off the 
route, and because negotiations to develop along 
this avenue were more politically feasible than 
elsewhere (CCC 2013). Implementation of BRT along 
Insurgentes Avenue was rushed to completion before 
the former mayor left his post to run for national 
election in 2006 (Lopezobrador.org.mx 2018). 
CTS’s role in this first BRT line was primarily that 
of technical assistance, which included overseeing 
planning, negotiations, and project implementation. 
CTS modeled this first line after Bogotá’s successful 
TransMilenio system, which served as an excellent 
example of an effective urban BRT system. Although, 
planners in Mexico City failed to leave enough leeway 
in the project to adapt to local challenges, leading 
to early setbacks in the project (CCC 2013). Rushing 
to implement a brand-new project to meet a 2006 
deadline led to several technical failures, which

Following its early success, Metrobús has slowly 
expanded across Mexico City to include seven BRT 
lines (see Figures 4 and 5). The strategy to develop 
Mexico City’s BRT system has been consistent 
over time, which is at least partly attributable to 
the long tenure of the director of Metrobús from 
2004 to 2018 (Hernández 2018). CTS’s involvement 
in the project diminished over time: the intense 
technical support needed at the beginning of the 
project faded as the implementation process became 
more systematic and Metrobús was able to operate 
as its own entity through ongoing learning. 19
Today, Metrobús is the largest BRT network in Latin 
America, with 140 km of dedicated bus lanes and a 
fleet of 688 buses, 9 of which are hybrids and 90 of 
which meet Euro VI standards (Metrobus.cdmx.gob.
mx 2018).20 The Metrobús system has proved durable 
and popular in Mexico City: According to a user 
survey conducted in 2015, 67 percent of riders were 
satisfied with Metrobús, compared to 35 percent who 
were satisfied with microbuses (an informal urban 
transportation system based on independently owned, 
often adapted from light commercial vehicle buses, 
operating in the city) (CTS EMBARQ Mexico 2015). 
As can be seen in Figure 9, the Metrobús system 
saves travelers time, which helps to improve city-wide 
productivity. The system is also cheaper than other 
mass transit modes such as the metro in terms of 
infrastructure and operation, which reduces the cost 
burden on lower and middle-income groups sensitive 
to even small fare changes (EMBARQ n.d., 59–66).
As the former mayor of Mexico City said:
Metrobús is one of the most successful initiatives 
that has affected mobility in Mexico City. It is 
the basis for a city-wide strategy to change the 
city’s mobility, which for more than four decades 
has prioritized private vehicles. We now have the 
challenge of continuing to provide [the Metrobús] 
service, and to improve and expand it(WRI 2011).
SCALING THE BRT SYSTEM BEYOND 
MEXICO CITY
From its involvement in the Metrobús project, CTS 
built up its expertise on BRT and sustainable transit 
more broadly, allowing the organization to attract 
funding for similar projects and scale its efforts 
across Mexico. Its strong relationship with the 
Mexico City government preceded close work with 
other local government officials in different cities. 
Some of the local government officials with whom 
CTS worked would join the organization, allowing 
for more direct knowledge and skill sharing across 
the public-nonprofit network in Mexico.21 As CTS 
expanded its work across the country, the organization 
embraced the complexity of BRT systems and grew 
adept at navigating the technical, cultural, and 
political challenges that came with implementing new 
transit systems in cities. 22 Eventually CTS and later 
CTS-EMBARQ focused on training local authorities 
in the planning process for implementing BRT 
systems, including designing concessions, creating 
effective management and governance structures, 
and strengthening bus operators’ rights, technical 
capacity, and representation in the system.
In 2007, CTS participated in the implementation 
process of the Macrobús BRT system of Guadalajara, 
Mexico’s second-largest city, providing key technical 
support and training over the course of three years. 
The expansion of BRT across Mexico revealed the need 
for federal financial and technical support for these 
projects as well as the need to create a national policy 
on public transit. With advice from the World Bank 
and CTS, the National Bank for Public Works and 
Services of Mexico (BANOBRAS) developed a proposal 
that would later result in the creation of PROTRAM, 
a financing program for mass transport projects 
in Mexico supported by the World Bank (Diario 
Oficial de la Federación 2008). PROTRAM increased 
interest from cities in implementing their own BRT 
systems and, since 2011, BANOBRAS has contracted 
CTS-EMBARQ and then WRI Mexico for ongoing 
support to advise, manage, and assess BRT projects 
in cities such as Chihuahua, Puebla, Monterrey, 
Culiacán, Oaxaca, Juarez City, Pachuca, Tijuana, 
Acapulco, León, and Mexico State. WRI Mexico has 
even provided more recent technical assistance to 
BRT projects internationally in Santiago, Chile; Dar 
es Salaam, Tanzania; and Matanza, Argentina.

LESSONS LEARNED FROM BRT

Given that road traffic is one of the main contributors 
to Mexico City’s GHG emissions, Metrobús has 
significantly reduced air pollution in the city, in line 
with its fundamental goal (Bel and Hols 2015). In 
total, considering available data on the reduction 
of emissions up to 2016, the six lines reduced CO2 
emissions by 143,952 tons since the start of line 1 
operations in 200523 (Figure 8) (Metrobus.cdmx.
gob.mx 2016). Along with this, according to the 
internal key performance indicator (KPI) calculations 
in 2017,24 travel times have also been reduced for 
commuters (Figure 9). The main benefit from the 
reduction of travel time for public transport users 
is the general improvement of living conditions and 
well-being, as commuters often travel long distances 
for daily activities such as work, study, and basic 
purchases, spending several hours in transportation. 
Mobility is one of the main challenges for Latin 
American populations and commuters in the Global 
South, a reflection of underlying inequalities. 
In 2009, actors involved in Metrobús, including 
the Mexico City government, Metrobús itself, CTS

Foundation, the Hewlett Foundation, and the Global

Environmental Award from the John F. Kennedy 
School of Government at Harvard University. This 
award recognized an “outstanding public-private 
partnership project that enhances environmental 
quality through the use of novel and creative 
approaches” (Harvard Gazette 2009). That same 
year, Metrobús was adopted as a model for mass 
transit projects as a Clean Development Mechanism 
within the United Nations Framework Convention 
on Climate Change (UNFCCC) (Secretaría de 
Ambiente de la Ciudad de México 2016, 87-88).25

LEARNING BY DOING: INSTITUTIONAL 
TRANSFORMATIONS POST-BRT
Documenting WRI Mexico’s expansion beyond its 
original BRT work in Mexico City helps to fulfill our 
first research goal of capturing and learning from 
the experiences in a deep dive city and also deepens 
our understanding of WRI Mexico’s role in affecting 
city-wide change in Mexico City. In this section, 
we discuss four projects that grew from the initial 
expertise on sustainable transport and broadened 
WRI Mexico’s approach to supporting sustainability in 
the city. Broadening WRI Ross Center’s institutional 
scope has brought transformations in methodologies, 
perspectives, and approaches, diversifying the staff’s 
expertise and experience to commit to a broader goal 
of urban sustainable development. Documenting 
institutional growth also helps us to understand 
the effectiveness of the deep dive method of long-
term and multisectoral engagement in a city, and 
how WRI can as an organization expand its scope of 
work from one catalytic urban project or challenge. 
In the Mexico context, this has not come without 
its challenges, which will be further discussed in 
the section ”From Project to City-Level Impact.” We 
hope to collect the experiential learning from these 
challenges to inform WRI Ross Center’s future work 
in deep dive cities. According to the methodology of 
this research, four strategic projects are presented 
to provide further evidence on institutional enablers 
and constraints: The first two projects we focus on 
stem directly from WRI Mexico’s BRT work—the 
Integrated Transport System (SIT) and coalition 
building around mobility. The second two projects we 
explore are the Building Efficiency Accelerator (BEA) 
and the Cooperation Action System (SAC) Tacubaya. 
These are catalytic energy and water projects, 
respectively, that contribute to WRI Mexico’s portfolio 
of work extending beyond mobility to include urban 
development more broadly by promoting internal 
expertise and external recognition in these areas.
THE INTEGRATED TRANSPORT  
SYSTEM (SIT)
When CTS conducted its first overall analysis of 
mobility in Mexico City in 2006, it proposed a SIT 
that connected different transport services like 
Metrobús, RTP, Metro, trolleybuses, conventional 
buses (peseros), and public bicycles (CTS EMBARQ 
Mexico 2015). This idea kept evolving in 2009, 
when CTS began collaborating with Bogotá’s 
TransMilenio project to promote an association 
of BRT systems in Latin America 26 and was 
included in the design and implementation of 
mass transit projects through PROTRAM. 27 
Besides having a solid foundation of expertise to 
implement BRT projects, the physical presence of a 
CTS office in Mexico City was a key enabling factor 
for the success of the Metrobús project. This allowed 
personal connections with local authorities to blossom, 
expanding funding opportunities and knowledge 
sharing between actors in the public transit space.

COALITION BUILDING
Convening external partners, civil society 
organizations, national and local governments, 
and private companies has long been part of WRI’s 
strategy for catalyzing and scaling change. Some 
of the coalitions with which the organization has 
been involved were established officially. The first 
coalition emerged in 2005, when the Hewlett 
Foundation brought together five different civil society 
organizations, including CTS, in an effort to create 
a community of urban mobility experts. Since then, 
other coalitions have emerged organically around 
two main topics: on the one hand, the collaboration 
between public authorities in different cities, and on 
the other hand, influencing public elections through 
providing information and shaping the public agenda.
On the first topic: the Connect Cities project was 
established in 2012. This network of city officials 
from across Latin America would come together 
under the Connect Cities group to share knowledge 
and experiences on urban issues with the goal of 
increasing institutional and technical capacity for 
urban sustainability work (WRIMexico.org 2018). The 
network of cities was initially led by the Institute of the 
National Housing Fund for Workers (INFONAVIT), 
which initially asked CTS to support and update urban 
development codes for local Mexican governments. 
Later, CTS created the Connect Cities brand, and 
since 2016 it has consisted of a variety of urban 
organizations, all of which have influenced WRI 
Mexico’s broadening scope of work.29 The coalition 
later received funding from the Shell Foundation, 
among others, though funding ended in 2018. The 
connections built through this network, however, have 
had lasting impact on WRI Mexico’s internal growth 
and development (WRIMexico.org 2019). For example, 
in 2017 a rise in oil prices generated social and political 
instability, and local governments requested WRI 
Mexico’s support to understand the causes in transport 
sector operations and to set the agenda to respond 
to the crisis. This led to the creation of the National 
Association of Mobility Authorities (AMAM), which 
comprises the participation of 28 Mexican states. 
Regarding the second topic: after raising awareness 
on sustainable mobility in the approach to the State 
of Mexico’s 2011 elections, CTS-EMBARQ worked 
to revive Hewlett’s 2005 coalition to create a new 
network of organizations working on sustainable 
urban mobility. From these efforts, Citizens with 
Vision was created in 2012 under the leadership of 
four partners and WRI, 30 plus support from the Civic 
Collaboration Center (CCC). This coalition included 
more than 110 organizations and individuals, making 
the ecosystem of organizations associated with WRI 
even more diverse (CTS EMBARQ Mexico et al. 2012). 
With financial support from Hewlett, Citizens with 
Vision was broadly intended to facilitate public-
private discourse on sustainable urban mobility, 
and its first outcome was the delivery of a mobility 
plan for Mexico City. In later years, the coalition 
submitted proposals for candidates in the 2015–16 
election cycle. In 2018, the coalition adopted the 
name Yo Me Muevo (I move) as part of a new outreach 
strategy aimed at advancing progressive policies at 
the local and national levels (see Appendices F and 
G for more information on the coalition’s history.31)
WRI Mexico’s involvement in both coalitions (Connect 
Cities and Yo Me Muevo) has helped the organization 
to broaden its sphere of influence and to learn 
best practices for effecting urban transformation. 
Bringing together partners in the nonprofit, private, 
and public sectors has been a strength of WRI 
Mexico and has allowed the organization to build 
and strengthen personal connections across its 
network of influence. In this sense, WRI Mexico has 
become socially adept at working across sectors and 
parties to implement projects and create impact.

THE COOPERATION ACTION SYSTEM 
(SAC) TACUBAYA
Though WRI Mexico has been intentional about 
expanding some areas of its work, others have 
come about more organically, arising from specific 
emergent needs. The SAC Tacubaya project pushed the 
organization into expanding its scope of work beyond 
the urban development and transportation projects 
portfolio. This urban redevelopment project supported 
by the Secretary of Housing and Urban Development 
(SEDUVI) opened doors for the organization to 
move into the water agenda, a program in which 
WRI Mexico continues to build expertise today.
In an effort to address different problems related to 
urban infrastructure provision and management, 
SEDUVI established a public trust for development 
and identified several pilot projects around the city 
to restore both natural and built infrastructure 
and improve quality of life for residents (Gaceta 
Oficial del Distrito Federal 2010).34 Mexico City’s 
Tacubaya neighborhood occupies a centrally located 
and highly dynamic area where multiple lines of 
transit intersect and a large number residents, 
commuters, and informal settlement dwellers 
interact (SEDUVI 2018, 36). Due to its centrality and 
transportation infrastructure, this neighborhood 
was a strong candidate for a redevelopment pilot—
it faced a multitude of challenges such as crime, 
poverty, informal housing, unemployment, and 
depopulation/displacement but also possessed 
significant potential to implement solutions 
that could benefit all the actors involved.
Around 2015, WRI Mexico was invited to conduct a 
sociodemographic analysis of the Tacubaya area as 
well as a thorough review of previous development 
projects to create an integrated urban development 
proposal that incorporated principles such as transit-
oriented development. An important outcome of this 
analysis was the identification of water availability 
as a major constraint to promoting densification in 
the area, in a TOD model redevelopment plan.   
The timing of this urban redevelopment project 
coincided with a project led by the WRI Global Water 
Program, on natural infrastructure in Monterrey—
the Green-Gray Assessment for Water Security .35 
The FEMSA Foundation, which was involved in this 
project, decided to take a step forward and fund 
the water analysis and proposal portion of the SAC 
Tacubaya project, creating space for knowledge 
development in the water space within WRI Mexico. 
In total, WRI has presented 12 actions to advance 
towards Tacubaya’s sustainable development, 
whose implementation must be approved by the 
Water System of Mexico City (SACMEX). From 
this experience it has become clear that aligning 
different actors around a common development 
goal is key to effecting change. Governments 
are well placed to set standards for access and 
sustainability of basic services (such as water), and 
private companies should support the regulated use 
of precious natural resources (Interviews 2018). 
WRI Mexico’s work in the water sector is still in its 
early stages; the process of building up expertise and 
a reputation for delivering results in Mexico takes 
time. Much can be learned from earlier experiences 
in the transport and urban development sectors, 
in which WRI Mexico has learned how to build 
trust, influenced public policy at local and federal 
levels, pursued diverse financing mechanisms, 
and expanded its technical capacity. Part of this 
institutional growth into new areas of work is a 
result of the deep dive commitment to long-term 
engagement in Mexico City and in the country 
in general, allowing the organization to diversify 
its portfolio of work while maintaining a strong 
reputation in the sustainable development field.

IMPROVEMENTS TO CITY-WIDE 
ACCESSIBILITY USING TRANSIT MODES
In an ideal city, all residents should easily be able 
to access services and opportunities like jobs, 
healthcare, and schools. Many transport systems 
provide the means of accessing these services and 
opportunities, yet many cities are experiencing a 
decline in accessibility due to a confluence of rapid 
urbanization, an increase in private car ownership, 
and a lack of public transport coverage in the 
periphery. Measuring accessibility is critical to 
understanding the effects of improved transport 
on the health and prosperity of a city as a whole.
In this section, we present accessibility analysis 
conducted in Mexico City. It was based on the average 
number of opportunities (proxied as formal jobs) a 
person can reach in 30-minute trips from different 
parts of the city, through single or multimodal trips 
walking and/or via mass public transit, from origin 
to destination.36 This will help us to get an idea of 
the changes and improvements achieved in levels 
of accessibility as they relate to WRI Ross Center’s 
work on the Metrobús project and improvements 
in public transport.37 We consider accessibility an 
important metric for measuring city-wide impact of 
a transit project, as access to employment, education, 
health, recreation, and other opportunities is the

34      |      WRI.ORG
main need to which public transport responds 
(Venter et al. 2019). It should be acknowledged that 
these calculations represent a conservative estimate, 
as they do not capture informal jobs, and informal 
transit—important in hard-to-reach areas—is not 
captured. In addition, there remain other external 
factors such as economic growth for which we have not 
controlled (more details are presented in Appendix B).
We define accessibility as the ease with which urban 
dwellers can reach opportunities and services. 38 
Accessibility depends on the effective use of land in the 
city and a good transport system that connects people 
to opportunities. Accessibility takes into account 
the spatial distribution of people and opportunities 
within a certain amount of time and evaluates the 
performance of the current transport modes based 
on their ability to take people where they want to go.

feelings of safety and comfort on the transit system.

As WRI Mexico has grown, its ability to remain 
nimble enough to adapt to changing political and legal

Navarrete 2015). Norms and legal institutions are not 
always stable, and laws do not guarantee continuity

levels, respectively, all public officials are changed 
and a new strategy is set, affecting significantly 
the government’s continuity and coherence. 
It is in this context that WRI Mexico has expanded 
its reach and has been integrated into WRI Global, 
which presents the challenge of adopting the 
planning and procedures of a large international 
NGO, while maintaining the capacity to respond 
to windows of opportunity and overcome barriers

CONCLUSIONS
WRI Mexico’s work in Mexico City, especially in the 
mobility sector, provides a base of experience to draw 
out insights on how to catalyze change at the city 
level (see the definition of change in the introduction 
and discussion of terms in Appendix H). From our 
accessibility, social mapping, and institutional 
strategy analysis, it is possible to trace WRI Ross 
Center’s work process and its city-wide effects, 
beginning with the Metrobús project, and what the 
organization has learned from its experience. Between 
2004 and 2014 overall measured accessibility in the 
city rose by 77 percent. These years corresponded 
to Mexico City’s partnership with and reliance on 
WRI Mexico for technical and managerial support 
in implementing Metrobús lines 1–5, adopting 
new regulations through the promulgation of the 
Mobility Law, and adoption of DOTS principles, 
among other policies and transport infrastructure 
changes at the time. These were also years in which 
WRI Mexico (then CTS and EMBARQ) grew as an 
organization, taking on new projects while remaining 
institutionally flexible and independent in order to 
respond to emerging opportunities. WRI Mexico 
has also expanded its network of organizational 
contacts over the years, through personnel movement, 
funding relationships, partnerships, and coalitions 
with whom WRI is involved. This network has 
expanded the organization’s reach and impact, 
through collaboration and coalition building, to 
advance sustainability causes in the country. 
LESSONS LEARNED FROM WRI 
MEXICO’S DEEP DIVE APPROACH 
This paper has documented and analyzed important 
experiences from WRI Mexico’s long-term 
engagement in Mexico City, beginning with its deep 
involvement in the Metrobús project. Some lessons 
that we can draw from these experiences and from 
our city-wide impact analysis will help to inform 
WRI Ross Center’s future work in other deep dive 
cities and enrich institutional learning processes.
The lessons that emerge from this deep dive research 
can be classified into three key groups: the importance 
of building trust with external partners, funders, and 
government; the critical role that public policy plays 
in institutionalizing and scaling innovative projects 
and ideas; and the importance of institutional and 
financial flexibility, which allows an organization to 
respond to emerging opportunities while pursuing 
a long-term strategy of sustainable development.

RECOMMENDATIONS FOR OTHER WRI 
DEEP DIVE CITIES
Developing WRI’s deep dive approach is an 
ongoing process. The hope is to continue to build 
on the legacy of CTS, EMBARQ, and WRI Mexico 
to expand the organization’s scope of work in 
Mexico City and achieve broader impact. From 
the lessons learned, we recommend the following 
actions to strengthen the deep dive work across 
the WRI network of international offices:
 ▪ Build on the strengths of local staff already 
present in WRI’s international offices to 
both foster a sense of ownership over deep dive 
work and craft a locally contextualized impact 
strategy. Though borrowing from WRI Global 
resources, systems, and processes opens doors 
for international offices, localization is key to 
avoid the challenge of “global NGO imperialism.”  
 ▪ Be transparent about project management, 
fund allocation, and personnel roles  
to ensure an environment of trust between 
staff and management. This also includes 
openness when projects are not going well and 
recognition of achievements when they are.
 ▪ Use flexible funds strategically to respond 
to emerging opportunities. One of WRI’s 
goals outlined in the latest strategic plan is 
to “act with more agility, accept more risk 
and manage it better” (Wri.org 2018). This is 
especially important in deep dive cities, and all 
international offices working in deep dive cities 
should work to bolster their store of flexible funds. 
 ▪ Establish internal mechanisms for 
collaboration between different teams to 
avoid work fragmentation and to tap into expertise 
across the WRI network. As WRI Mexico’s 
scope of work continues to expand, coordination 
across teams as well as with other international 
offices will be crucial to building internal 
capacity and creating integrated solutions.
 ▪ Create better systems to track, monitor, 
and measure projects and their impact in 
different cities. We hope that this document 
and the accompanying appendices will help to 
advance WRI’s institutional goal of becoming a 
learning organization that turns big ideas into 
action. This also includes relying on available, 
accurate, and systematized project information 
that would allow WRI Ross Center to conduct 
ongoing research in deep dive cities so as to 
understand a city’s needs (especially for vulnerable 
populations), predict windows of opportunity, and 
analyze political-economic factors that influence 
projects’ potential impact. The development of 
this document was a first approach to gathering, 
showcasing, and collecting lessons learned. 
 ▪ Strive for systemic and transformational 
change. With the urgency of climate change 
and the growing role that cities will have in 
tackling it, the organization needs to focus 
on pursuing integrated, innovative solutions 
that both catalyze change (like the Metrobús 
project) and reverberate across systems, 
political administrations, and economies. 
 ▪ Be proactive in engaging politically. 
Building on trust with actors and stakeholders 
of all types has allowed WRI Mexico to explore 
important environmental issues and policies 
across the political spectrum before, during, 
and after elections. Collaborating with political 
actors and maintaining public relations are key 
enabling conditions for action and impact.
 ▪ Be realistic about reaching institutional 
goals and objectives, through the 
establishment of achievable and measurable 
indicators to track impact and document 
experiences, while acknowledging the urgency of 
WRI Ross Center’s mission and the underlying 
high ambition to pursue transformative changes.
WRI Mexico will work to internalize these 
recommendations and promote them across WRI’s 
ever-expanding network. With the right tools and 
support mechanisms in place, WRI international 
offices are well-placed to engage in deep dive cities in 
order to catalyze systemic and transformative change. 
From this paper and other deep dive research we hope 
to continue to learn and refine WRI Ross Center’s 
practice to effect change that has the widest possible 
impact while documenting key decisions, actors, and 
processes that led to it. This is necessary to ensure 
livable and prosperous cities for generations to come.

Appendix A. General Methodology 
INTRODUCTION
This appendix lays out a method to capture and analyze WRI Ross 
Center’s deep dive engagement approach: document the process and 
actions taken, present and analyze evidence of changes that occurred, 
and learn from this analysis. The case piloted in this publication is Mexico 
City. The analysis will be used by the teams to define WRI Ross Center’s 
strategy and learn from the experience. We identify strides towards 
progress and missteps that set us back. Given WRI’s influence in the 
past, we hope to better understand and develop tools and methods to 
document the Center’s work, analyze, measure results when possible, 
and expand the organization’s impact in contributing to transformative 
change in the city.
Our thesis is that the deep dive method allows us to achieve more with 
a less expansive and flexible approach to working. This appendix will lay 
out a method to apply to so that we “tell the story” of WRI’s way of work-
ing in deep dive cities, and the resulting influence and impact towards 
city-wide change. WRI believes that transformative change in cities is 
the result of interdependent actions and efforts, with changes in a part 
of the city influencing other sectors. The practice note will capture what 
and how was done, and the results towards creating more sustainable, 
equal and economically productive cities. An additional component of 
WRI Ross Center’s work will include a focus on alignment, engagement, 
and collaboration, and how this has contributed to the Center’s way of 
working in its deep dive examples. 
THE WRI CONTEXT
WRI is acknowledged for conveying and engaging enabling partners, 
coalition building, and influencing policy (Sewell et al. 2016), complex 
processes embedded on the ground and policy-driven projects that oc -
cur over varying timeframes. The WRI Ross Center for Sustainable Cities, 
which grew out of WRI’s EMBARQ program, undertakes specific projects 
in this vein, but also pursues deep engagement in a small number of 
cities. These “deep dive” cities are typically the location of an office and 
a portfolio of projects beyond one sector, and have some funding that is 
flexible. These non-project-based funds are used to maintain relation -
ships and take advantage of short-fuse opportunities that arise from 
them. This appendix lays out a methodology for capturing the impact of 
this approach in any of WRI’s deep dive cities.
The substance and objectives of projects WRI undertakes across geogra -
phies are very similar (e.g., supporting implementation of a BRT corridor 
or bike lanes, working with entrepreneurs to improve last mile connectiv -
ity, or driving a national/regional policy reform), but the process followed 
to achieve an outcome may be quite different. What works in one country 
would not necessarily work in another, and there can be differences 
even within countries on what will work to move cities forward towards 
sustainability. The steps followed, obstacles faced, and lessons learned 
by WRI staff in these processes have been seldom captured and are 
consequently not well-defined internally or externally. 
The tacit knowledge from experience is crucial to how WRI Ross Center 
tells its story, tracks its progress and impact, learns from successes 
and challenges, and delivers in the future, as well as to convince 
potential funders of the organization’s value proposition. Over the longer 
term, this allows WRI to instill a cultural shift in the Cities program by 
identifying and cultivating practices to record important processes and 
engagements, a culture of learning, and a richer narrative of the Center’s 
project portfolio.
This document is part of a pilot effort to document its deep dive ap -
proach, structure a methodology to explore results, and begin to better 
understand and strengthen WRI Ross Center’s work on transformative 
change triggered by interventions in deep engagement cities where the 
organization has worked for several years across multiple sectors using

other changes throughout the city, the mechanisms and processes used,

Appendix B. Accessibility 
In an ideal city, all residents should be able to easily reach their desti -
nations. Transport systems should be designed and operated to grant 
people’s access to satisfiers, allowing them to participate in activities 
distributed over space and time. 
Yet geographic and transport realities often prevent this access, reveal -
ing an accessibility division amongst different transport modes, and the 
people who can afford them. Measuring accessibility can thus provide us 
with an objective way to understand and measure these differences. This 
appendix covers the definition of this concept and the methodology used 
to assess it in this paper. 
Accessibility can be defined as people’s ease to reach valued goods, 
services, destinations, and activities located in an area, whether urban or 
rural, in order to meet their needs and desires (Bertaud 2014). Acces -
sibility measures depict the collective performance of land use and 
transport systems, determining how well that complex system serves its 
residents. It can be measured for: i) various transport modes; ii) different 
types of destinations; iii) different individuals or groups of people; and 
iv) diverse activities happening during different days of the week and 
times of the day.
In practical terms, this means that accessibility will be influenced not 
only by the transport systems, but also by the spatial organization of 
a city, and how well the former works to interconnect housing, jobs, 
services, and other urban satisfiers geographically defined by the latter. 
Connectivity measurements, on the other hand, focus mainly on the ur -
ban grid and street network structure, failing to provide a comprehensive 
representation of a city’s ability to solve access. 
To perform the accessibility analysis, we use the Open Trip Planner 
Analyst (OTPA), which allows us to assess city-level impacts of transport 
projects (Conveyal 2015). The regional analysis shows the average 
accessibility that each location in the city experiences in a time cutoff. 
This analysis is a good way to compare and visualize the relative levels of 
accessibility for different parts of the city, and to identify areas that may 
have gained unexpected improvements in accessibility thanks to the 
network effects. It also allows us to assess the city-wide impact in the 
levels of accessibility with the different transport investments. 
Resulting maps provide us with a very useful way to visualize and 
identify the impacts in accessibility levels throughout the city. However, 
it is also necessary to quantify these impacts and aggregate them into 
a measure that is easily comparable between scenarios, and cities. To 
produce these city-wide aggregate measures, we use a concept that 
was developed by the urban economist Alain Bertaud  (2014) in which the 
access measures for each grid are aggregated to the region of the city 
studied with a population-weighted average. 
This number gives us the average number of jobs accessible to all 
residents within the region in question, which means that a resident 
can reach a certain percentage of the jobs available in the metropolitan 
area within a specific amount of time. It gives us one single number that 
can be comparable throughout time and for benchmarking across cities. 
However, we know that one single number can be deceiving given the 
size of any location, and because people and jobs are not evenly spread 
out throughout the region, therefore covering up potential variation in 
accessibility throughout the region. 
BACKGROUND AND CONTEXT 
Access to opportunity is a goal not often achieved : As we discussed 
before, convenient access to preferred destinations should be a main 
asset in a metropolitan area. Empirical evidence, however, points to 
significant differences in accessibility across different demographic 
groups. People residing in different geographic areas have different 
levels of access to jobs, health services, or education centers; likewise, 
the ability to use certain transport modes (such as private automobiles) 
have dramatic effects on the ability to reach all these different types of 
destinations, revealing an accessibility-divide  amongst different transport 
modes, and the people who can afford them. Measuring accessibility 
can thus provide an objective way of understanding and measuring 
these differences.
Accessibility can then be defined as people’s ease to reach valued 
goods, services, destinations and activities located in an area, 
whether urban or rural, that they must reach in order to meet 
their needs and desires. 44 These destinations can also be viewed as 
the number of opportunities that a person has, constrained by how far 
they are willing and able to travel (quality of transport options), and how 
much they are willing to pay (travel time and costs). Therefore, measuring 
accessibility means measuring collective performance of land use and 
transport systems, and determining how well that complex system 
serves its residents. It can be measured for: i) various transport modes; 
ii) diversity of destinations; iii) different individuals or groups of people; 
and iv) diverse activities happening during different days of the week and 
times of the day. Mathematically, accessibility in its simplest form can

Moving beyond traditional approaches: It’s important to note that the 
conventional approach to appraise transport projects has traditionally fo -
cused on travel time savings and congestion relief. However, researchers 
have challenged this assumption (Metz 2011) and argue that using travel 
time savings as the main measure to evaluate transport investments is 
faulty, and accessibility should be an outcome measured by transport 
investments. It has become critical to measure how new transport proj -
ects/investments add up to the overall accessibility of the city, instead of 
performance measurements of improvements at the corridor level in an 
isolated way. It is also important to disaggregate these impacts for the 
different socio-demographic groups, differentiating whenever possible 
by age, gender, income-level, and other relevant subgroups. 
Moving beyond connectivity to accessibility : Another important 
consideration is that accessibility is not the same as connectivity nor 
proximity to transit. These last two concepts provide only a partial view 
of what accessibility means (Figure B1). Connectivity measures the 
capability to connect places, regardless of their importance (density 
is irrelevant, for example). It considers distance from point A to point B, 
and the properties of connections and networks (shape, grid density, 
frequency of modes, speed, etc.), but not people’s location nor their 
preferred destinations. Proximity to transit or transit coverage, on the 
other hand, is a metric that focuses on the share of the population that 
can access the public transport network by walking, therefore measuring 
people’s proximity to the nearest transit stop, regardless of where or 
when it takes them. 
Accessibility incorporates to the analysis the same attributes as connec -
tivity (how easy it is for someone to travel from A to B), and people near 
transit (by incorporating the travel time of walking) but takes the analysis 
a little further by incorporating spatial distribution and destination of 
people, and evaluates the performance of the current transport modes 
based on their ability to bring people to where they want to go. Acces -
sibility will be influenced by transport systems, spatial distribution of a 
city, and how well these two components interconnect. This is important 
given that constraints related to computational power and access to data 
have made measuring connectivity or proximity to transit easier and a 
more prevalent practice, requiring less resources and technical capacity 
than measuring accessibility. 
However, with today’s decreasing computational power costs, these 
constraints are no longer a major hurdle, and it is possible to perform 
computations quickly enough to allow interactive exploration of transport 
and land use scenarios, as well as changes to the accessibility indica -
tors themselves.

is meaningless unless people are using these transport services as 
enablers to access other opportunities (e.g., jobs, education centers, 
and other amenities). The network effect produced by various transport 
modes in any city is greater than the benefits from each individual 
mode. Applying this model means analyzing the entire public transport 
network, including all transport modes for which data were available. 
This allows us to capture network effect positive externalities in terms of 
improved access.

The data presented in this practice note display a set of city-wide regional

accessibility with Metrobús’ investments and improvements. The travel 
time cut-off used for the analyses presented in this paper is 30 minutes.

POPULATION-WEIGHTED AGGREGATE MEASURES AND 
CUMULATIVE IMPACTS 
Maps provide us a useful way to visualize and identify the impacts in 
accessibility levels throughout the city. However, it is also necessary to 
quantify what these impacts are and aggregate them into a measure 
that is easily comparable between scenarios, and even between different 
cities. To produce these city-wide aggregate measures, we use a concept 
that was developed by the urban economist Alain Bertaud (2014) that 
aggregates the access measures for each grid to the city being analyzed 
with a population-weighted average. 47 This number gives us the average 
number of jobs accessible to all residents within the city, which means 
that on average, a resident within the city can reach X percent of the jobs 
available in the area within X minutes of travel.

LIMITATIONS
Despite accessibility studies becoming more widespread in the trans -
portation field, they are only as good as the data they use. While the 
Conveyal tool is optimized to take full value of GTFS feeds and OSM lay -
outs, the challenges of data in the Global South, as well as the economic 
structures, make calculations less accurate and reliable than similar 
analysis in the Global North. Population data are collected every 10 years, 
while the economic census is produced every 5 years, those two not 
corresponding.  The prevalence of informal jobs means that reliance on 
formal job data, such as the one used in this analysis, is missing large 
portions of economic reality. Likewise, the importance of informal transit 
means this analysis fails to capture much of the movement happening 
in the city.  Calculations and maps provided should be considered 
broad estimates for patterns of movement, access, and challenges, but 
do not accurately capture the public transport availability and reach 
in Mexico City.

Appendix D. BRT systems in Latin America
BRT’s development in Latin America concurred with economic changes 
in the region: after a period of protectionism that led to severe crisis 
during the 1980s, a process of liberalization transformed the market and 
generated growth in the service sector, giving the state a secondary role 
and favoring private initiative (Figueroa 2005, 41-47). Within this context, 
BRT emerged as a joint effort of private and public entities to improve 
public transportation.
Moreover, it represented a low-cost and low-carbon solution for urban 
mobility, addressing some of the main challenges of Latin American 
cities due to rapid urban growth: an increase of private cars, traffic 
congestion, and the need to reach longer distances in cities. Its initial 
success in Curitiba, Brazil (Lindau et al. 2010), and further adoption in Bo -
gotá, Colombia (Sandoval and Hidalgo 2012), made those cities become a 
reference for sustainable public transport, leading to paradigm shifts in 
public transport, public space, and other dimensions of urban planning 
(Hidalgo and Huizenga 2012).

Appendix E. Expansion of the Metrobús System from One to Seven Lines 
In 2007, the planning process of the southward extension of line 1 of 
Metrobús began, along with the challenge of route fragmentation; 
contractual restrictions led the government to establish two different 
concessions, forcing users to transfer mid-route. CTS participated direct -
ly in negotiations, showing the effects of fragmentation on operations 
and quality of service. Finally, in March 2008, the south corridor of line 1 
started operating as a continuous route, adopting bi-articulated buses 
and using less-polluting fuel. The extent of the line is seen in Figure E1. 
During the same year, financial crisis and centralization of fiscal resourc -
es in Mexico pushed the government to look for alternative financing 
through public-private partnerships for the second line. New challenges 
stemmed from the multiple routes in the corridor; five transport compa -
nies were involved in negotiations, making the process more complex 
and impeding the creation of a unified administrative structure for the 
maintenance of the fleet, fuel purchases, and driver training. Additionally, 
the success of line 1 produced a general desire among transport opera -
tors to join the Metrobús project.
Although there were high expectations, this line was about to have the 
same difficulties as the southward extension of the previous line, with 
dangerous transfers due to the fragmentation of the corridor. Once 
more, CTS supported the Secretary of Mobility with technical analysis, 
reaching an agreement to start operations in December 2009. Since then, 
Metrobús has achieved connectivity in all the lines of the Metro system.

Appendix G. Yo Me Muevo’s 
Transformations and Further Steps
In 2011, WRI Mexico produced a Mobility Plan for the Metropolitan Area of 
the Valley of Mexico that included eight agreements on mobility around 
three topics: urban development, public urban transport, and public 
space and nonmotorized mobility. As it was an adaptable model to other 
contexts, especially Mexican cities characterized by low planning regard -
ing mobility and public transport, organizations from other states came 
to WRI Mexico to develop similar processes in cities such as Guadalajara, 
with the participation of cyclist groups, and reaching a direct influence 
on candidates for heads of government, mayors, and even deputies of 
different states throughout the country. 
This again shows how work begun in Mexico City and expanded to other 
cities reaching a wider result for 2015–16’s elections, and more recently, 
the project relaunched as Yo Me Muevo for the 2018 elections. This 
change also brought about a transformation in the participation on the 
agenda by candidates as an attempt to guarantee visible outcomes from 
influencing the public agenda. Before, candidates could sign online and 
there were no monitoring mechanisms to determine the accomplishment 
of the agreements, while the new modality has a deep commitment and 
process to sign onto the agenda.
During 2018’s electoral period, 75 percent of candidates in Mexico 
City signed the agenda, and not only mass media but also politicians 
themselves have shown their interest in knowing the real impact of these 
actions, beyond commitment. Further steps would be creating monitor -
ing and evaluation mechanisms and documenting successful cases to 
develop a stronger process of accountability and transparency in public 
administration. It would also be important to provide technical support 
to the elected public officials who, as candidates had signed the agenda 
to maintain and spread the path of change through WRI’s technical 
expertise, for which funds are required. 
Appendix H. WRI Mexico’s Staff Discussion 
of Transformational Change
One of the main objectives of this document is to understand WRI 
Mexico’s impact at city-level regarding the concepts of transformational 
change. According to Janus’ second step on capturing lessons and 
experiences, this appendix provides an open discussion on WRI’s theory 
of change by reporting and analyzing key staff opinions on the matter, 
especially former and present directive members of WRI Mexico’s staff, 
who have been involved in decision-making processes on CTS,  
CTS-EMBARQ, and WRI Mexico’s evolution and projects (see the  
Interview list in References).
The complexity of defining transformational change can be summarized 
in the following words: “Change has ups and downs, successes and fail -
ures, it’s not a line. You have generated a great energy of change and have 
made many systems move from there. If that’s transformational change, I 
think we have achieved it in some sectors. […] The issue is to narrow what 
is transformational change. You put a motor that moves the system, but as 
every good motor of change, it has its limits of temporal scope.”
The meeting point between the different perspectives lies in the 
existence of a change that generates further change, which means that 
actions become triggers, catalysts, or seeds for wider transformations at 
a system level, referring sometimes to systemic change. Considering the 
city’s complexity and CTS’s previous focus on mobility, it is possible to 
see deep change in the mobility system when observing Metrobús and 
its stemming projects, mostly because of a change in the understanding 
of mobility and the emergence of a new cultural perspective to approach 
related issues: “The first great change is that today in Mexico City we don’t 
talk about transport, but we talk about mobility.”
For example, Metrobús in Mexico City has demonstrated the feasibility of 
innovative projects regarding public transport, thus generating hope and 
trust among the public and private sectors, plus civil society, as well as 
obtaining international positive reactions.
“When you can demonstrate that you can make changes, you generate 
that consistency which gives trust on what you can do, and that opens 
doors to generate new niches of transformative change, which doesn’t 
mean that it’s easy or that it’s guaranteed, but you have the door of trust 
open again, and that is very valuable.”
Metrobús’ outcomes reveal the necessity of long-term actions in order 
to achieve significant changes, which introduces an essential variable 
when approaching systemic change: time. A systemic or transfor -
mational change cannot be experienced in a short period; it requires 
continuity to generate a real shift in the know-how of processes. It also 
requires a wider perspective that includes technical, political, social, 
and cultural elements. Therefore, time is needed to experience real and 
perdurable changes regarding visible outcomes and deep structural 
transformations.

“We can only achieve transformational change if we are capable of gener-
ating processes or projects that break the threads, that sectoral approach, 
and can translate that break into different scales: into a punctual scale 
with punctual benefits and individuals, but under an intellectual reflection 
that allows to scale it into a public policy which really changes things.” 
Some interviewees discussed the centrality of institutional long-term 
transformations to reach transformational change, since institutions are 
in charge of determining the structure of a system. This explains the need 
for trained and skilled staff members to guide the process of adopting 
and applying technological advances. When addressing WRI’s work in 
cities, one staff member said:
“A smart city is one in which technology is at the service of people, from its 
aware and responsible use, for which trained institutions are required to 
use it efficiently for responding to big urban challenges.”
Therefore, institutional changes imply continued and sustained 
transformation, beyond temporary political shifts, unsustained or short-
term innovations, and interventions. Key political actors prove to be 
decisive to trigger and maintain the path of change of institutions and 
their viewpoint, considering that “You cannot build up projects without 
political influence.”
For example, Metrobús and SIT were projects that required engagement 
with the same institutions but, due to separate time and different people 
involved, led to different results. As one of the interviewees said: “The 
environment weighs too heavily on what we can or cannot do.” 
Metrobús counted on the former Secretary of Environment’s leadership, 
who acted as the “champion” of the project, the main promotor and sup-
porter in charge of generating commitment and trust in the project (Rizvi 
2014). This role becomes fundamental at the moment of implementing 
innovative solutions and generating transformational changes, so you 
need a leader with the capacity of gathering and coordinating political, 
technical, and financial support, as well as the ability to use windows of 
opportunity and persevere despite difficulties (Howell and Higgins 1991). 
The SIT did not have such a champion.
To sum up, WRI’s impact has only reached the change of mobility system 
and the integral vision of this system of urban development issues, 
seeing DOTS/TOD as a multiplier, complementary, or trickle-down effect 
of the development of public transport (Zamorano 2018). Technical 
and political supervision is required to guarantee long-term, coherent, 
and equitable transformations beyond temporary and limited actions. 
Therefore, a transformational change would imply the appropriation of 
an integrated vision of urban, social, and environmental problems, which 
needs a wider perspective towards national and global relevant matters 
in order to provide systemic solutions through the promotion of collab -
orative and interdisciplinary work. Until now, WRI’s work has proved to 
have a considerable impact in the evolution of the city and the ecosys -
tem around sustainable objectives, although there is more to be done to 
achieve transformational change at city-level in Mexico City. Figure H1

ABOUT WRI
World Resources Institute is a global research organization that turns big 
ideas into action at the nexus of environment, economic opportunity, and 
human well-being.
Our Challenge
Natural resources are at the foundation of economic opportunity and 
human well-being. But today, we are depleting Earth’s resources at rates 
that are not sustainable, endangering economies and people’s lives. 
People depend on clean water, fertile land, healthy forests, and a stable 
climate. Livable cities and clean energy are essential for a sustainable 
planet. We must address these urgent, global challenges this decade. 
Our Vision
We envision an equitable and prosperous planet driven by the wise 
management of natural resources. We aspire to create a world where the 
actions of government, business, and communities combine to eliminate 
poverty and sustain the natural environment for all people.
Our Approach
COUNT IT 
We start with data. We conduct independent research and draw on the 
latest technology to develop new insights and recommendations. Our  
rigorous analysis identifies risks, unveils opportunities, and informs smart  
strategies. We focus our efforts on influential and emerging economies 
where the future of sustainability will be determined.
CHANGE IT 
We use our research to influence government policies, business strategies, 
and civil society action. We test projects with communities, companies, 
and government agencies to build a strong evidence base. Then, we work 
with partners to deliver change on the ground that alleviates poverty 
and strengthens society. We hold ourselves accountable to ensure our 
outcomes will be bold and enduring.
SCALE IT 
We don’t think small. Once tested, we work with partners to adopt and 
expand our efforts regionally and globally. We engage with decision-mak-
ers to carry out our ideas and elevate our impact. We measure success 
through government and business actions that improve people’s lives 
and sustain a healthy environment.
