Foreword
Motorcycles have become an integral piece of the trans-
portation system in many parts of the world, providing 
reliable access and mobility for many road users. Afford-
able, versatile, and efficient, motorcycles have even become 
a preferred choice for on-demand delivery and taxi services 
across cities in Africa, Latin America, and Asia. The rapid 
adoption of motorcycles has been closely tied to economic 
growth and development, but it has also brought new road 
safety challenges.  
Motorcycles have become one of the riskiest modes of road 
travel today. In 2021, an astounding 1.2 million people were 
killed in traffic crashes globally, with nearly a third of these 
fatalities involving users of motorized two- and three-wheel-
ers—more than any other type of road user. The risk of death 
in the event of a crash for riders can be as much as 20 to 35 
times higher compared to those in four-wheeled vehicles. 
Motorcycle safety is not just a transportation problem; it is 
a matter of socio-economic development, public health, and 
urban design. Increasing road fatalities disproportionately 
impact young people and populations in dense urban areas, 
leading to significant economic, health, and social costs. 
Furthermore, low- and middle-income countries—who have 
the largest motorcycle fleets—experience a disproportion-
ate number of road deaths and injuries. In Southeast Asia, 
motorcycle use has increased by 273 percent since 2011, and 
today, motorcyclists account for 46 percent of all road deaths 
in the region.  
The growing adoption of motorcycles brings mobility ben-
efits, but also significant risks that demand urgent attention. 
While encouraging helmet use and building safer vehicles are 
crucial measures for improving road safety, these interven-
tions do little to address the root causes of traffic crashes. This 
complex issue requires a comprehensive approach. 
 There is a pressing need for more evidence and data to 
guide sound policy on motorcycle safety. This report shows 
how road design, infrastructure, traffic speeds, and land use 
patterns have an important impact on two-wheeler safety 
and should all be considered as part of a multifaceted “Safe 
System” approach. In addition, evidence shows that speed 
management and good design of urban road infrastructure 
can create safer environments for not only motorcyclists, but 
all road users. 
To achieve the United Nations’ ambitious global goal of 
halving road deaths by 2030, we must adopt a comprehensive, 
evidence-based approach. Motorcycle safety cannot go under 
the radar. It is critical that we reverse the alarming trends 
in deaths and injuries to meet global sustainable develop-
ment goals. Collaboration within cities and across regions 
is a vital foundation for success, bringing together those 
working to shape safer roads and more resilient mobility 
systems worldwide.  
ANI DASGUPTA  
President & CEO

Foreword
Saving lives and reducing injuries from road crashes is 
a priority at Bloomberg Philanthropies. Road traffic 
crashes claim 1.19M people each year, and are the lead-
ing cause of death for 5-29 year olds; yet they still don’t 
get the attention they require. Since 2007, the Bloom-
berg Philanthropies Initiative for Global Road Safety 
(BIGRS) has supported evidence-based road safety 
interventions in low- and middle-income countries and 
cities worldwide. Through our local and international 
partners, we are focused on strengthening road safety 
laws, improving data collection and surveillance, chang-
ing road user behavior, creating safer infrastructure, and 
enhancing vehicle standards.
As urban populations grow, the demand for reliable 
and flexible mobility solutions has led to an increase 
in motorized vehicles on our roads, with motorcycles 
becoming a key component of the transport system 
in every region of the world. Through the work of the 
Bloomberg Philanthropies Initiative for Global Road 
Safety, we have witnessed a rapid rise in motorcycle use 
in many regions of the world, accompanied by steep 
increases in road fatalities and injuries. Today, users of 
motorized two- and three-wheelers account for more 
road fatalities globally than any other road user. Over 
half of all road fatalities involve vulnerable road users—
pedestrians, cyclists, and motorcyclists—and more 
than 90 percent of fatalities occur in low- and middle-
income countries.
Addressing motorcycle safety presents an opportunity 
to tackle one of the most pressing road safety chal-
lenges of our time. We cannot reach the UN Decade of 
Action for Road Safety goal of halving road deaths by

motorcycle safety.

of data to develop evidence-based countermeasures.

activities and our partners, we have collaborated with

including the six cities featured in this study. With 
research and reports such as this, we have gained 
a deeper understanding of the factors impacting 
motorcycle safety and are better equipped to address 
issues like traffic speeds and road infrastructure 
issues that contribute to powered two-wheeler road 
deaths and injuries.

Executive summary
Rapidly growing motorized two-wheeler fleets in low- and 
middle-income countries have led to a rise in road death and 
injury. Addressing motorized two-wheeler safety requires 
a comprehensive approach that includes the design and 
planning of our road networks. This report uses data from 
six cities to understand how the urban built environment 
contributes to motorcycle deaths and injuries and suggests 
recommendations that can save lives.

HIGHLIGHTS
 ▪ Motorized two- and three-wheelers account for 
30 percent of total traffic fatalities globally—the 
highest share by road user. In 2021, riders of 
powered two- and-three wheelers represented 
46 percent of the total reported deaths by road 
user type in Southeast Asia, 25 percent in the 
Americas, and 14 percent in Africa (with increases 
in deaths since 2013 of 39 percent, 67 percent, 
and 100 percent, respectively). Meanwhile, there 
has been a 175 percent increase in powered 
two- and three-wheelers worldwide since 
2011 (WHO 2023). 
 ▪ Motorized two-wheeler safety is an economic, 
social, developmental, and equity issue. 
Addressing it is necessary to meet global road 
safety, poverty, and climate goals.
 ▪ Infrastructure, land use, and road environment 
factors can impact the safety of motorcyclists. 
Speed, road width, road complexity, and 
uncontrolled conflict points are some of the 
factors that can have severe impacts on 
motorcyclist safety.
 ▪ Tackling motorized two-wheeler safety requires 
a shift in policies, enforcement, and road 
management, as well as a broader area-wide 
and comprehensive approach to speed and 
conflict management. 
 ▪ In the long term, investments in safe infrastructure 
for safer modes of travel—such as for pedestrians, 
cyclists, and public transport—can also have 
direct and indirect benefits for motorized 
two-wheeler safety.
The UN Global Plan of Action on road safety aims to 
reduce the 1.19 million annual global road fatalities in half 
by 2030. Motorcycle users—along with pedestrians and 
bicyclists—are vulnerable road users, accounting for half of 
global road deaths (WHO 2023). Both the new UN Global 
Plan of Action on Road Safety and the 2030 UN Sustainable 
Development Goals call for action to provide safety mobility 
by prioritizing the most vulnerable, with targeted actions for 
motorcycle safety. Reaching these goals will require a renewed 
focus to reduce road deaths and injuries among users of 
motorized two-wheelers (UNGA 2020).
Across Latin America, Africa, and Asia, the motorized two-
wheeler serves a vital mobility function, and is considered a 
working tool (deliveries, freight, and moto-taxi), especially for 
those in lower socioeconomic groups; however, they impose 
a significant risk in terms of road deaths and injuries. To 
achieve UN targets, the UN Global Plan calls for a holistic 
approach that ties road safety goals to urgent global needs for 
climate action such as addressing global inequity, air pol-
lution, chronic disease, and poverty (UNGA 2020; WHO 
2023). For developing economies, the need for this holistic 
approach is clear: Although road crashes can affect all road 
users, the population groups most vulnerable to road crashes 
are those in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), 
with poorer access to safe mobility options, safe street infra-
structure, working opportunities, and life-saving emergency 
care. These groups also tend to be those who are most vulner-
able to challenges imposed by inequitable and unsustainable 
development. Motorized two- and three-wheelers account for 
30 percent of global traffic fatalities; therefore, meeting global 
goals for road safety and equitable, sustainable transport 
requires tackling the safety of motorized two-wheelers.
About this report
This report presents the results of World Resources Institute 
(WRI) research and data analysis, as well as the application of 
findings for infrastructure design elements that have shown 
an impact on safety of motorized two-wheelers in urban 
areas of LMICs. The findings are based on data analysis, 
statistical modeling, and field observations for cities in Latin 
America, Africa, and Southeast Asia from 2014 to 2020. 
The overall objective is to present evidence on the impact of 
road infrastructure design on motorized two-wheeler safety 
and discuss implications for design and policy. The report is 
intended to supplement local knowledge as a resource for

decision and policymakers, as well as designers and plan-
ners, to address motorcycle deaths and injuries and develop 
safer road systems.
The report is organized as follows: The introduction outlines 
why it is critical to address motorcycle safety and focus on 
infrastructure and road environment factors. “Data and 
methods” provides an overview of research methods and find-
ings from the models. “Results” provides a discussion of the 
findings, and “Recommendations” discusses the implications 
and recommendations for infrastructure policies and design. 
Finally, the conclusion provides suggestions for next steps 
toward implementation and further research.
Research overview
For this research, WRI analyzed data from six cities:  Accra 
(Ghana), Bangkok (Thailand), Bogotá and Cali (Colombia), 
Buenos Aires (Argentina), and Nairobi (Kenya). Based on 
statistical models, this report examines the effects of different 
road and built environment elements on motorcyclists’ safety. 
For this purpose, data on the arterial road network of these 
cities were collected and analyzed, such as counts of traffic 
crashes and/or motorcyclist victims, variables of infrastructure 
data, built environment and land use (such as vehicle speeds, 
road medians, block sizes, lane and road widths, land-use 
patterns, public transport stops, heavy vehicle use, and income 
levels); and variables of a road’s operation (traffic speeds at 
different times of the day). 
Data were collected from several sources of information. 
While the crash data and land use data come from each 
city’s official geocoded information or open data sources, 
information regarding infrastructure settings, built environ-
ment conditions, and traffic speeds come from observations, 
measurements, and queries from several Google information 
platforms. The data were then used to study the impact of 
each of these elements on road safety outcomes.
Data availability and quality are often a problem in low- and 
middle-income countries; however this report shows how 
much insight can be obtained from the actual available data. 
More data and research are needed to understand the impact 
of the built environment on speed and injury risk, of road 
conditions such as surface quality or weather, to develop 
better recommendations for urban infrastructure design—
particularly intersections—and to understand the impact of 
motorcycle traffic on other vulnerable road users.

Summary of recommendations
Our research identifies the following five key recommenda-
tions for motorcycle safety:
1. TACKLING VEHICLE SPEEDS IS CRITICAL
In all cities examined, higher road speeds of all vehicles 
are the most significant risk factor for motorcycle safety. 
Improving motorcycle safety therefore requires safe 
operating speeds at a corridor level of all vehicles, not 
just of motorcycles. Speed management measures should 
target all vehicles. Speed interventions should also be 
comprehensive and continuous, rather than targeting 
specific locations.
2. CONSIDER THE IMPACT OF LAND USE ON  
ROAD CONFLICTS
Dense urban areas and areas with concentrated com-
mercial land use showed a higher risk of conflict. Low-
income areas in Bogotá also saw higher risk for motor-
cyclists’ safety. These areas need a more comprehensive, 
area-wide approach to managing both traffic speed and 
traffic conflicts. Motorcycle safety therefore needs to be 
addressed on a larger scale, with special attention paid to 
high-risk areas where conflicts are more likely.
3. IMPROVE DESIGN AND REDUCE COMPLEXITY 
Locations with road intersections pose higher risk for 
motorcyclists than other users. Although more intersec-
tion points (or a shorter average block length) on a road 
segment may help moderate the speed of traffic, there are 
still higher risks for motorcyclists due to their inherent 
vulnerability at traffic conflict points. While car drivers 
are protected by the body of the vehicle, motorcyclists 
are not. Therefore, even minor collisions that result in no 
injury to other vehicle drivers could more likely injure a 
motorcyclist, under similar speeds and traffic conditions. 
Further research is needed into specific risk factors at 
intersections, and may include issues of speed, visibility, 
compliance, design, signal phasing, etc. In general, reduc-
ing speed, complexity, and conflicts at these locations can 
lead to safer intersections.
4. CONSIDER ROAD WIDTHS, MEDIAN OPENINGS,  
AND MERGING
Wide carriageways with medians and wide arterials with 
adjacent median-separated service roads are associated 
with increases in injuries. While the median itself is not 
a safety issue and in fact can make roads safer, mid-
block openings for vehicles in the medians allow for 
uncontrolled, high-speed merging and turns across the 
median between carriageways. In many cases the design 
of median openings does not allow for safe merging of 
vehicles, which can result in severe conflicts. Wider car-
riageways encourage higher speeds, weaving, and other 
riskier road use, which particularly makes motorcyclists 
more vulnerable. Turns and merges should be redesigned 
or restricted for safer maneuvering.  
5. COMBINE ACTIONS FOR MOTORCYCLIST SAFETY 
WITH ACTIONS FOR OTHER ROAD USERS
Improvements for pedestrians, bicyclists, and other road 
users can be opportunities to also address motorcyclist 
safety. Many interventions that reduce risks for other 
vulnerable road users can also benefit motorcyclists. For 
example, intersections can be high-risk conflict points 
for all vulnerable users—that is, motorcyclists, pedes-
trians, and bicyclists. Road safety interventions such 
as safe at-level crossings, curb extensions, better traffic 
signaling, traffic calming, and speed management can 
therefore benefit all road users, including motorcyclists. 
Similar cobenefits come from reducing road widths and 
intersection size. Good design and investment in public 
transport systems with safe access to stops and stations 
can improve safety for transit passengers and minimize 
conflicts for motorcyclists.
This research shows that tackling unsafe road speeds and 
infrastructure can reduce motorcycle deaths and injuries. 
However, rapidly increasing motorcycle fleets and increasing 
motorization can lead to worse road safety outcomes. This 
means that in addition to tackling road design and speeds 
as part of a Safe System approach, the continued growth in 
motorcycle use and dependency will also need to be addressed 
for long-term and sustainable road safety outcomes. This 
will require cities and countries to work toward sustainable 
mobility systems and urban development that systemically 
and equitably reduce dependence on motorcycles and provide 
safer, reliable alternatives.

Introduction
Motorized two-wheeler fleets are growing rapidly in Southeast 
Asia, the Americas, Africa, and the Western Pacific. These 
vehicles have become integral to our mobility systems, but 
they account for 30 percent of global road traffic deaths—
more than any other type of road user—with the highest share 
of fatalities in low- and middle-income countries. Achieving 
the UN’s global goal of halving road deaths by 2030 will 
be challenging without addressing motorcycle safety. A 
comprehensive approach is needed to address the complex 
socio-economic development and equity issues associated 
with motorcycle use and road safety. Road infrastructure is a 
key component of a Safe System approach, and the design 
and planning of our road networks should be a necessary 
component of motorcycle safety.

Why is tackling motorcycle  
safety important?
Motorized two-wheelers play a key role in mobility systems. 
Motorized two-wheelers have become an integral part of 
mobility systems in many low- and middle-income econo-
mies and are likely to remain so for the near future. Rapid 
motorization has accompanied economic growth in the 
Global South and motorcycle use has significantly increased 
in the past few decades with huge impacts on transport, 
health, climate, and equity (Nishitateno and Burke 2014). 
Motorized two- and three-wheelers are also a high-risk mode 
of transport and currently account for 30 percent of global 
road deaths (WHO 2023). With the global motorcycle fleet 
expected to grow rapidly, motorcycle safety will continue to 
be a pressing challenge for low- and middle-income countries 
(LMICs), and tackling motorized two-wheeler safety will 
be critical to meeting the UN’s global goal of halving road 
deaths by 2030.
Motorcycle fatalities and injuries rise 
as motorcycle fleets continue to grow
The reasons for this rapid growth are diverse and complex. 
From a rider’s view they are seen as an affordable, versatile 
transport option due to lower costs of ownership, financing, 
fuel, and operation compared to other motor vehicles. They 
provide a level of personal mobility and accessibility that was 
previously unavailable to many—often preceding car owner-
ship for families and replacing bicycles. They may be less 
regulated than other modes of transport in some countries 
through lighter licensing and insurance requirements, and 
lower vehicle standards. In very congested cities, motorized 
two-wheelers promise a quicker journey because of their 
ability to cut through traffic. Their ability to travel and park 
anywhere can provide door-to-door journeys that other 
modes cannot provide. 
From a mobility systems perspective, they fill critical gaps 
by offering access to jobs and opportunities to those either 
unable to use, or are underserved by, existing mobility 
systems—especially public transit. These typically include 
lower-income or vulnerable communities unable to afford or 
access other modes of travel.
From an economic standpoint, motorized two-wheelers are 
also a significant economic engine for developing econo-
mies—both in the manufacturing, export, and sale of vehicles, 
as well as a vehicle for commercial use (Nishitateno and 
Burke 2014). Motorcycles have provided livelihoods for 
vendors and hawkers, taxi services, transportation for goods 
and materials, and delivery of essential goods and services in 
many regions of the world. In recent years, the use of motor-
cycle-based taxis and delivery services in Latin America, 
Asia, and Africa has seen exponential growth driven by the 
rise of mobile phone-based apps for on-demand services. The 
COVID-19 pandemic also further accelerated motorcycle 
uptake by increasing demand for delivery services and reduc-
ing ridership on public transit.
BOX 1  |   Defining motorized two-wheelers
The term “motorized two-wheeler” can refer to a range 
of powered two-wheel vehicles such as motorcycles, 
scooters, and in some contexts, micro-mobility vehicles 
like e-bikes and kick scooters. However, a clear defini -
tion is necessary to address road safety implications 
because vehicle classes determine how regulations 
and standards are applied and enforced. An e-bike, 
for instance, would be able to travel on sidewalks and 
be regulated like a bicycle, while a motorcycle would 
not. For the purposes of this report, the term 
“motorcycle” and “motorized two-wheeler” are 
used interchangeably and are defined as two-
wheeled vehicles capable of traveling faster than 
25 kilometers per hour, legally able to operate on 
roadways, and regulated as motor vehicles.  By this 
definition we include vehicles such as motorcycles, 
mopeds, and scooters in our analysis, but not e-bikes or 
three-wheelers.

Motorcycle users face higher risk  
of death and injury on the road
Despite their versatility and convenience, motorized two-
wheelers remain a high-risk mode of transport on roads 
worldwide, especially in low- and middle-income countries 
(see Figure 1). Motorcycles can travel at speeds similar to 
other motorized vehicles, but in the event of a crash it is 
the rider that absorbs most of the kinetic forces and impact 
forces, which can be well beyond what the human body can 
tolerate. Motorized two-wheelers also have smaller profiles 
that reduce their visibility by other road users and are inher-
ently prone to instability—exacerbated by unsafe speeds, 
sudden directional changes, poor road conditions, weather 
conditions, or mechanical issues. In a crash, riders are easily 
ejected from the vehicle and are at risk of being run over or 
sustaining friction-induced injuries. As a result, motorcycle 
riders involved in a crash are more likely to suffer severe 
injury or death.  
The risk of death for motorized two-wheeler users is signifi-
cantly higher per vehicle-mile traveled, even in high-income 
countries: over 20 times that of car occupants in European 
Union countries (WHO 2004), and 29 times that of car 
occupants in the United States (NCSA 2021). In São Paulo, 
Brazil, fatality risk for a motorcyclist can be about 35 times 
higher than for a car occupant (Prefeitura Municipal de 
São Paulo 2019).
Like other vehicles, motorized two-wheelers also cause injury 
and death to other road users—particularly in crashes involv-
ing pedestrians and bicyclists or other motorcyclists. 
Motorcycle safety is a  
development issue for low-  
and middle-income countries
The rapid uptake of motorcycles is not just a mobility or road 
safety issue. It is a larger socioeconomic development issue 
that requires systemic consideration. There is evidence that

Motorcycles connect economic growth to improvements in 
access to growth, opportunity, education, and healthcare. As a 
result, the victims of motorcycle crashes also tend to be typi-
cally low-income and young adult riders who represent the 
core of the economic workforce in low- and middle-income 
countries. For example, in Latin America, people between 
the ages of 15 and 44 account for more than 60 percent of 
all motorcycle deaths (PAHO 2018). Increased road deaths 
and injuries can therefore stunt economic growth, preventing 
a quicker transition away from motorcycle-based transport 
(World Bank 2017). It is a core principle of Vision Zero poli-

evidence-based policy and design. This makes comprehen-

deaths a necessity for growing economies from a develop-
ment perspective.
Motorcycle safety is an equity issue
Impact on poverty and low-income 
populations 
Motorcycle safety also has equity implications for vulner-
able population groups. Motorcycles provide mobility where 
mobility options are lacking, especially in low-income, 
low-accessibility areas of cities in developing countries. 
Lower income areas are often along the peripheries of cities, 
requiring longer trips to opportunities. Motorcycles may 
provide quicker and cheaper trips compared to other modes, 
increasing exposure to road safety risk. Low-income areas are 
often not prioritized for investments in road upgrades and 
maintenance. As a result, these areas also tend to have poorer 
quality road infrastructure, which can further exacerbate the 
risk of road injury. Outside of urban areas, public transport 
coverage may be poor or nonexistent, which would also com-
pel rural and peri-urban populations to use motorcycles. Thus, 
low-income groups tend to have more exposure and a higher 
risk of serious road crashes as motorcyclists. 
Gender and safety
As with all modes of transportation, it is likely that motor-
cycle crash victims globally are mostly male riders, since most 
motorcycle users are also men. However, motorcycles can also 
have significant direct and indirect impacts on women’s safety 
and access to opportunities, as well as their decisions on what 
trips they choose to make and what forms of transport they 
use to make those trips. 
Safety and security are typically the primary considerations 
for women in making transport choices. Where public 
transport is unsafe or unavailable, the motorcycle may offer 
transport benefits even if it is not an ideal choice. In India, for 
example, women use motorcycles to access education or for 
leisure and shopping trips, offering mobility opportunities to 
women who feel that public transport is “unreliable, dirty, and 
unsafe” (Pai et al. 2014). Motorcycle taxis have also provided 
transport solutions for women who do not typically own or 
drive their own vehicles or where public transport is viewed 
as unsafe. These have also generated personal safety concerns 
for women, particularly on unregulated informal services 
where instances of harassment or violence have been reported 
against women passengers. However, when given a choice, 
women were less likely to choose motorcycles as a transport 
option (Ng and Acker 2018). 
Motorcycles also impact nonriders. Surveys in Jakarta, 
Indonesia, have shown that women associated streets with 
motorcycle traffic as unsafe for them and their children 
(Hidayati et al. 2020).
Impact on child safety
Globally, road traffic injuries are already the leading cause 
of death for children and young people (WHO 2023). In 
Southeast Asia, motorcycles are involved in 50–60 percent of 
transport-related injuries and deaths in children 15 years and 
younger. In Thailand, which has the highest reported motor-
cycle fatality rate in the world, motorcycles were involved in 
74 percent of all transport-related deaths for children below 
17 years, with higher rates for adolescents. In addition to 
riders, data from 2009 also show that among child pedestrian 
victims, 60.8 percent were hit by a motorcycle. Public health 
surveillance pilot projects in India, Nepal, and Sri Lanka also 
showed child motorcycle users sustaining the largest propor-
tion of transport-related injuries compared to other vehicle 
types (WHO 2014).

But research and practice to understand and address environ-
mental and infrastructure risk factors to prevent motorcycle 
crashes is lacking, despite significant research on similar risk 
factors for other road users such as pedestrians or bicyclists. 
A lot of the existing research is from cities and countries 
where motorcycle mode shares are lower than what we see in 
many low- and middle-income countries, which have large 
volumes of daily motorcycle users. In countries with larger 
motorcycle mode shares, despite being disproportionately 
impacted by road crashes, motorcyclists are often seen unfa-
vorably as contributing to unsafe road environments by being 
more likely to engage in speeding or unskilled, risky driving. 
Why infrastructure and road 
environment matter in a Safe 
System approach
Addressing motorcycle safety presents unique challenges 
compared to other modes due to their increased vulnerability 
as they operate alongside other vehicles. Global efforts to 
address motorcycle safety have tended to focus on individual 
behaviors and protecting the rider in the event of a crash, 
including equipment mandates such as helmet-wearing, use 
of antilock braking systems (ABS), and daytime running 
lights, all of which are proven to reduce fatalities and injuries. 
Road user behavior modifications, as well as restrictions 
on when, where, and how motorcycles can be used, have 
also been used with varying efficacy in different countries 
(Afukaar et al. 2019; Oteng-Ababio and Agyemang 2015).

do not have death or serious injury as an outcome. Motorcycle safety is a complex and multifaceted issue that cannot be tackled 
with siloed or isolated efforts. It is a systems issue that requires systems thinking and solutions, with shared responsibility among 
multiple stakeholders.

This may have also contributed to a focus on modifying road 
user behavior—which is one component of road safety—over

While much research and design guidance exist to design 
safer roads for pedestrians, bicyclists, public transit, and 
motor vehicles, there is insufficient research into how road 
design impacts motorcycle safety, especially in LMICs. As 
a result of this gap, the road environment has been designed 
based on the needs and tolerances for these other road user 
groups, which may not necessarily be safe for motorcyclists 
(Doctor and Ngo 2022). Road design often does not consider 
motorcyclists, as four-wheel vehicles were the original “pri-
mary” design users. This, coupled with the increased vulner-
ability of motorized two-wheelers, has created almost “unfair” 
road conditions (Elvik 2009). 
Infrastructure design, road environment, land use, speed, and 
transport systems are all critical elements that impact road 
safety. A Safe System approach (see Box 3) requires a com-
prehensive and proactive look at the systems that contribute 
to road fatalities, including the growth in motorcycle use and 
associated fatalities and injuries. While safe equipment like 
vehicles and helmets, better enforcement, and education are 
all important and effective elements of a safe system, there 
is a need to expand the scope of motorcycle safety efforts 
to bring about broader systems change and increase action 
in other areas. 
What this report contributes
This report aims to fill some of the gaps in research and 
understanding of how road design and road environment 
contribute to motorcycle fatalities and injuries. Our research 
examines the relationship between road environment (specifi-
cally road infrastructure) and motorcycle safety in selected 
cities from LMICs across the globe. The report uses statisti-
cal analysis of infrastructure and built environment data to 
provide recommendations based on evidence for improving 
motorcycle safety. 
This report aims to answer the following key questions:
1. Is there a link between road environment factors, 
particularly speed and infrastructure, and motorcycle 
road safety in low- and middle-income countries?
2. What are the road environment factors that impact 
motorcycle safety?
Based on the results of this study, we examine how cities 
can tackle speed and road infrastructure issues to improve 
motorcycle safety performance. To answer these questions, 
the research tested how motorcycle-related injuries and 
fatalities could be associated with built environment variables 
including infrastructure, speed, and land use.

Dedicated motorcycle infrastructure has shown mixed results for safety. The evaluation of motorcycle lanes was not included in 
this report since motorcycle lanes do not exist in the cities that were analyzed. While pedestrian and cyclist segregation from 
motorized traffic is not new, exclusive facilities for motorcycles remain relatively unexplored. a In some cities with high motorcycle 
mode shares, the use of dedicated motorcycle lanes (as seen in Figure 2), as well as motorcycle boxes at intersections modeled 
after bicycle boxes, have been used, with mixed results. For example, studies in Indonesia, Malaysia, and Taiwan saw a signifi -
cant reduction in motorcycle crashes after implementation,  while studies in Brazil and Argentina saw an increase in motorcycle 
crashes after implementation. b They have also been implemented on a range of road types—from exclusive lanes on highways or 
arterials to segregated lanes or turn boxes on urban roads. The mixed safety performance of dedicated motorcycle infrastructure 
may therefore be dependent on factors outside of the scope of this report, such as road type, driver behavior, traffic volumes, driv -
ing culture, placement and design characteristics of the lane, or mode shares. 
In general, segregating a more vulnerable mode of transport into dedicated lanes away from mixed traffic conditions can be 
expected to improve safety for that user while using those lanes. This is why well-designed, dedicated infrastructure is safer for 
road users at higher risk, such as bicyclists and pedestrians. Such lanes can potentially minimize risky maneuvers in traffic and 
conflicts with other vehicles. However, these do not eliminate conflicts at junctions or intersections. For bicyclists and pedestrians 
these conflicts are managed by prioritization through well-designed crossings, signals, signal phases, traffic calming, lower speed 
limits, and other safety features.

But without similar prioritization measures, motorcyclists can remain especially vulnerable even if segregated lanes are provided. c 
In denser urban areas we can expect dedicated motorcycle lanes to be intersected by numerous openings for turning vehicles, 
property entrances, and other curbside amenities such as parking, bus stops, or crossings, all of which are potential conflict 
points. Unlike bicycles on bicycle lanes, higher speeds of motorcycles on dedicated lanes can make these conflicts severe while 
providing a false sense of safety. 
Moreover, there are fewer reasons to prioritize motorcycles on the road. Prioritizing pedestrians and bicyclists or even public 
transit vehicles in road design offers multiple other benefits—including safety, public health, economic, air quality, climate, and 
accessibility benefits. These benefits are derived from increased use of sustainable transport options, increased physical activity, 
and lower emissions, which can counter the negative externalities of motorized transport. d A similar prioritization of motorcycles 
would not provide these benefits, but may in fact have the opposite effect by prioritizing more motorized travel.
The question of dedicated motorcycle lanes and infrastructure should therefore be considered within the larger mobility system, 
broader sustainable transport and road safety goals, to enable the safest use of limited road space that serves all road users. 
Research on the broader systemic impact of motorcycle infrastructure is limited. While they can offer some protection to motorcy -
clists in specific situations, they need to be considered carefully and with caution, as they may also encourage further motorcycle 
uptake, higher speeds, or divert resources from other necessary road safety or mobility improvements. 
In places with limited road capacity, such as dense urban areas, the opportunity cost for adding motorcycle lanes should be evalu -
ated, especially if these roads are missing other important infrastructure elements for pedestrian safety or public transit. Investing 
and prioritizing both nonmotorized and public transport have been proven to reduce road deaths and injuries. e It is important to 
find out—case by case—under which conditions dedicated motorcycle infrastructure might be safe and how to allocate scarce 
physical space. Nevertheless, motorcycles should never be prioritized over safer modes of transport such as walking, biking, and 
public transit, and investment in any dedicated infrastructure for motorcycles should not be made at the expense of high-quality 
nonmotorized or public transport infrastructure.

Traffic crash data
Geocoded crash data were obtained for all six cities for the 
most recent years of data available, using up to five years of 
data where available. The data for Bangkok were collected 
by the Thai Roads Foundation, with the support of WRI 
through the Bloomberg Philanthropies Initiative for Global 
Road Safety (BIGRS). In the case of Nairobi, crash data were 
made available by the National Transport and Safety Author-
ity of Kenya with support from the World Bank, which were 
then geocoded by WRI to the closest street location identi-
fied in the crash reports.
For Cali, Bogotá, Buenos Aires, and Bangkok, the data were 
made available by the cities through our BIGRS partner Vital 
Strategies. All crash data points were assigned to the closest 
corresponding major and arterial road segments for analysis. 
Intersection locations between major roads were excluded 
from the analysis to avoid double counting (Vergel-Tovar et 
al. 2020), as shown in Figure 4.
Serious injury and fatal crashes were modeled where infor-
mation on severity of the crash was available. For Accra and 
Nairobi, however, information on crash severity was not 
available so all crashes were examined for these cities. 
As the exact extent and geographical pattern of underreport-

across the arterial road network. In the context of this report, 
arterial or major road network refers to high-capacity urban

residential streets, access roads, and small collector roads. 
In all cases, main roads and arterial roads come from Open

Speed data
While research on motorcycle safety has been limited, 
particularly in developing countries, previous research 
focusing on the built environment and road safety suggests 
that vehicle speeds are one of the leading risk factors in 
road safety (Dumbaugh and Rae 2009). Speed is already 
recognized universally in road safety research literature as a 
key risk factor, and the most significant one for the safety of 
all road users (Elvik 2005). Higher vehicle speeds increase 
the likelihood of collisions, or of a driver losing control of 
the vehicle, and decrease the amount of time available for a 
driver to anticipate and react to road hazards. Higher speeds 
also increase the severity of a crash, with higher impact forces 
causing more-severe injuries, especially for vulnerable road 
users, including users of motorized two-wheelers. The risk 
of collisions is higher when vehicle speeds are unsuited for 
the road environment, such as in dense urban environments. 
For motorcyclists in the cities examined, more-severe crashes 
also occur at night when congestion is lower, and traffic flows 
unimpeded at higher speeds. In other words, traffic speeds 
increase both the likelihood and severity of traffic crashes. 
But in general, traffic speed data from arterial roads in the 
studied locations were not easily available from local sources. 
Therefore, as a proxy, the study uses predicted average traffic 
speeds under free-flow conditions. These are defined as speeds 
a motorist would travel in the total absence of congestion or 
adverse conditions. These speeds were obtained using Google 
Distance Matrix API, which predicts average travel times 
for a given distance (see Box 5 to learn more about Google

Distance Matrix API). As a result, the study used the average 
speeds at 3 AM for each road segment in all six cities—which 
is the time of day with the highest speeds (which is a proxy 
variable to free-flow speed conditions). Road speeds tend 
to change over the course of the day in response to conges-
tion, targeted enforcement, construction, weather, and other 
changes in road conditions. Our model does not consider the 
influence of the daily variation of speeds for the analysis of 
crashes. However, the free-flow speed value represents how 
fast a driver could go on the road in the absence of traffic 
congestion given the existing road design and built environ-
ment conditions. On the other hand, the severity of crashes 
tends to be higher in the night and early morning hours (see 
Figure 7) and it is no coincidence that it occurs when traffic 
speeds are higher.
Road infrastructure data
Infrastructure data were collected with a combination of 
existing geocoded data from each city (for cities that had 
available information on land and infrastructure features from 
their own institutions), information from open sources (like 
Open Street Maps) and visual observations using Google 
Street View imagery. The complete list of variables and 
descriptions are included in Appendix A and a general repre-
sentation for each can be seen in Figure 5. Variables collected 
include the following:
 ▪ Traffic lights. A variable to designate corridors with 
traffic lights on them (meaning the presence of signalized 
intersections) was included, building on previous research 
finding traffic lights to be associated with an increase 
in conflicts for vulnerable road users and with a higher 
association with motorcyclist casualties compared with 
pedestrians and cyclists (Vergel-Tovar et al. 2020). 
 ▪ Pedestrian bridges. This variable indicates the presence 
of a pedestrian bridge along the segment. While intended 
as a safety feature, the decision to construct an elevated 
crossing for pedestrians is an indicator of an existing 
safety problem. Indeed, previous research has found the 
presence of pedestrian bridges to be associated with 
increased crash risk for all road users, not just pedestrians 
(Vergel-Tovar et al. 2020). 
 ▪ Pedestrian crossings. This variable was included to reflect 
the presence of marked paths across the corridor designed 
for pedestrians to cross the corridor. This variable was also 
included, based on the results of traffic lights found in 
previous research by Vergel-Tovar et al. (2020).
 ▪ Nonsignalized intersections. In the models, this 
variable considers the existence of places where vehicles 
or pedestrians can cross the street without a controlling 
signal. This variable was chosen due to significant 
results found by Vergel-Tovar et al. (2020) influencing 
overall casualties.
 ▪ Motorcycle boxes. This variable was included for Buenos 
Aires, where there are designated areas for motorcycles to 
queue ahead of traffic at a signalized intersection in some 
arterial corridors. The existing research on this type of 
infrastructure has led to contradictory findings (see Box 4).  
BOX 5  |   Google Distance Matrix API
The Distance Matrix API is a service that accepts an 
HTTPS request containing origins and destinations for a 
given mode of transport and returns travel distance and 
duration for each combination of origin and destination 
that has been specified. With this data, travel speeds 
along a road segment can be calculated. For each 
request, additional options, such as departure time, 
travel mode (the study uses “driving” as the default 
option to reflect mixed traffic circulation on a corridor), 
and traffic model can also be specified. 
There are three traffic models used to calculate the 
expected travel time between two locations under dif -
ferent traffic conditions:
 ▪ Optimistic: considers the expected duration in traffic 
to be shorter than the normally expected travel 
time on most days (though occasional days with 
particularly good traffic conditions may be faster than 
this value).
 ▪ Best guess: considers the best estimate of expected 
travel time given what is known about both historical 
traffic conditions and live traffic conditions.
 ▪ Pessimistic: estimates travel times that are longer 
than the normally expected travel time on most days 
due to congested traffic conditions. 
Based on these combinations, speed data for different 
traffic conditions and different times of the day can be 
calculated for each road segment. The optimistic traffic 
model was selected for the purposes of this publication, 
as it reflects the riskiest possible conditions in traffic 
in terms of speed on a given corridor at a given time 
of the day.

Method—statistical models
This study used statistical models to test the relationship 
between the speed and built environment variables with the 
number of motorcycle crashes, injuries, and fatalities. Draw-
ing on similar road safety research, the study uses negative 
binomial and Poisson models—depending on the location—
to test the effect of infrastructure and built environment on 
motorcycle safety (crashes, injuries, and fatalities of motor-
cyclists) in the selected cities (Dumbaugh and Rae 2009; 
Vergel-Tovar et al. 2020). A detailed list of each variable, its 
description, and source can be found in Appendix A.
Considering the different nature and distribution of all 
crashes and serious crashes that involve motorcyclists, we ran 
Poisson models for the African cities and negative binomial 
models for the other cities. The model of best fit for each 
location was selected considering the following:
 ▪ The values of the dependent variable (see Table 1) tend 
to be positively skewed (i.e., follow a right-skewed 
distribution), especially the values of fatalities. 
 ▪ In cases where the variance is higher than the mean 
(overdispersed) or the frequency of zero observations 
(locations with no crashes) is notably higher, negative 
binomial models can be used. This was done in 
four of the cities.
 ▪ In cases where the data are not overdispersed, Poisson 
regression is commonly used to characterize count data 
(crashes) where the highest frequencies correspond to the 
lower values of the variable. In the African cities, due to 
the nature and limited availability of data, information 
on severity of crashes was not available. In those cases, 
Poisson distribution using crash data was a better 
choice of model.

Limitations 
While this study draws valuable conclusions using the data 
currently available, there are limitations that stem from the 
data and data collection process and the methodology used. 
These are opportunities for future studies on motorcycle road 
safety on urban roads when data are more readily available. 
Limited information on other road safety– 
related variables
Information on other variables that affect road safety in 
general, or motorcyclists’ road safety, was not available for 
WRI, could not be adequately characterized, or in other cases 
was simply nonexistent. Information such as vehicle type 
shares and volumes, infrastructure quality and conditions, 
weather, local policies, etc. (Bezabeh et al. 2022) could make a 
significant difference in the results. Variables related not only 
to crash risks but also to exposure and injury risks (severity 
of crashes) must be more precisely and extensively gathered 
to understand in full detail which factors affect motorcy-
clists’ road safety.
 ▪ Data on traffic volumes were only available in the case 
of Bogotá, so they were included in the Bogotá results. 
However, the models of the other cities under study 
lacked this information as inputs for the statistical 
models. Traffic volume can be a big predictor of crashes 
due to increased exposure, but other variables, such 
as road widths, can be used as a proxy variable for the 
capacity of the road. 
 ▪ Land-use data were not available for all the cities in 
general in the same quantity or quality. The models had to 
be adapted to the available or missing information. 
 ▪ Information on speeds was available for general traffic, yet 
we still know little about how big the difference between 
motorcyclists’ and other vehicles’ speeds at various 
locations might be.
 ▪ Speed of motorcycles and vehicles, particularly at the time 
of collision, was unavailable. Such data would be very 
useful for determining the relation between impact speed 
and injury or fatality risks.
 ▪ Information on victims of crashes would be useful 
to determine risks to riders, pillion riders, as well 
as pedestrians and other road users involved in 
motorcycle crashes.

Analysis does not include dedicated 
infrastructure for motorcyclists
Cities included in the analysis did not have any dedicated 
infrastructure for motorcyclists and, therefore, this variable 
was not included in the research. Dedicated infrastructure 
for motorcyclists has shown mixed results (see Box 4). In 
Malaysia, it showed a positive impact on road safety in a 
highway context (Radin Sohadi et al. 2000) while in São 
Paulo’s urban context, there was an increase in serious traffic 
crashes, and such infrastructure was removed (Gerência 
de Segurança no Trânsito 2014). The long-term impact of 
dedicated lanes for motorcyclists is also something that 
should be studied because investment in such infrastructure 
could induce demand for more motorized travel or take away 
road space and investment in safer, more sustainable modes 
of travel, such as walking, cycling, and public transit, espe-
cially in places with scarce public space dedicated to mobility 
(Welle et al. 2015).
Intersections and local roads are not included
The information included in the models includes characteris-
tics of infrastructure and the associated built environment of 
segments of arterial thoroughfares in the cities under analysis. 
Information regarding characteristics of local roads and 
intersection characteristics was not included in the analysis. 
Future studies could apply elements of this methodology to 
local road segments in different types of urban environments. 
Intersections can share the characteristics of two or more 
corridors that go through them and have their own physical 
and operational characteristics. In this study we find associa-
tions between each crash and the characteristics of a corridor 
where they “belong,” thus making it unsuitable to study 
intersections. The physical and operational characteristics 
of an intersection would have to be studied under a differ-
ent methodology. Due to these reasons, intersections were 
excluded from the scope of this study.  
More detailed crash information not available
While the data used in this study provide information on 
where a crash occurred, the data lack more detailed informa-
tion on the type of crash that occurred, the characteristics 
of involved parties, or information on the vehicles that were 
involved. The addition of this information would greatly aid 
efforts to improve road safety in LMICs—if properly col-
lected by local authorities and made available to researchers.

Main findings 
This section discusses the models’ most relevant findings. The 
variables with significant influence on motorcyclists’ traffic 
crashes will be examined in more detail in terms of the inter-
pretation of results and possible applications for planning, 
policymaking, and infrastructure design in general.
Speed
The results show that vehicle speed is a significant variable 
associated with fatalities, injuries, and crashes in all cities 
under study. More importantly, this association shows a clear 
link between motorcyclist deaths and speeds of all vehicles, 
not just motorcycle speeds. According to the results, for 
every 10 km/h increase in the free-flow speed of a road, there 
is a corresponding increase of between 7 and 24 percent 
in motorcycle injuries, and 20 to 31 percent in motor-
cycle fatalities. 
Based on the analysis of time of day of all the available crash 
data, it can be observed that motorcycle crashes tend to be 
much more severe during night hours (see Figure 7) when 
free-flow conditions are more likely. Although traffic patterns 
during night hours may change according to cultural and 
local dynamics associated with nighttime activities in each of 
the cities, in general, for all of the cities under analysis, the 
times of the day between 10 PM and 5 AM saw a concentra-
tion of the most severe traffic crashes. Similarly, the density of 
motorcycle fatalities increases after 30–40 km/h (see Figure 
8) which supports the positive impacts of safe speeds for 
motorcyclists. Nevertheless, more extensive research on this 
topic is needed.

Road infrastructure
Road width. Roadway width was significant in two cities 
analyzed (Buenos Aires and Nairobi) and wider roads are 
associated with increases in injuries. This may be due to 
increased filtering and weaving by motorcyclists and higher 
speeds on wider corridors with multiple lanes (Peña Cabra 
2014). This variable also aimed to be a proxy for traffic 
volume, which might also explain the result.
Median openings. The presence of medians that separate 
traffic on central and service carriageways showed an associa-
tion with increases in motorcycle victims and overall crashes 
in three of the cities. This might be connected to very wide 
medians in urban environments, which allow for uncontrolled 
merging at high speeds and poorly designed exits that induce 
serious conflicts.
Blocks and traffic lights. More frequent intersections are 
associated with an increase in fatalities and injuries in five of 
the six cities. This may be associated with an increase of con-
flict points, particularly where vehicle turn movements may 
conflict with motorcycle movements ( Jiménez Flórez 2015). 
This does not imply that smaller block sizes, traffic lights, or 
intersections should be avoided, because these elements have 
several safety benefits and help manage speeds. It implies that 
road safety risks to motorcyclists are higher than for other 
users at these locations and highlights the need for a com-
prehensive plan to ensure speeds and conflicts are managed 
at locations with high density of blocks. Even at traffic lights, 
poor compliance and higher speeds put vulnerable road users 
like motorcyclists at a higher risk than other motorists.

Recommendations
In this section, we provide recommendations for implementing 
safer road infrastructure for motorcyclists based on the 
statistical models’ results. As part of a Safe System approach, 
safe roads and safe road design are among the essential 
areas of action for a road system that includes the safety of 
motorcyclists.

General principles
The implementation of the interventions and recommenda-
tions provided in this section should be guided by these 
general principles:
 ▪ Systemic changes have the most sustainable impact. 
Tackling motorcycle safety should not be a collection 
of disparate or isolated interventions. A Safe System 
approach is necessary to address road safety holistically.
 ▪ Give priority to locations where acting first could 
potentially save the most lives by avoiding serious 
crashes and deterring risky behaviors on the road. This is 
something that becomes necessary in the context of low- 
and middle-income countries that have heavy demand but 
limited resources for road safety interventions.
 ▪ Data analyses, road safety audits, and evidence-based 
design are critical. Interventions should not just be 
responsive to motorcyclist crash occurrence patterns. Data 
must be used in a proactive approach to save as many lives 
as possible before crashes can occur.
Main takeaways
There are five key takeaways from the research. They are:
 ▪ Tackling vehicle speeds is critical
 ▪ Consider the impact of land use on road conflicts
 ▪ Minimize conflicts at intersections
 ▪ Consider road widths, median openings, and merging
 ▪ Combine actions for motorcyclist safety with actions for 
other road users
In this section, each of these takeaways are discussed along 
with their implications for design, policy, and implementa-
tion. Recommendations for each of these takeaways are 
provided below.

significant risk factor associated with increases in

 ▪ Improving motorcycle safety requires comprehensive

 ▪ Motorcycle hot spots tend to relocate when point-based

1. Tackling vehicle speeds is critical 
Vehicle speeds are a critical factor in preventing motorcycle 
crashes, injuries, and fatalities. Analysis consistently showed 
vehicle speeds to be one of the most fundamental risk factors 
for motorcycle safety. Higher free-flow vehicle speeds in all 
six cities were associated with an increase in motorcycle-
related injuries and fatalities. On average, a 10 km/h increase 
in free-flow vehicle speed resulted in an increase in injuries of 
up to 24 percent and in fatalities up to 31 percent. 
Speed is already universally recognized in road safety research 
literature as a key risk factor, and the most significant one for 
all road users (Elvik 2005). Higher vehicle speeds increase the 
likelihood of collisions or a driver losing control of the vehicle 
and decrease the amount of time available to anticipate and 
react to road hazards. Higher speeds also increase the severity 
of a crash, with higher impact forces causing more severe 
injuries, especially for vulnerable road users, including users 
of motorized two-wheelers. The risk of collision is higher 
when vehicle speeds are unsuited for the road environment, 
such as in dense urban environments. For motorcyclists in 
the cities examined, more severe crashes also occur at night 
when congestion is lower, and as a result vehicles move 
at higher speeds.
The implications of these findings for design, policy, and 
implementation are as follows:
Speed management should target all  
vehicles and not just motorcycles
Traditional approaches to motorized two-wheeler safety, 
especially through speed enforcement, have tended to focus 
on road user behavior by targeting motorcyclist behavior and 
speeds. However, our research shows that it is insufficient to 
Motorcycle safety and urban road infrastructure  |  43

tackle motorcycle speed in isolation because higher free-flow 
road speeds have a negative impact on motorcycle safety. 
To address motorcycle safety, it is necessary to get all motor 
vehicles to operate at safer speeds that are appropriate for a 
corridor’s built environment conditions. This calls for better 
speed management policies and designing roads to align with 
safe speeds. Some European countries are now requiring 
Intelligent Speed Assist (ISA) systems and speed limiters 
in all vehicles to enable better compliance with speed limits. 
From a Safe System perspective, impact speeds should be as 
low as possible to prevent traffic-related injuries and fatalities.
Although the precise safe speed for motorcycles depends 
on the typical variables in a speed management strategy—
context, road use, road quality, road users, and available 
infrastructure of the road—it can be observed that the rate 
of motorcycle fatalities increases considerably at free-flow 
speeds higher than 30 km/h. But in general, conclusions 
regarding the exact numerical relationship between speed 
and motorcycle safety cannot yet be drawn from the avail-
able information. More research is needed about safe speeds 
under each type of road depending on its use and general 
characteristics.
Evidence was found in all cities for the free-flow speed of all 
vehicles being associated with higher numbers of fatalities, 
injuries, and crashes. Speed management strategies are there-
fore necessary because targeting speed measures for motorcy-
cles will not be sufficient to reduce road risk for all vulnerable 
road users—safe, well-designed infrastructure is crucial.

Speed interventions should be  
continuously distributed
Speed management and interventions are key for motor-
cycle safety. While hot spot analysis is useful to identify and 
prioritize critical locations for motorcycle safety, this study 
shows that safe speeds need to be enforced on a larger scale—
corridor-wide, area-wide, or citywide. Motorcycle hot spots 
are observed to relocate in response to point-based interven-
tions due to risk compensation behavior such as speeding up 
after passing a speed camera (Lopez Valderrama et al. 2023). 
Designing roads for target speeds is necessary, but other 
measures can be used, such as average speed enforcement or 
point-to-point speed enforcement, placing multiple consecu-
tive cameras or speed humps, designed for motorcyclists, 
with clear markings to achieve a target safe speed. Speed 
humps should be indicated with advanced warning, designed 
with appropriate area-to-width ratio, be clearly indicated, 
well-maintained, and properly spaced to encourage driving 
at the target speed (Welle et al. 2015). Implementing speed 
humps is only recommended if road users are not surprised 
by these humps; they should be highly visible, on roads with 
street lighting and enough sight distance. In a Safe System 
approach, while the most critical corridors can be prioritized 
for speed management, the end goal should be to keep speeds 
safe across the network, not only at specific locations. 
Safe speeds for roads where 
motorcycles are expected to be

2. Consider the impact of land  
use on road conflicts 
Tackling motorcycle safety requires us to take a broader, 
area-wide approach. Three main issues impacting motorcy-
clist safety will be discussed in this section: 1) dispersion of 
motorcycle-related crashes; 2) influence of land use, built 
environment, and income level on motorcyclist casualties; and 
3) displacement of motorcyclist hot spots. 
First, motorcycle crashes tend to be more geographically 
dispersed when compared to crashes involving other modes 
of transport, and they tend to be more randomly distributed 
along the street network. Wider dispersion of motorcycle-
related crashes is even more evident in the African cities 
analyzed, where street networks and road hierarchies are rela-
tively less developed and land-use patterns are mixed. A geo-
graphic hot spot analysis also shows few or zero statistically 
significant motorcycle crash hot spots in Nairobi and Accra. 
Second, our research shows that motorcycle fatalities and 
injuries are influenced by land use and development factors. 
High-speed major roads and arterials in areas with higher 
commercial land use and population densities had a nega-
tive impact on motorcycle safety; for example, commercial 
land use and density were associated with an increase in risk 
of motorcycle fatalities. This is because dense commercial 
sectors generate more activity and hence more conflicts, as 
well as the convergence of different vehicle types on the same 
road, such as freight vehicles, public transport, bicycles, cars, 
and two-wheelers.
Additionally, lower-income neighborhoods are associated 
with a higher risk of motorcycle fatalities and injuries due 
to higher exposure and poorer conditions of infrastructure. 
Low-income populations are more likely to use motorcycles 
as the main solution for their mobility needs, and more 
motorcycles are used in low-income areas for business, as 
delivery vehicles or to serve in public transportation capacities 
(Herwangi et al. 2015; PAHO 2018). In Cali and Bogotá, all 
motorcycle hot spots were in lower-income areas. Riding on 
roads in Bogotá’s lowest-income neighborhoods was associ-
ated with a 44 percent increased risk of motorcycle fatalities. 
Third, motorcycle hot spots tend to relocate in response to 
point-based countermeasures such as camera enforcement, 
even when they have had a positive impact on safety for 
other road users. This can be attributed to the “Kangaroo 
effect,” which means that motorcyclists will compensate for 
enforcement mechanisms by speeding up more quickly than 
other road users after passing speed-enforced locations. An 
analysis of speed camera enforcement undertaken in Bogotá, 
for example, showed that while speed cameras had a positive 
impact on road safety by decreasing the number of fatalities 
by 21 percent, they also shifted motorcycle hot spots to other 
locations away from the cameras (Lopez Valderrama et al. 
2023) (see Box 6).

implementation are as follows:
Motorcycle safety needs to be

patterns have an impact on motorcycle safety that goes 
beyond infrastructure deficits at any one location. Point-

3. Minimize conflicts at intersections 
The risk posed to motorcyclists at intersections may be higher 
than for other motorized road users because of increased 
physical vulnerability and increased exposure at conflict 
points. In general, intersections are complex locations where 
multiple traffic flows converge, leading to conflict points that 
raise the risk of crashes. An intersection’s safety can depend 
on how this complexity is managed. A built environment 
with shorter blocks and more frequent intersections can help 
reduce vehicle speeds and provide safer crossing opportunities 
for pedestrians, further reducing risks (Welle et al. 2015) for 
different types of road users, including two-wheelers. While 
the increase in number of conflict points can increase the risk 
of crashes, these crashes tend to be less severe (Dumbaugh 
and Rae 2009).  
In the case of motorized two-wheelers, however, shorter 
blocks and more frequent intersections are associated with a 
higher risk of severe crashes in all cities that were analyzed. 
This does not imply that fewer intersections or fewer traf-
fic lights are the solution, because intersections do serve 
to improve accessibility, walkability, and connectivity, and 
play a role in regulating vehicle speeds. The reason for this 
finding might be associated with the fact that intersections 
allow a higher number of conflicts, especially with other 
vehicles ( Jimenez et al. 2015). Several other factors may be 
responsible for the increased vulnerability of motorcyclists at 
intersections and traffic lights, which should be analyzed in 
detail in future studies. 
Reduced visual conspicuity of motorized two-wheelers due 
to their smaller visual profile may make them less visible 
to other motorists, and therefore more vulnerable in spots 
with obscured vision caused by bigger vehicles and urban 
equipment, among others. There is evidence that motorists 
may be less likely to notice motorcyclists compared to other 
road users, and more likely to improperly judge speeds and 
collision risks of oncoming motorcycles (Gould et al. 2012). 
Motorcyclists are also likely to filter to the front of stopped 
traffic at intersections, have lower compliance to traffic lights, 
and less likely to come to a complete halt or yield at unsignal-
ized stops (Muttart et al. 2011), thereby increasing exposure.

inherent vulnerability.
 ▪ More frequent intersections, even if controlled, could help 
lower speeds but still increase risks for motorcyclists.

Analyze and manage conflicts  
at intersections 
Managing conflicts is key for safe intersections. The design of 
intersections on road networks and in cities where there are 
many motorized two-wheelers needs to be carefully consid-
ered. Intersections that are compact in size can minimize 
complex and unpredictable traffic movements and improve 
road safety performance. This is of particular importance in 
developing countries, where very large, complex intersections 
with multiple possible turn movements are common on major 
and arterial roads and with a multiplicity of road users such as 
pedestrians, cyclists, and informal modes, including animal-
drawn vehicles. One possible solution is to reduce the number 
of movements and conflicts by moving them away from 
intersections. Making intersections easier to navigate using 
changes in infrastructure, reducing exposure, signage, road 
marking, and guides, along with enforcement and education, 
can also make clear what type of movements are permitted at 
intersections. Changes at corridors and intersections aimed 
at reducing conflicts should be made using tools such as

Enforcement can help influence 
motorcyclist behavior at intersections
Increasing traffic light compliance at intersections can be 
aided by better enforcement and education. Proper enforce-
ment with measures such as cameras can influence motor-
cycle riders to adhere to safe speeds and prevent red-light 
jumping or speeding up at intersections, and while approach-
ing intersections. When motorcycles offer the quickest travel 
times, especially in congested cities, riders are sensitive to the 
time-cost of red traffic lights—more so than other road users. 
Riders in Bogotá indicated that they would choose to speed 
up at intersections to avoid red lights that would impose 
travel time increases. This implies that better signal phasing 
with lower wait times may help increase compliance, as it 
does with pedestrians (Duduta et al. 2014), and should be 
studied in further research.

4. Consider road widths, median 
openings, and merging
Generally, road safety practice recommends the use of central 
medians to reduce the risk of head-on collisions, restrict 
uncontrolled turns across the road, and provide refuge islands 
for pedestrians crossing the road. In general, medians have 
been shown to reduce the risk of crashes in urban areas (Elvik 
et al. 2009) and improve pedestrian safety (DOT 2007; 
Welle et al. 2015). 
However, this analysis shows that in Latin American cit-
ies, the presence of medians separating outside access lanes 
from through-moving traffic in the center of the right-
of-way is associated with a 28–64 percent higher risk of 
injuries for motorcyclists. This can be mostly attributed to 
uncontrolled median breaks, which were intended to allow 
the flow of vehicles between the service road and the cen-
tral carriageways. 
Major arterial roads in Latin American cities tend to be 
wide, with large medians and service roads. This creates the 
perception of a highway-type road environment and leads 
to high-speed driving behavior. Openings, access points, or 
gaps in such medians may allow for uncontrolled, high-speed 
merging of vehicles or turns across the median. These loca-
tions are often not designed for safe or high-speed merges. In 
this road environment, motorcyclists can be at higher risk for 
severe crashes. A geographic analysis of motorcycle crashes 
as well as site observations from the cities in this report also 
shows hot spots located at the sites of such intersections 
where vehicles merge into traffic. 
Wider roads with more than two lanes in each direction were 
also found to be associated with a higher risk of motorcycle 
fatalities and injuries. Wide roads in urban areas create larger 
intersections and create a higher speed environment, and they 
also encourage risk-taking among motorcyclists by enabling 
them to weave between traffic or travel between lanes 
(Peña Cabra 2014).

The implications of these findings for design, policy, and 
implementation are as follows:
Review road function  
and classification
A mismatch in road classification, function, design, and actual 
use can cause more conflicts. Urban arterial roads in many 
LMICs often handle multiple functions. Many are classi-
fied or perceived as expressways with high speeds but also 
handle local traffic with multiple access points, junctions, 
and vulnerable road users (see Figure 15). Without clear and 
appropriate classification, road design may be inadequate for 
the actual use of the road, and this can lead to severe conflicts 
with the highest-risk-vulnerable road users. 
Assessing and classifying the road network is a first step in 
implementing safer roads and speeds (Turner et al. 2024) and 
can help ensure that speed and design are appropriate for the 
expected function of the road. While highways and motor-
ways were not the focus of this study, roads that are expected 
to perform as high-speed motorways should be designed as 
high-speed motorways—access points should be restricted, 
and merges and turns be controlled or designed safely. 
The use of motorcycles on such roads should be evaluated 
carefully because survivability deteriorates rapidly at impact 
speeds above 30 km/h for a motorcyclist. 
If, on the other hand, the road is expected to function as an 
urban road with multiple types of users and access points, 
its design and speed should be adjusted accordingly. Speeds 
should correspond to the types of road users present. A 
highway-type design for such a road is typically inappropriate 
for an urban setting. Very wide medians, service lanes, and 
multiple lanes of traffic will lead to highway-type driving 
behavior and potentially severe conflicts.

Reconsider the geometry of medians 
in urban environments
While median-widths are highly context-specific, there can 
be benefits to reducing very wide medians in urban areas 
where vulnerable road users are present. The space occupied 
by medians could be narrowed in exchange for other benefits, 
such as increasing pedestrian sidewalk spaces, more green 
space, transit priority, shorter walking and crossing distances, 
and allowing for the design of more compact intersections 
with less complex traffic movements. Smaller medians also 
enable tighter turn radii and hence slower turns across the 
median, which can aid in better speed management. Conflict 
and speed management is essential where turns and merges in 
traffic are expected.
Restrict or redesign uncontrolled 
median openings and merges
Access points from/to service lanes in an urban road context 
need to be carefully considered and designed appropriately 
for the expected speed of the road. 
On roads with higher speeds such as arterials or expressways, 
where service roads are commonly used, merges and access 
or egress points should provide sufficient room for vehicles 
to accelerate or decelerate safely, with proper visibility and 
minimizing the risk of potentially severe conflicts. Uncon-
trolled openings in the medians could also be closed off to 
prevent high speed, uncontrolled turns, undesired crossings, 
and other maneuvers across the median. Where demand 
exists for turns across the median, one option may be to 
consider a signal-controlled intersection, or the redesign of 
the opening with sufficient space and visibility to allow for 
safe merging of turning traffic and to minimize the speed dif-
ferential with other traffic, preferably by the implementation 
of merging lanes. 
On local and urban roads, turns, access and egress points are 
more frequent. Traffic calming and lower speed limits can 
help reduce the potential for severe conflicts at these points. 
Elements such as raised crossings, signage, or speed humps 
can also help to slow down vehicles. 
Finally, proper visibility must be assured at such points to 
reduce the possibility of conflicts. Road safety audits and 
inspections can identify elements that restrict driver visibility 
of vehicles joining the road. This can be especially dangerous 
for motorcyclists who already have a less visible profile and 
conspicuity compared to other road users.
Ensure that the right-of-way is 
designed to clearly communicate 
hazards and expected operating 
behaviors
Roads must clearly communicate expected road user behav-
ior. Having lanes of varying widths on the same road may 
encourage unexpected road user behavior leading to an 
increased risk of conflicts. In the case of Buenos Aires, for 
example, some roads have a discontinuous wider side lane 
(over 3.5 m) used for multiple uses such as on-street parking, 
storage of garbage containers, and used by motorcyclists as 
an informal motorcycle lane. The presence of such lanes was 
associated with a 53 percent increase in motorcycle injuries, 
which can be in large part attributable to inconsistencies in 
road cross-section and unclear road design.

5. Combine actions for

actions for other road users
The findings show that there are opportunities to address 
motorcycle safety through action plans that focus on road 
safety for other road users as well. The findings reveal that 
some interventions that work well for other users can also 
positively impact motorcycle safety, making a case to incor-
porate motorcycle safety considerations into other road safety 
and infrastructure projects. 
Pedestrian safety. In Cali, the presence of pedestrian bridges 
is correlated with the number of motorcyclists’ fatalities, but 
roads with more public spaces around it saw fewer motorcy-
cle-related injuries. This shows that pedestrian facilities and 
activity can impact motorcycle safety. Road safety literature 
shows that pedestrian bridges and pedestrian underpasses 
are usually ineffective in urban areas, and also correlated with 
an increased risk for pedestrians (Duduta et al. 2014; Vergel-
Tovar et al. 2020). There are several possible reasons as to why 
pedestrian bridges also show this correlation with motorcy-
clist fatalities. These may include the following:
 ▪ Pedestrian bridges are often built to facilitate or maintain 
higher vehicle speeds by removing pedestrians from the 
roadway. Higher speed environments increase risk for 
all road users. 
 ▪ Pedestrian bridges might be located at already dangerous, 
high-speed, or high traffic volume locations in response to 
existing perceived or actual risks to pedestrians. 
 ▪ Bridges serve to segregate road user types for safety, 
but they may not address any other underlying causes 
of risk at that location—whether it is speed, road 
geometry, or other conflicts, because they do not alter the 
design of the road. 
It therefore may not be surprising that risks for motorcyclists 
are also higher at such locations. However, further study is 
needed to determine if this is the case, and if so, what aspects 
of pedestrian bridge design are contributing to these higher 
risks for motorcyclists. Other environmental conditions such 
as roadway design, traffic speeds, volumes of pedestrians 
crossing, pedestrian behavior, traffic conditions, and the inter-
actions (i.e., conflicts) between motorcyclists and pedestrians 
must be examined to understand the usually complex causal 
effects among these variables. 
Nevertheless, this result indicates that locations with pedes-
trian bridges can be a priority space for investigation and 
intervention, where improvements to bridge and road design 
and other interventions could have a powerful effect in reduc-
ing motorcyclists’ fatalities. 
Wider road widths, large intersections, and longer crossing 
distances also increase risks for both pedestrians and motor-
cyclists, which imply that road diets where lanes are removed, 
and curb extensions can have positive impacts for both 
pedestrians and motorcyclists.
Public transit. The presence of public transport stops and 
stations produced mixed results, and this may be based on 
the design as well as the conflicts generated by these stops. In 
Latin America, the presence of well-designed BRT stations 
was associated with a decrease in motorcycle injuries, but 
the same effect was not seen with regular bus stops. This 
is because BRT projects have typically resulted in major 
retrofits or transformations of the road, with segregated 
lanes for buses and safer intersections and crossings. These 
improvements also benefit motorcyclists because they 
minimize conflicts and provide adequate road space alloca-
tion for all modes. Research has shown that a well-designed 
BRT system can reduce road deaths and injuries because 
of improvements made to the street (Duduta et al. 2014).

Conclusion
Motorcycle safety in low- and middle-income countries is a 
multifaceted issue with strong links to traffic speeds and road 
infrastructure variables. A shift in strategy is needed, moving 
from focusing solely on riders to including road designers 
and decision-makers. Comprehensive speed management, 
more detailed data collection, and system-wide infrastructure 
changes through multi-stakeholder dialogue are key for 
reducing traffic fatalities. For cities with a heavy motorcycle 
dependency, a shift toward safer and sustainable transport 
should be a long-term priority. This section offers next 
steps for implementation as well as for further research on 
motorcycle safety.

Motorcycle safety is a complex issue encompassing econom-
ics, development, equity, planning, and design. In the absence 
of accessible and safer mobility options for all, motorized 
two-wheelers are likely to remain a critical road safety issue 
for LMICs, disproportionately impacting the most vulner-
able population groups. Mitigation efforts have typically 
targeted motorcyclist behavior, personal protective equip-
ment, enforcement, and some specific infrastructure ele-
ments. While these efforts have been shown to be effective in 
reducing risks, understanding and addressing the root causes 
and factors impacting motorcycle use, as well as crashes, 
fatalities, and injuries, is critical, since attention to the early 
parts—error and crash avoidance, rather than mitigating the 
effects of crashes—can lead to better results for motorcyclists' 
safety (ITF 2015). 
This research shows a strong connection between infrastruc-
ture and speed variables on motorcycle safety for LMICs. The 
most important variable explaining motorcycle safety in the 
six cities analyzed was free-flow traffic speeds (measured here 
through nighttime speeds), according to the statistical model 
and the scope of this research. Other significant variables 
found were road width, number of blocks, intersections, traffic 
lights, pedestrian bridges, land use, population density, and 
income level. Because this report could not include data on 
risk exposure, causal relationships between those underlying 
variables were not explored here; nevertheless the findings 
of this report provide clear priorities for interventions and 
further in-depth studies.
Reducing traffic speeds for all road users, road widths, and 
intersection complexity while focusing on high-risk, high-
conflict environments through a comprehensive area-wide 
approach can improve safety for motorized two-wheelers. 
Addressing these road environment factors requires a shift 
in strategy— shifting responsibility for safety from focusing 
primarily on the rider to also include decision-makers and 
road designers, and from primarily location-specific counter-
measures to broader area and systemwide changes. 
Moving toward implementation
Look for quick wins
Systemwide infrastructure changes can require time and 
investment, particularly to address large complex intersections 
or corridor-wide speed management. The use of motorcycles 
has many advantages in terms of convenience and improved 
accessibility for their users. Motorcyclists, however, remain 
a vulnerable category of road users, so making the use of 
motorcycles safer by reducing fatalities and serious injuries 
quickly should be a priority. Finding opportunities to align 
motorcycle safety actions with pedestrian safety actions 
and public transport improvements such as BRT , along 
with speed reductions, can potentially offer quick results in 
reducing motorcycle deaths. Taking a proactive approach to 
addressing mobility challenges and infrastructure quality in 
lower-income areas can bring results to locations where the 
need is greater. 
Speed management plans will  
help all road users
Road speed is a critical road element to address, not just for 
motorcycle safety but to improve safety for all road users. 
Cities with high volumes of motorcycles should consider pri-
oritizing the implementation of comprehensive speed man-
agement plans with citywide application. Such plans should 
include the presence of motorcyclists as a key consideration 
when establishing speed limits and road classifications. This 
can help keep road speeds at safe levels to maximize surviv-
ability in the event of a crash. Even cities with lower motor-
cycle volumes can work to address and reduce speeds in areas 
where motorcycles are most likely to be present.
Data, planning, and scaling is key
This report was developed using readily available data in cities 
and regions where data are often unavailable and unreli-
able. It demonstrates how much information can be gained 
from scarce but available data, and how much more there 
is still to learn from good data analysis. With stronger data 
and monitoring, more targeted, contextual action plans and 
goals can be developed to tackle motorcycle safety. Local 
pilot programs can be developed to test interventions and 
recommendations that can then be scaled up. Motorcycles 
should also be included in citywide mobility, land use, and 
traffic management plans. The data have shown that while

motorcycle safety issues are diverse globally, there are regional 
similarities in motorcycle safety trends, and lessons learned in 
one city can be shared regionally.
Reducing dependency on motorcycle 
use should be a long-term strategy
For cities and regions with heavy motorcycle-dependency, 
comprehensive road design and transport policy can reduce 
the high rates of motorcycle-related fatalities and injuries. 
In rapidly motorizing cities, growing motorcycle fleets can 
lock cities into urban mobility patterns and systems that will 
be increasingly detrimental to road safety. In the long-term 
for such cities, tackling motorcycle safety requires mobility 
strategies and plans that shift away from motorcycle depen-
dency toward safer and more sustainable travel. This can be 
a difficult paradigm shift for regions with high motorcycle 
dependency. Heavy motorcycle dependency can also prevent 
investments in integrated multimodal mobility systems that 
work for all people, including women, children, and people 
with different mobility needs. It is necessary to offer safer, 
more efficient, and affordable alternatives to motorcycles, 
make increased investments in robust public transportation 
systems, plan for high-quality infrastructure for walking and 
cycling, and integrate land-use planning with mobility plan-
ning; doing so can also make roads safer for all road users. 
Multistakeholder dialogue  
is necessary
Due to the complex nature of motorcycle safety issues, mov-
ing away from motorcycle dependency would require open 
dialogue and strong policy backing to ensure that all voices 
are heard. In many places, motorcycles are the source of liveli-
hoods and economic opportunity. In other places, motorcycles 
are the backbone of robust informal transit systems, delivery 
services, and unregulated public transport. Cities would need 
to address these needs through open dialogue and collabora-
tion with users and citizens to ensure equitable access to 
mobility options and opportunities. There may also be lessons 
to consider from the way cities have managed other informal 
or paratransit systems.

Next steps for research
The study looked primarily at infrastructure elements with 
available data from six cities. More data from other cit-
ies would help broaden the scope and applicability of the 
recommendations and identify regional differences. There are 
also infrastructure elements that are missing in the study. For 
example, one limitation of this research is that it excludes 
variables such as weather, street lighting, intersection design, 
and road surface conditions. Such data are difficult to obtain 
but likely to influence safety, especially for motorcycles; 
other elements that could not be considered include vehicle 
ownership and use, mode share impacts, the role of delivery 
and freight services, the types of crashes (not available in all 
cities), and the role of dedicated motorcycle infrastructure. 
These variables could have a significant impact on motorcycle 
safety and should be considered in future studies. 
Additionally, the research did not consider interactions 
between variables such as speeds, infrastructure, and land use. 
The identification strategy of our statistical analysis needs to 
be stronger (Angrist 2022) to properly establish causal rela-
tionships and know which elements of the road infrastructure 
would require modifications or complements (Elvik 2024). 
More research on this matter is needed to also better under-
stand safe speeds, given the context for motorcyclists. Despite 
these limitations, the research provides valuable insights into 
motorcycle safety and can inform future efforts to improve 
road safety for motorcyclists.
Detailed information on types of crashes affecting motor-
cyclists was not available and therefore not included in this 
model. The collection and analysis of this information in 
the future is necessary to learn more about factors that lead 
to road crashes. Researchers can use this to identify crash 
patterns and understand how to more effectively design 
infrastructure that prevents conflicts and serious traffic 
crashes that affect motorcyclists’ safety. In-depth studies 
could identify issues that are not currently visible due to the 
underreporting and reporting biases in police crash data 
and develop a taxonomy of crash patterns (Wegman and 
Schepers 2024). The demand levels of crossing movements 
and the behavior of all road users at locations with pedestrian 
bridges or intersections can serve as an example of situations 
where more data and better reporting mechanisms are a 
strict necessity.

Future research must study the road behavior of motorcyclists 
in more detail, using proper methodologies for data collec-
tion and analysis of the desired information, which are not 
usually collected by local authorities. The obedience of traffic 
rules at different types of roads and built environments, as 
well as intersections, is a topic of special interest, particularly 
behaviors such as speeding and red-light running, which are 
relatively frequent among motorcyclists (Esmaeli et al. 2022; 
Satiennam et al. 2018). 
The growing role of motorcyclists in urban delivery services 
are creating new trends in urban transportation. Motorcycles 
offer lower cost and faster delivery operations for both large 
on-demand delivery services, as well as small businesses. 
However, they may also be posing a significant safety risk to 
road users and delivery drivers due to economic incentives to 
deliver quickly, or even penalties or pressure to meet deliv-
ery time constraints. While safe speeds and infrastructure 
interventions mentioned in this report can improve safety 
for road users, more research is needed on policy and regula-
tory interventions that can improve safety associated with 
motorcycles operating freight, delivery, and transport services 
on demand in the gig economy (Christie and Ward 2023).
Considerations of lane width and road geometry should 
be analyzed in more detail. While safe urban street design 
recommends the use of narrower lane widths to reduce 
vehicle speeds (Welle et al. 2015) and improve road safety 
performance, this variable seems to present a mixed picture 
in the case of motorcyclists. Based on the results, the effect 
of lane width may therefore be dependent on local road user 
behavior and the nature of the lane itself. For motorized 
two-wheelers, especially in LMICs, wider lane widths typi-
cally encourage the use of filtering or lane-sharing to weave 
through stopped or slow traffic. The evidence on the safety of 
this practice is also mixed (Clabaux et al. 2017; Sperley and 
Pietz 2010). Therefore, a general recommendation cannot yet 
be formulated, and this must be subject to further research, 
considering diverse scenarios of motorcycle circulation and 
infrastructure development. 
Finally, there is also a real need to examine how to design 
intersections to mitigate the risk for motorcyclists. While this 
study highlights the hazards motorcyclists face at intersection 
locations, more research is needed to develop modifications 
to the design or control of intersections that can specifically 
benefit motorcyclist safety.

The incidence rate ratio (IRR) is a useful metric as it becomes 
easier to interpret and analyze the variation of risks in terms of 
exposure to a condition, which in this case is a condition of the 
built environment.
Where the value of IRR is greater than 1, it indicates that the risk 
due to exposure (i.e., expected count of crashes) is greater as the 
value of the independent variable increases by one unit.
Discussion on key variables:
 ▪ Speed: As the value of free flow speed increases by 1 km/h, the 
risk of having motorcycle crashes would be 1.3 percent higher 
in average for the case of Accra and 5.8 percent in average for 
the case of Nairobi. 
 ▪ Blocks per km: The increase of 1 block (i.e., reduction of the 
average length of the block) increases the risk of motorcycle 
crashes between 5.6 and 8.6 percent.
 ▪ Other variables: Variables such as land use and sidewalk 
condition, which are influential for the risk of crashes, become 
more difficult to interpret without a prior knowledge of the 
traffic conditions and typical behaviors in traffic of all the road 
users, motorists, and nonmotorists.

Glossary 
Arterial  High-capacity or major urban roads that deliver or carry traffic from or between local collector roads 
and urban centers
Corridor  Linear distinct length of roadway carrying one or more modes of traffic
Design speed   Target speed at which drivers are intended to travel on a street.
Exclusive motorcycle facilities   Design features to segregate motorcycles from main traffic using marked, designated spaces on the 
carriageway, like motorcycle lanes or motorcycle boxes. 
Free flow speed  Average speed that a vehicle would travel if there were no congestion or other adverse conditions 
(such as bad weather or poor visibility).
Lateral medians   Barriers that divide carriageways—normally a service road and a central roadway—with traffic going 
in the same direction. Many arterial roads have been designed with this type of median dividing the 
roadways to allow faster vehicular flow in the central roadways with slower vehicles or local traffic in 
the service roadways. 
Peri-urban   Referring to or located in an area immediately adjacent or surrounding a city or urban area. 
Safe speeds  A safe speed is the speed that maximizes survivability in the event of crash, allows a driver to react 
to expected road hazards, and stop or maneuver safely under the prevailing conditions of traffic and 
road environment.
Sight distance  The length of roadway ahead of a vehicle that a driver needs to be able to see clearly. It is important 
for drivers on a road to see far enough down to recognize an object in the path, potential conflicts at 
junctions, traffic control devices, oncoming traffic, or vulnerable road users and react appropriately 
and in time to avoid a crash.

About WRI Ross Center  
for Sustainable Cities 
WRI Ross Center for Sustainable Cities works to make urban 
sustainability a reality. Global research and on-the-ground 
experience in Brazil, China, Colombia, India, Mexico, Türkiye, and 
the United States combine to spur action that improves life for 
millions of people.
