### Highlights

- Large, decentralised countries such as Indonesia, Brazil and India must overcome fragmented adaptation governance and ensure vertical policy coherence across all governmental levels to facilitate more robust and effective climate adaptation actions. A mandatory national coordination framework with clear mandates and binding protocols is essential to achieving this.
- We used a qualitative, case-study-based, exploratory approach, combining literature reviews and stakeholder engagement, to examine the extent to which climate adaptation is integrated vertically and aligned with national policies and local needs across Indonesia, Brazil and India.
- Based on a comparative analysis, the study reveals common barriers to effective climate adaptation, including governance and institutional fragmentation, uneven capacity across governmental levels and limited financial resources.
- To address these barriers and advance locally led, resilient climate adaptation aligned with the Global Goal on Adaptation, we recommend strengthening cross-sectoral governance, building institutional capacity and improving data and information systems.

### Executive summary

**As the impacts of climate change intensify, large, decentralised countries face challenges in implementing effective adaptation measures.** Based on our findings in the case countries, national adaptation policies often cannot be implemented in local areas most vulnerable to climate risks, while sub-national governments struggle with limited resources, weak coordination and inadequate access to climate-related data.

**Vertical integration—defined here as the alignment and coordination of national and sub-national governments in planning, implementation, and monitoring and evaluation—can help address these gaps.** It ensures

By fostering coordination, aligning funding and promoting inclusive decision-making across different levels of government, countries can transform policy commitments into tangible outcomes that build resilience.

**There is currently strong momentum to advance vertical integration in climate adaptation**, supported by the emerging United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC) adaptation indicators and initiatives such as the Coalition for High Ambition Multilevel Partnerships for Climate Action. Vertical integration can accelerate climate adaptation by embedding shared indicators across governance levels, strengthening coordination and aligning finance and implementation efforts. With growing global momentum following the 30th Conference of the Parties to the UNFCCC, there is an opportunity to establish clearer guidelines for decentralised coordination, while promoting a more locally led adaptation approach that reflects community needs and contexts. Ultimately, strong vertical integration would enhance governance, improve multisectoral collaboration and enable more effective and responsive climate action.

## About this working paper

**In this working paper, we examine how the vertical integration of climate change adaptation can be strengthened, using Indonesia, Brazil and India as comparative case studies.** We selected these countries based on their similar governance nature: large, diverse, democratic nations that emphasise multilevel governance, making vertical coordination both important and highly relevant.

**Drawing on desk research, structured and unstructured interviews, and focus group discussions conducted by WRI between January and September 2025, we provide empirical evidence on common barriers, highlight opportunities and offer practical recommendations for policymakers, planners and development partners on how coordination across governmental levels can be enhanced.** These findings also contribute to ongoing global discussions under the UNFCCC relating to the Global Goal on Adaptation as well as the design of national adaptation plans.

## Key findings

**Indonesia, Brazil and India face several common barriers to coordinating climate adaptation across governmental levels (see Table ES-1):**

- Fragmented governance: weak institutional coordination, hindering the integration of climate change adaptation
- Capacity gaps: different levels of understanding and advancement of climate adaptation issues among national, sub-national and local government officials, including varying understanding of the concept of climate adaptation at the sub-national and local levels
- Financing gaps: limited financial and human resources for managing and implementing climate adaptation policies, especially at the sub-national level due to a lack of data and accountability gaps

**The three countries face similar barriers to vertically integrating climate adaptation, including unclear governance roles, limited sub-national financing, local capacity gaps and fragmented coordination.** Stronger alignment of financing, data and accountability systems is key for overcoming these challenges.

**Based on these findings, we have identified four key recommendations for these emerging-market countries to advance the vertical integration of climate adaptation:**

- Improve governance across sectors and institutions, including by increasing coordination; strengthening accountability across sectors and levels of government; and ensuring that roles, responsibilities and decision-making processes are clearly assigned and can be effectively monitored for vertical integration.
- Enhance the capacity of government officials at different levels to plan, implement, monitor and evaluate the climate adaptation programme—thereby strengthening understanding of climate adaptation issues—and improve accountability across all project phases, enabling institutions to deliver on commitments and be held responsible for results.
- Strengthen data and information and harmonise risk assessment methodologies and systems to reinforce accountability through robust monitoring and evaluation of climate adaptation progress at different levels, allowing transparent tracking of adaptation progress and improving credibility to attract investment.
- Ensure that financial mechanisms allow funds (both national and international sources such as funding from multilateral development banks) to be allocated and used in a traceable and accountable manner across national and sub-national levels.

## Introduction

### The imperative of vertical integration for climate change adaptation

The unprecedented global challenge of climate change requires robust and coordinated adaptation efforts at all levels of governance. This will ensure that national-level plans and policies are implemented not only in capital cities, but throughout countries. There will also be a need for vertical implementation, financing and technology to match the scale, speed and scope of the adaptation actions required.

Vertical integration in this context is the intentional and strategic linkage between national and sub-national governance levels across planning; implementation; and monitoring, evaluation and learning (MEL). It enables national climate strategies and adaptation priorities to reflect and respond to local realities (Rodríguez et al. 2023; Dazé et al. 2016; Ziervogel et al. 2019), thereby channelling critical resources to the communities and systems most in need. In addition, vertical integration is important for securing the buy-in of sub-national governments, which tend to be the first responders to climate hazards and those implementing climate adaptation actions.

Vertical integration has proven challenging for many countries—particularly large, diverse ones with decentralised forms of government. \( ^{1} \)  In such countries, a wider range of physical assets, as well as more diverse human and environmental systems, means that climate risks will have a range of impacts and require distinct solutions (Rodríguez et al. 2023). India's National Action Plan on Climate Change (NAPCC) mandated the development of State Action Plans on Climate Change (SAPCCs), demonstrating efforts toward vertical integration of climate adaptation (Government of India

2018). However, integration is not seamless or without difficulty, as there are still gaps in financing and in the technical capacities of officers.

This paper explores the challenges and opportunities of vertical integration in large, decentralised countries by focusing on three cases: Indonesia, Brazil and India. We selected these three countries as case studies based on their similar governance nature: large, diverse, democratic nations that emphasise multilevel governance, \( ^{2} \) which includes sub-national authorities that address more local issues (European Union 2024; Sunkad 2025; Ward et al. 2010) including climate-related challenges. We evaluated whether and how these countries are facilitating policy alignment, including in supporting elements such as flows of information across levels of government as well as how well the contexts, needs and preferences of sub-national entities are being reported back to and integrated and reflected at the national level. We also compared the strengths and weaknesses of the relevant plans, policies and enabling environments of each country. The comparative analysis identifies the factors critical for effective vertical integration and provides recommendations for policymakers and stakeholders to facilitate more efficient, equitable and sustainable adaptation outcomes through improved vertical integration.

Adaptation strategies must address the varying climate risks at multiple levels of governance to be effectively implemented. Responsibilities for planning, implementation, and monitoring and evaluation must be shared and aligned across the national and sub-national levels. This coordination facilitates budgeting processes at the sub-national level that are aligned with national adaptation priorities and helps channel additional funds from both national and international sources to where they are needed most, in coordination with local needs and priorities. In addition, the national budgeting process needs to align with the sub-national reality. Ultimately, vertical integration makes resources, capacities and knowledge more accessible to a wider range of stakeholders engaged in adaptation at different levels (C40 Cities 2020; Dazé et al. 2016).

Effective vertical integration maximises the potential of each level of governance through collective efforts and promotes the exchange of information and resources for a multiplier effect in the economy, moving toward a more sustainable future. Rather than a one-way flow of mandates (i.e. information and resources from the national level to more localised levels), vertical integration provides a two-way street in which sub-national jurisdictions continuously feed their experiences, needs and preferences back to higher levels of governance, translating context-specific adaptation actions to the national level and ensuring that progress is being made. Strategic linkages between national and sub-national governance levels must be in place throughout the adaptation cycle: planning, implementation and MEL (Rodríguez et al. 2023).

Previous studies have identified some common barriers to vertically integrating climate adaptation across countries, including the following:

- An absence of clear processes that link national and sub-national planning efforts, with limited clarity on roles and responsibilities and a lack of formal mechanisms (LDCEG 2012)
- Insufficient financial resources making their way to the sub-national level, impeding the scaling up of adaptation actions (LDCEG 2012)
- Limited capacity and necessary skills among sub-national stakeholders to effectively tackle the challenges of climate adaptation (LDCEG 2012)
- Structural barriers (e.g. silos and fragmented coordination, non-aligned policies) that prevent the full integration of sub-national and local governments into adaptation processes (Touboul et al. 2023)

In addition to these shared challenges, certain countries may face circumstances that render some of these barriers less relevant, so we examined the climate risk and policy environments, as well as the barriers to vertical integration, in our focus countries: Indonesia, Brazil and India.

## Country contexts: Indonesia, Brazil and India

### Indonesia’s climate risks and climate adaptation policy

As an archipelagic nation with the world’s third-longest coastline, Indonesia is highly prone to droughts, storms, floods, tidal waves and water pollution (Gaborit 2022). These risks are exacerbated by climate change, which intensifies rainfall patterns and results in more severe and frequent flooding with varying impacts across Indonesia’s regions (Vij et al. 2017).

As populations grow, urban areas face challenges in adapting to climate change. Projections indicate that approximately 72.9 percent of Indonesia’s population will be living in urban areas by 2045, compared with just over half in 2012 (Salim and Hudalah 2020; Gaborit 2022). Cities such as Cirebon in West Java—the province that experienced the highest number of disasters in 2022 (DIBI BNPB n.d.)—struggle with issues such as limited spatial planning, high population density and rapid land conversion (Gaborit 2022).

Meanwhile, in rural areas, droughts in East and West Nusa Tenggara have reduced crop yields and water availability, with rice production in 2024 falling by 7.18 percent (55,084 tons) compared with 2023 due to the El Niño event (Government of Indonesia 2025a). With current ambitions, these climate-induced hazards could lead to severe economic losses, as evidenced by the period 1991–2020, during which intensifying floods in the western and northern regions, recurrent droughts in the southeast and increasing coastal hazards in northern Java resulted in recurring losses equivalent to about 0.61 percent of gross domestic product annually, or approximately Rp 102–115 trillion (about US$5.7 trillion–6.4 trillion) (Government of Indonesia 2022).

As a consequence, Indonesia cannot achieve the Global Goal on Adaptation under the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC) without strong and sustained commitment at the national level. In response, the government of Indonesia has begun integrating more ambitious climate adaptation targets into the country’s nationally determined contributions (NDCs) and national development plans with some targets having been further formalised through various regulations and policies.

For example, the National Long-Term Development Plan (2025–45), coordinated by the Ministry of National Development Planning (Bappenas), prioritises climate resilience and adaptation to reduce potential economic losses due to climate change (Government of Indonesia 2024a). The government is prioritising climate resilience strategies in four main sectors—marine and coastal; water; agriculture, forestry and fisheries; and health—in priority locations.³ These strategies are further elaborated upon in the policy directions of these four sectors

The government of Indonesia has also addressed its climate change adaptation commitment through its Long-Term Strategy for Low Carbon and Climate Resilience 2050; its Enhanced NDC, submitted to the UNFCCC in 2022; and its Second NDC (SNDC), submitted to the UNFCCC in 2025. In the same year, the government also submitted its national adaptation plan (NAP) to the UNFCCC. This was achieved with the technical support of the United Nations Development Programme through the Green Climate Fund. The NAP provides a national framework to translate NDC commitments into a coherent set of operational measures (Government of Indonesia 2025a).

These policy documents are further underpinned by Presidential Regulation No. 110/2025, which governs the implementation of carbon economic value instruments and the control of national greenhouse gas emissions. This regulation established the framework for climate change adaptation actions, covering the stages of planning, implementation, and monitoring and evaluation. Under this framework, adaptation planning follows a top-down approach. National-level ministries are mandated to conduct climate change impact assessments, establish climate resilience baselines, set climate resilience targets and formulate adaptation action plans. These plans act as a foundation for the creation of regional or sub-national adaptation action plans (Government of Indonesia 2025c).

Although provincial and regency or city governments are granted the authority to develop their own climate change adaptation plans, these sub-national action plans must align with higher-level adaptation frameworks in addition to referring to the respective sub-national development planning documents. This requirement may create implementation challenges, particularly considering timing inconsistencies: The NAP was finalised and submitted to the UNFCCC at the end of 2025, whereas sub-national development planning documents were required to be established earlier, by August 2025.

This misalignment in planning cycles may limit the extent to which the latest national adaptation priorities and frameworks can be effectively integrated into sub-national plans. As a result, there is a risk that local adaptation strategies may not fully reflect updated climate risk assessments or national policy directions, potentially leading to gaps in coherence, reduced effectiveness of adaptation actions and the need for subsequent revisions or adjustments at the sub-national level.

## Brazil’s climate risks and climate adaptation policy

Brazil exhibits medium-to-high socio-environmental vulnerability to climate change, a condition shaped by its vast territorial extension, climatic diversity and socio-economic inequalities. According to the *First Biennial Transparency Report of Brazil*, the past decade revealed a dramatic intensification of climate-related disasters (Government of Brazil 2024a). Approximately 83 percent of Brazilian municipalities were affected, exposing more than 177 million people and damaging over 1.57 million homes. The estimated financial and material losses surpassed R$421 billion (about $75 million) in this period (Government of Brazil 2024a). At least eight extreme events of national relevance have occurred since 2011, ranging from severe floods and droughts to unprecedented wildfires, including the historic floods in Rio Grande do Sul and large-scale wildfires recorded in 2024.$^{4}$

Future projections reinforce this pattern of intensifying risks. With high confidence, climate models anticipate rising maximum and minimum temperatures; more frequent and longer-lasting heat waves; persistent extreme rainfall in the North, Northeast, Center-West and Southeast; and increasing drought episodes in the Northeast, Center-West and Southeast (Government of Brazil 2024a). Severe wind events and coastal impacts, such as sea level rise, ocean acidification and marine heat waves, are also projected to intensify. Regional disparities remain critical: While certain areas of the North and Northeast may experience higher annual rainfall, others face intensified drought conditions. These variations are influenced by geographical factors such as ocean proximity and coastal topography (Government of Brazil 2024a).

The combination of structural vulnerabilities—including high population density along the coast, an economy heavily reliant on agriculture, high poverty rates and marked regional inequalities—amplifies exposure to climatic hazards. Moreover, the presence of the world’s largest tropical rainforest, with its ecological and cultural richness, underscores the global significance of Brazil’s climate risks. Taken together, the evidence indicates a scenario where both the frequency and severity of extreme events are expected to grow, with profound implications for ecosystems, infrastructure and human security (Government of Brazil 2024a).

Brazil’s institutional responses to the climate hazards and risks of the past two decades have been uneven. The National Policy on Climate Change, established in 2009, laid out the groundwork for regulatory and governance frameworks. Building on this, its NAP, launched in 2016 and resubmitted to the UNFCCC in February 2026 (MoECC 2025d), sought to integrate adaptation into 11 strategic sectors, ranging from agriculture and water resources to vulnerable populations and coastal zones (MoE 2016). At the international level, Brazil’s second NDC, submitted in 2024 during the 29th Conference of the Parties to the UNFCCC (COP29), consolidated adaptation as a central component of national climate policy (Government of Brazil 2024b). It explicitly linked adaptation commitments to social justice and human rights, recognising the differentiated vulnerabilities of Indigenous and traditional populations. Complementing this, the AdaptaBrasil platform has expanded its scope to monitor risks across strategic sectors, including health, energy, transport and infrastructure, with ongoing updates to include biodiversity and Indigenous peoples.

There are sub-national initiatives that illustrate the decentralisation of adaptation governance in the country. Law 14.904/2024 established guidelines for adaptation planning at the municipal, state and national levels, with an emphasis on supporting the most vulnerable municipalities. Concurrently, federal programmes such as the Resilient Green Cities Programme and AdaptaCidades were launched to strengthen urban resilience, promote sustainable practices and build local capacity for accessing climate finance. The latter programme, initially designed to train 200 municipalities, expanded its scope to 450, supported by the reinforcement of the Climate Fund, whose resources were set to increase to R$20 billion (about $3.9 billion) in 2025 (MoECC 2025a, 2025b).

These policy innovations are embedded within a broader commitment to ‘climate federalism’, formally adopted in 2024 through Resolution No. 3 of the Federation Council. This agreement established shared responsibilities among

the union, states and municipalities, aligned with the Coalition for High Ambition Multilevel Partnerships (CHAMP) principles, to ensure multilevel coordination and progressive integration of climate policies into long-term development planning. Collectively, these advances highlight an ongoing institutional transition: from fragmented and reactive measures to a more structured, justice-oriented and multilevel adaptation framework, with Brazil positioning itself as a potential global leader in climate resilience (MoECC 2025c).

## India’s climate risks and climate adaptation policies

An analysis of India found that 27 of the country’s 35 states face high vulnerability to extreme hydro-meteorological disasters and their compounded impacts (Mohanty and Wadhawan 2021). The study created a climate vulnerability index using indicators for exposure at the micro level, assessing sensitivity through spatio-temporal analysis and analysing adaptive capacity through socio-economic and governance mechanisms. The study applied a framework based on that presented in the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change’s special report *Managing the Risks of Extreme Events and Disasters to Advance Climate Change Adaptation* (IPCC 2012).

Analysis also shows the western and central zones are more vulnerable to droughts; northern and north-eastern zones to floods; and eastern and southern zones to extreme cyclones, floods and droughts (Mohanty and Wadhawan 2021) (see Figure 1).

In 2008, India released its initial National Action Plan on Climate Change (NAPCC), conceptualising it through eight national missions to promote research and development, energy efficiency, sustainable natural resource management and other strategic priorities for climate action (Government of India 2018). Together, the missions envision mitigation and adaptation priorities across sectors through top-down mandates and target-setting. More information about the NAPCC can be found in Appendix B.

While the NAPCC defines priorities across the eight national missions, State Action Plans on Climate Change are more specific plans rooted in the state’s context. They consider climate vulnerabilities by mapping risk exposure and community adaptive capacities, as illustrated in Figure 2. ## Methodology

This study employed a qualitative case study and exploratory research design to examine the vertical integration of climate adaptation in three large, decentralised countries: Indonesia, Brazil and India. The aim was to assess the extent of vertical integration in these countries and to analyse the barriers, challenges and opportunities that could inform policy improvements in climate adaptation planning, implementation, and monitoring and evaluation. The analysis was grounded in a comparative qualitative approach combining a literature review of policy and legal documents and previous research, and stakeholder engagement across three countries, as detailed in Table 1.

Between January and August 2025, the WRI Global and WRI India teams drew on their experiences working on climate adaptation and its governance and conducted desk-based analysis to enrich the cross-country discussions and outline how to apply them in different countries. During the same period, the WRI Brasil team gathered relevant data and information through a review of desk-based documents and engaged with government stakeholders. Between February and September 2025, the WRI Indonesia team conducted data collection and analysis through desk studies, structured and unstructured interviews, and focus group discussions analysing the barriers and opportunities to advancing vertical integration of climate adaptation across different levels of government through the creation of the country's climate adaptation framework (see Table 2). This study has some limitations. First, the analysis focuses on Indonesia, Brazil and India, which, while representative of large, decentralised, emerging economies, may not fully capture the diversity of governance structures, fiscal capacities and socio-political contexts in other regions. Second, data availability and comparability across countries remain constrained, which may affect the depth of cross-country comparison. Future research could build on this study by expanding the scope of analysis to include a broader set of countries, particularly smaller or more centralised states, to assess the applicability of these findings across different governance contexts. There is also a need for more quantitative and longitudinal analysis to assess the effectiveness of vertical integration mechanisms over time, including the use of standardised indicators such as those being developed under the UNFCCC framework. Further research on the role of local actors and community-led adaptation as well as financing instruments, integration of data systems and accountability mechanisms would also help strengthen understanding of how these elements interact to enable more effective and scalable climate adaptation outcomes.

## Results and discussion

### Challenges and opportunities to effective vertical integration

This section reviews some of the challenges and opportunities we identified when comparing the three countries' experiences advancing climate adaptation actions through vertical integration (see Tables C-1–C-3, Appendix C, for an overview). Information and data were collected from national and sub-national government officials from each country.

#### Indonesia

In Indonesia, the challenges and opportunities associated with achieving more effective vertical integration of climate adaptation can be summarised into four main areas: coordination, capacity, financing, and measurement and reporting.

1. The proliferation of policies and instruments without clear authority has led to overlapping mandates and fragmented implementation across sectors and governance levels. This has created confusion and complexity for local governments and slowed effective integration of climate adaptation. National leadership (e.g. the president or coordinating ministries) can establish a clear and binding coordination mechanism (such as a presidential directive) that defines the role and mandate of a lead agency for adaptation. This should be supported by streamlining overlapping planning instruments and strengthening enforcement. At the same time, existing systems like the National Registry System, Vulnerability Index Data Information System and Village Climate Risk Index can be integrated into an interoperable data & measurement, reporting and verification (MRV) framework, while targeted capacity-building and simpler tools can help sub-national governments move from administrative compliance to more effective and integrated adaptation implementation.
2. Uniform technical requirements for climate analysis are applied despite uneven capacities among sub-national governments across regions, resulting in procedural compliance rather than meaningful use in decision-making. Less-resourced areas are particularly disadvantaged in meeting these expectations. National ministries (e.g. Bappenas, Ministry of Environment) can provide targeted technical assistance and mentoring to sub-national governments, in collaboration with universities, research institutions and development partners, through, for example, training on climate risk modelling, development of local indicators and hands-on support in integrating adaptation into regional planning.
3. Although various financing instruments exist, they remain fragmented, limited in scale and often skewed toward mitigation, making adaptation projects less attractive to investors. In addition, underdeveloped investment pipelines at the sub-national level (where projects are often not translated into bankable proposals) limit access to national, private and international funding, thereby constraining the vertical integration of adaptation priorities across levels of government. ## Brazil

We identified six challenges and opportunities for Brazil in the areas of legal and institutional frameworks; multi-scalar and cross-sectoral integration; intergovernmental coordination; participatory governance and local innovation; capacity gaps and inequalities; and integration as an opportunity and a source of tension.

1. Although Brazil has a solid legal and institutional foundation for climate adaptation, institutional fragmentation, uneven municipal capacities and political asymmetries undermine the effective implementation of these mandates. To address this challenge, Brazil must strengthen its vertical coordination mechanisms to ensure that mandates are translated into effective action. This can be achieved by providing greater institutional clarity; building municipal capacity, both financial and in terms of human resources; and offering federal support.
2. There is insufficient coordination on adaptation action across territorial scales (from neighbourhoods to metropolitan regions and states) and alignment of policy sectors such as housing, sanitation, mobility, education and environmental protection. To avoid siloed approaches and ensure that adaptation is embedded in urban planning, infrastructure, development and social services, the Brazilian government should integrate critical urban sectors and multiple territorial scales, making adaptation more coherent, results-oriented and aligned with development policies.

3. Intergovernmental coordination is hindered by political discontinuities and fiscal imbalances, which leave municipalities with limited resources. However, there is an opportunity to strengthen the Federal Council and the Interministerial Committee on Climate Change as drivers of the coordination and vertical integration of adaptation policies and measures.
4. Only some of Brazil's sub-national governments carry out bottom-up climate adaptation assessments. An opportunity exists to fill in this gap by scaling up pioneering city-level initiatives in risk mapping, adaptive master plans and vulnerability indexes, supported by federal incentives and technical assistance.
5. Many municipalities lack the technical and financial capacity to collect climate data, implement nature-based solutions or upgrade infrastructure to resilient standards. To address this, Brazil must invest in inclusive, participatory processes and municipal capacity-building, ensuring that adaptation policies promote climate justice, reduce inequalities and empower vulnerable groups.
6. Vertical integration is a pathway to systemic resilience, but also a source of structural tension. This tension stems from the existing normative framework, which relies on bridging institutional gaps, investing in municipal capacity, improving intergovernmental coordination and ensuring participatory processes that prioritise the most vulnerable. In response to this challenge, the country should transform these tensions into a cohesive national adaptation strategy that combines climate justice, institutional strengthening, intergovernmental coordination and municipal capacity-building.

## India

In India, we identified four challenges to and opportunities for more effective vertical integration of climate adaptation: inconsistency in the conduct of vulnerability assessments; lack of a common and standardised framework for integrating resilience and adaptation actions with development planning; lack of integration of climate adaptation within disaster management plans; and lack of high-resolution climate and vulnerability data.

1. Different technical institutions support states, districts and cities in carrying out vulnerability assessments, but the frequency and methodology adopted are not always consistent. This challenge can be addressed by introducing a vulnerability assessment-driven approach at the national and sub-national levels to support the development of a concrete MEL framework or process that goes beyond output and financial progress.

## Recommendations for improving vertical integration

Based on these findings, we've identified some key recommendations for large, decentralised countries like Indonesia, Brazil and India to advance the vertical integration of climate adaptation.

### Indonesia

#### 1. Establish a clear and empowered coordination mechanism

While the proliferation of adaptation-related instruments, such as the Environmental Protection and Management Plan, National Action Plan for Climate Change Adaptation, National Climate Change Registry System and Village Climate Risk Index, has strengthened Indonesia's adaptation policy framework, it has also created overlapping mandates and fragmented implementation. Although there are positive examples, such as the use of the Vulnerability Index Data Information System across ministries and levels of government, overall coordination remains uneven. To address this, the government of Indonesia should do the following:

- Establish a clear and binding coordination mechanism to harmonise adaptation planning, implementation and reporting across sectors and levels of government
- Introduce a presidential directive that defines a formal coordination hierarchy and standardised protocols, transforming ad hoc collaboration into an institutional obligation
- Create a single authoritative coordination point to reduce overlapping mandates and improve policy coherence
- Strengthen horizontal coordination alongside vertical integration to enhance cross-sectoral, multilevel and multi-stakeholder collaboration in climate adaptation governance

#### 2. Adopt a differentiated and supportive capacity development strategy

Uniform policy requirements, such as ecosystem-based planning and climate risk modelling, must be complemented by differentiated technical support that reflects the starting capacity of each region. The national government, together with partners such as universities, nongovernmental organisations and development agencies, should implement a tiered capacity-building framework, which could include the following:

- Mentoring programmes pairing advanced regions or institutions with those still developing capacity

- Regular formal and informal coordination on climate adaptation
- Targeted technical assistance focused on practical tools for vulnerability assessments and adaptation planning
- Phased milestones that allow for gradual compliance, aligned with regional readiness

### 3. Strengthen the financing ecosystem for adaptation

Although Indonesia has pioneered instruments channelled through the Environmental Fund Management Agency, adaptation finance remains underdeveloped. A key challenge lies in the limited flow of private finance into adaptation, which constrains the scaling up of cost-effective measures. Increasing private sector participation (through blended finance, risk-sharing mechanisms and clearer policy signals) can help lower the overall cost of adaptation and accelerate implementation by mobilising resources beyond public funding. Therefore, to make adaptation more attractive to investors and support the integration of resilience-building features into investments, national agencies should do the following:

- Quantify and communicate the co-benefits of adaptation, such as reduced disaster recovery costs and improved productivity. For example, Brandon et al. (2025) found that every $1 invested in adaptation can yield over $10.50 in benefits, with average returns of 20–27 percent.
- This includes guarantees to de-risk investments, grants for capacity-building, sovereign debt instruments to finance climate-resilient infrastructure and risk transfer mechanisms such as parametric insurance, catastrophe bonds and contingency loans. Such instruments should recognise the protective value of adaptation, rather than relying solely on conventional cash flow returns.

### 4. Enhance measurement, reporting and learning systems

To assess progress consistently across governance levels, Indonesia should strengthen its adaptation monitoring framework by doing the following:

- Aligning national indicators (e.g. economic loss reduction) with local-level metrics that capture social and ecological resilience outcomes
- Integrating these indicators into planning systems such as the National Long-Term Development Plan, National Medium-Term Development Plan, Regional Long-Term Development Plan and Regional Medium-Term Development Plan

- Ensuring data interoperability among national systems (e.g. National Climate Change Registry System) and local information platforms
- Developing a unified adaptation monitoring framework that draws on UNFCCC guidance and national long-term strategies to enable evidence-based policy learning and resource allocation

### Brazil

Brazil has made substantial progress in developing a comprehensive legal and policy framework for climate adaptation. However, effective vertical integration will depend on operationalising coordination mechanisms, strengthening municipal capacities, improving access to finance, expanding localised data systems and embedding climate justice principles in participatory governance. These actions are essential to transforming Brazil’s adaptation agenda from fragmented initiatives into a coherent, multilevel governance system capable of delivering equitable and resilient outcomes across its diverse territories. The following are recommendations for improving the vertical integration of climate adaptation in Brazil, covering coordination, capacity-building, financing, data accessibility and integration, participatory governance, justice-oriented governance, and sectoral and planning integration.

#### 1. Institutionalise a multilevel coordination mechanism for adaptation

Brazil should consolidate and formalise its emerging institutional architecture for adaptation by establishing a clear, strong and participatory mechanism for coordinating among the federal, state and municipal levels. The existing Interministerial Committee on Climate Change, which includes an inter-federative chamber with representation from states and municipalities, and the Federation Council represent important foundations but are quite new. The mandate has yet to be fully clarified since the National Action Plan was not adopted. It will require a stronger mandate, consistent participation and defined responsibilities for each governance level on how the verticalisation will happen, not only for adaptation actions but also for cross-cutting issues such as MRV, means of implementation and climate action, among others. Such institutional clarity would strengthen accountability and create synergies among federal programmes, such as AdaptaCidades and the Resilient Green Cities Programme, and other adaptation actions conducted by states and municipalities.

## 2. Strengthen sub-national capacities and reduce territorial inequalities

To advance vertical integration, Brazil must invest in differentiated capacity development across municipalities and states, reflecting their uneven technical, financial and administrative capabilities. A tiered support framework should be designed to provide tailored assistance to municipalities based on risk exposure, population size and institutional readiness. This framework could include long-term training programmes on how to conduct vulnerability assessments, project implementation and access to climate finance; partnerships among universities, federal agencies and local governments; and the expansion of national initiatives such as AdaptaCidades to include technical mentoring and peer learning. Prioritising capacity-building in under-resourced regions would address territorial inequalities and ensure that adaptation measures reach the most vulnerable communities.

## 3. Expand access to climate finance and strengthen local implementation

Despite the increase in Climate Fund resources, access to adaptation finance at the municipal level remains limited. To address this structural gap, Brazil should undertake comprehensive regulatory reform to establish enabling conditions that facilitate the design and implementation of simplified financial mechanisms. These reforms should adapt accessibility criteria to the fiscal and administrative realities of smaller municipalities—many of which face high climate exposure but have very limited fiscal space and technical capacity. The Ministry of Finance, the Brazilian Development Bank and other financial agencies, including regional and local, could establish a dedicated adaptation finance window for municipalities, focusing on nature-based solutions, resilient infrastructure and climate justice initiatives. The integration of adaptation criteria into fiscal transfers and public investment guidelines would also incentivise local implementation and accountability.

## 4. Enhance data accessibility and integration across scales

Enhancing data accessibility and integration across scales is essential for effective vertical integration. While the AdaptaBrasil platform provides valuable national-level information, significant gaps remain at the intra-municipal and urban levels, particularly in smaller municipalities that often lack the technical and financial capacity to generate localised data. To address this, the government should not only expand the spatial and temporal granularity of climate information but also promote the localisation of data collection and analysis, ensuring that indicators reflect neighbourhood-level vulnerabilities and urban dynamics. Beyond improving interoperability among systems, the development of cross-sectoral indicators

tors and integrated analyses is equally important. These tools would enable a holistic understanding of how climate risks intersect with sectors such as health, housing, infrastructure and civil protection. Establishing interoperable data systems that link AdaptaBrasil with sectoral databases—and aligning them with the Climate Plan (2025–35)—would enhance consistency, support evidence-based policymaking and strengthen coordination across governance levels. Collaborative partnerships with research institutions and civil society are key to maintaining continuous updates, validation and use of this localised, intersectoral data.

## 5. Promote participatory and justice-oriented adaptation governance

Brazil’s strong participatory governance tradition offers a foundation for integrating social inclusion and climate justice into adaptation processes. To leverage this, adaptation frameworks should embed participatory mechanisms—such as consultative councils, citizen observatories and participatory budgeting—within national and sub-national adaptation planning. These mechanisms must ensure representation from vulnerable groups, including women, Indigenous populations and low-income communities. Embedding participatory processes in inter-federative structures like the Federation Council would promote transparency, foster shared ownership of adaptation priorities and align decision-making with social equity objectives.

## 6. Integrate adaptation across sectors and planning instruments

To overcome persistent sectoral silos, Brazil should operationalise mechanisms that mainstream adaptation into existing planning and budgeting systems. The integration of adaptation targets into the pluriannual plans and into municipal and state climate action plans would align adaptation with development priorities. Strengthening cross-sectoral coordination among ministries and secretaries responsible for urban policy, infrastructure, agriculture and social development would ensure that adaptation is embedded in core development decisions. Moreover, expanding the use of the ‘climate federalism’ principle established by Resolution No. 3 of 2024 would create the institutional foundation for cross-sectoral alignment between mitigation and adaptation, ensuring consistency across territorial scales and policy domains.

The NAP will provide an overarching guideline for what adaptation and resilience could look like in the country, including, for example, regional priorities, capacities required, and technological barriers to address. ## Conclusion

The findings from Indonesia, Brazil and India point to a consistent conclusion: The main obstacle to making progress in adapting to climate change is no longer the absence of climate plans or policy commitments, but the persistent discrepancy between national ambition and effective implementation at the sub-national and local levels.

The comparative evidence also shows that, despite differences in political systems, fiscal structures and administrative traditions, these three large, decentralised countries face a common set of challenges:

- **Fragmented governance:** weak institutional coordination which hinders the integration of climate change adaptation
- **Capacity gaps:** different levels of understanding and advancement of climate adaptation issues among national, sub-national and local government officials
- **Finance gaps:** limited financial resources for the management of climate adaptation policies, especially at the sub-national level, due to unstandardised methodologies and data for attracting investment, which lack accountability

These—and other large, decentralised—countries could advance the vertical integration of climate adaptation by doing the following:

- Improving governance across sectors and institutions, including by increasing coordination; strengthening accountability across sectors and levels of government; and ensuring that roles, responsibilities and decision-making processes are clearly assigned and can be effectively tracked for vertical integration.
- Enhancing the capacity of government officials at different administrative levels to plan, implement, monitor and evaluate the climate adaptation programme, thereby strengthening understanding of climate adaptation issues, improving accountability across all project phases and enabling institutions to deliver on commitments and be held responsible for results.
- Strengthening data and information by harmonising risk assessment methodologies and systems to reinforce accountability through robust monitoring and evaluation of climate adaptation progress at different levels, allowing transparent tracking of adaptation progress and improving credibility to attract investment.

- Ensuring that financial mechanisms allow national funds (from both domestic and international sources such as MDBs) to be allocated and used in a traceable and accountable manner across national and sub-national levels.

This study suggests that the challenges of vertically integrating climate adaptation may be broadly similar across large, decentralised and emerging economies, as illustrated by the cases of Indonesia, Brazil and India. Across these countries, progress is constrained by unclear governance linkages and roles, limited financial flows to the sub-national level, capacity gaps at the local level and fragmented institutional coordination coupled with misaligned policies.

However, we further show that financing, data and accountability are linked and together can support the vertical integration of climate adaptation. This study builds on previous research by highlighting the commonalities among Indonesia, Brazil and India—including recommendations that could benefit all three—as well as the specific opportunities available to each country to improve vertical integration.

There is growing momentum to strengthen the vertical integration of climate adaptation in decentralised and emerging economies such as Indonesia, Brazil and India, particularly as the UNFCCC develops adaptation progress indicators that can be embedded across national and local governance systems (UNFCCC 2025). Initiatives such as CHAMP for Climate Action can help align climate finance, coordination and implementation across levels of government (WRI n.d.). With the period after COP30 seen as a critical window for action, it is essential that governments and other stakeholders take action to enhance climate resilience. Stronger vertical coordination and institutionalisation can support locally led adaptation, improve multi-sectoral governance, strengthen domestic and financial coordination and enable more effective responses to growing climate risks while ensuring adaptation efforts remain relevant to community needs.

## Appendix A. Detailed data collection methods for the three case countries

### Indonesia: iterative process of interviews and focus group discussions

The WRI Indonesia team employed desk reviews and structured interviews and discussions to formulate recommendations for advancing the vertical integration of climate adaptation in Indonesia, including the development of a vertical integration framework for climate adaptation.

Initially, the team focused on developing human-centred indicators that align climate adaptation with the country's socio-economic development agendas, such as the Climate Resilience Development Policy 2025–2045, as well as other climate commitment documents, such as its enhanced NDC. In developing this set of indicators, the team combined two human-centred development frameworks: the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development's well-being indicators and Bhutan's Gross National Happiness Index (OECD n.d.; Ura et al. 2012). Using thematic mapping approaches, the team coded the indicators into seven domains: economic; education; health; governance; social; environment; and well-being or life satisfaction. These comprised a total of 38 outcome indicators. This set of human-centred outcome indicators was tested and validated with the help of other experts as well as national and sub-national government officials. The WRI Indonesia team conducted interviews with five experts, including four academics, on 28 May and 2 June 2025. The interviewees recommended developing outcome and output indicators. These indicators were defined in line with the definitions provided by Goonesekera and Olazabal (2022): Output indicators are the immediate, attributable outputs of climate adaptation interventions, while outcome indicators are longer-term changes in behaviours, conditions or capacities.

Following the creation of the indicators, the team developed a vertical integration framework which was discussed in structured focus group discussions with national and sub-national government officials. The first series of discussions were held on 3 June 2025 with 8 national government officials, on 5 June 2025 with 10 officials from the government of Jakarta, and on 16 June 2025 with 25 officials from the government of West Sumatera. These discussions tested the set of human-centred outcome indicators on climate adaptation and emphasised the need for standardised monitoring and evaluation tools to improve the vertical integration of climate adaptation in the country. Following feedback from stakeholders, the team integrated the set of human-centred indicators into a programme management framework comprising the planning, implementation, and monitoring and evaluation stages, with feedback flowing back into the planning stage. The team also emphasised the need for two types of climate adaptation: proactive, or anticipatory, and reactive (IPCC 2001). To test the developed framework and receive feedback on it, the team conducted another series of focus group discussions with national government officials from the Ministries of National Development Planning and Environment and 20 sub-national government

officials in Jakarta on 7 August 2025 and in West Sumatera on 14 August 2025. This helped the team analyse the findings of this paper, particularly regarding barriers, challenges and opportunities, and formulate recommendations.

### Brazil: informal and unstructured data and information collection

The methodological approach adopted for the Brazilian component combined desk research with iterative qualitative engagement with federal government institutions. The desk research stage entailed a systematic review of policy documents, national strategies and institutional arrangements related to climate adaptation and multilevel governance. Insights gathered through this process informed a series of unstructured meetings held with officials from the Ministries of Environment and Cities. These meetings provided contextual understanding of how national frameworks for climate action are operationalised and how institutional responsibilities are distributed across governmental levels.

The interactions were designed to serve as exploratory dialogues rather than formal consultations, allowing for open discussion on the challenges and opportunities associated with the vertical integration of adaptation policies. Rather than seeking consensus, the objective was to identify how different administrative bodies interpret and apply coordination mechanisms in practice and to assess the extent to which national guidance translates into sub-national implementation. The flexible format of the meetings also allowed participants to share evolving institutional perspectives on Brazil's climate governance landscape.

Over the course of a year, key ideas regarding the verticalisation of adaptation actions were iteratively tested and refined through continued exchanges with government representatives and experts. This cyclical process enabled the WRI Brasil team to continuously validate their assumptions about institutional coordination, information flows and accountability mechanisms between the national and sub-national levels. The team also conducted complementary desk-based analysis of legal instruments, policy plans and national adaptation frameworks to triangulate findings and ensure methodological consistency across stages.

Together, these combined methods provided a structured yet adaptive framework for analysing vertical integration within Brazil's climate governance system. By grounding the inquiry in both documentary evidence and direct engagement with public officials, the approach ensured that conceptual developments reflected the operational realities of Brazilian institutions, offering a robust empirical foundation for cross-country comparison with India and Indonesia.

|  Coordination: policy proliferation without clear authority | New regulations such as the Environmental Protection and Management Plan, National Action Plan for Climate Change Adaptation, and village-level climate funding, alongside monitoring systems like the National Registry System, Vulnerability Index Data Information System and Village Climate Risk Index, provide strong instruments on paper.
