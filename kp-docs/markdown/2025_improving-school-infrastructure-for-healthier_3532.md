Summary 
For families across the United States, public school buildings serve as essential 
facilities for learning, athletics, meals, community gatherings, and emergency 
response—yet they suffer from decades of underinvestment, as do school 
grounds and student transportation. This pattern of underinvestment strains 
school budgets, impairs student health and academic performance, and puts 
community safety at risk. While recent federal and state investments have begun 
to address the challenges facing our K–12 public education infrastructure, much 
more is required. This expert note presents evidence and describes actions that 
Congress and governors can take to promote infrastructure investments that 
improve the health and well-being of students and generate important cost 
savings for communities. 
Introduction
Overall, the condition of the nearly 100,000 US public school facilities 
was rated a “D+” in the most recent infrastructure report card issued by 
the American Society of Civil Engineers (2021; NCES n.d.b). Persistent 
underfunding means significant gaps exist between actual school district 
spending on facilities maintenance, operations, and capital improvements 
and the full investments needed for health and safety (Filardo 2021). Aging 
buildings and inefficient heating, ventilation, and air conditioning (HVAC) 
systems are widespread (NCES 2024; US GAO 2020). In a 2019 survey, the US 
Government Accountability Office found that 41 percent of US school districts 
needed HVAC upgrades or full replacements in at least half of their schools 
(US GAO 2020). 
These inadequate building conditions deprive many children of a safe and 
healthy environment conducive to learning. Poor ventilation and indoor air 
quality at schools are related to a range of health conditions, including asthma,

and are associated with negative impacts on student attendance and academic performance (Eitland et al. n.d.; US EPA 
2015). Furthermore, by deferring needed maintenance, schools are missing out on efficiency improvements that could reap 
long-term cost savings and environmental benefits (US EPA 2011). Energy costs school districts $8 billion each year—their 
second-highest expenditure after personnel (US EPA 2011).
This pattern of underinvestment extends beyond the walls of school buildings to nearby grounds and student transportation. 
K–12 public school buildings are located on 2 million acres of land, including schoolyards, which are typically made of 
asphalt, an impervious surface that compromises storm recovery and ecological health (Filardo 2016; Trust for Public 
Land 2021). Over a third of public school students attend school in areas known as “heat islands,” with grounds that lack 
vegetation and disproportionately absorb and maintain heat, resulting in elevated and potentially unhealthy temperatures 
(Trust for Public Land 2021, 2020). Additionally, the school bus fleet of 480,000 buses is overwhelmingly powered 
by diesel and other fossil fuels, whose exhaust endangers the health of students, impacting school attendance and 
learning (WRI n.d.). 
Moreover, the effects of deferred maintenance of public school buildings, heat islands, and the legacy diesel school bus 
fleet disproportionately impact communities of color and low-income communities (Filardo 2021; Trust for Public 
Land 2021; Lazer et al. 2024). Addressing these challenges also offers an opportunity to address long-standing and 
cumulative inequities. 
Schools also need to be well maintained to fulfill their vital role as emergency response centers. Nearly 40,000 schools in 
the United States are designated as community shelters for emergency scenarios such as natural disasters, and they must be 
ready to offer safe shelter and resilient electrical infrastructure (US DOE n.d.). With the right investments and technical 
assistance, schools will be able to play this essential emergency response role for the community, while contributing to better 
academic outcomes and healthy lifestyles. 
Key policy measures and recommended actions 
Members of the 119th Congress and governors have central roles to play in advancing investments in K–12 infrastructure 
that improve the health and well-being of students, while driving cost savings for school districts. They should consider 
funding programs, policies, and technical assistance to promote clean energy and energy efficiency, green and resilient 
school grounds, electric school buses, and active transportation such as walking and cycling. Drawing on a review of 
literature and policy, as well as expert knowledge, we outline below opportunities for these leaders to protect and maximize 
recent investments while supporting policies and programs that unlock new opportunities to modernize school buildings, 
grounds, and transportation. 
Members of Congress 
The 119th Congress is well positioned to support school districts to become healthier, more resilient, and cost-effective 
through two main avenues for infrastructure investments: by protecting districts’ ability to claim energy and transportation 
tax credits and by supporting the deployment of existing funding programs.
Recommendation: Maintain support, and the original timelines, for clean energy tax credits, including the Section 
48/48E, 45W, and 30C credits, which offer opportunities to make needed investments in school infrastructure. Protect 
the elective pay provision . 
The most relevant clean energy tax credits available to school districts are being met with increasingly bipartisan support, as 
they bring significant capital investments and new jobs to states across the country. These tax credits include the following:
 ▪ The Section 48/48E Clean Electricity Investment tax credits, which defray the costs of solar energy, energy storage, and 
ground-source heat pumps.
 ▪ The Section 45W Commercial Clean Vehicle tax credit, which offers up to $40,000 per electric vehicle.

 ▪ The Section 30C Alternative Fuel Refueling Property tax credit, which provides a credit of up to 30 percent, capped at 
$100,000, for each qualified item of electric vehicle charging infrastructure (WRI 2024b, 2024a). 
These noncompetitive and unlimited credits provide opportunities for school districts urban and rural, large and small, 
to invest in healthier, more efficient technologies with lower operating costs (Levinson et al. 2025). From Utah to West 
Virginia to Florida, relevant projects are already underway in school districts across the United States, with many school 
districts and communities counting on these credits to support their projects. (UndauntedK12 n.d.). 
Previously, to monetize tax credits, entities without tax liability relied on other taxpayers to claim the credits and pass along 
savings, making projects more complicated and potentially reducing the subsidy received by the tax-exempt entity. Section 
6417 of the Internal Revenue Code expanded options for these organizations, allowing for elective payments for credits 
earned in tax years 2023 through 2032 and simplifying the process for school districts. With districts beginning to receive 
their elective payment checks at the end of 2024, momentum is building (Goldsmith et al. 2025). Members of Congress 
should ensure that this elective payment option remains in effect both for projects underway, which may be dependent on 
tax credit payments in budgeting, and for future projects across the country. They can also help raise awareness of the tax 
credits and the elective pay provision among their constituents.
Recommendation: Ensure that the remaining Clean School Bus Program funding rounds are deployed according to 
congressional intent.   
Members of Congress can also enable the deployment of funding for the Clean School Bus Program (CSBP). The CSBP 
represents a top opportunity to remove a source of daily pollution exposure for many schoolchildren: their ride to school on 
diesel-burning school buses. This program is boosting manufacturing and jobs in states across the country, and a diverse 
set of states and districts has already shown interest (Hosansky et al. 2025; Bermel et al. 2024; Zepka 2024). A five-year 
program with two remaining years of implementation, the CSBP has been consistently oversubscribed and has helped 
distribute support widely across the country, ensuring that communities in both rural and urban areas are able to make 
important vehicle updates (Zepka 2024). 
Recommendation: Work with the US Department of Transportation to ensure that remaining funds from the Safe 
Streets and Roads for All Program are deployed to support projects that promote active transportation and improve 
safety for pedestrians and cyclists, including during school commutes. 
Congress can help school districts invest in road safety and improve conditions for young people who walk and bike to 
school. The share of students commuting to school on foot and by bike dropped from 42 percent in 1969 to 10 percent in 
2022, while those relying on private vehicles more than tripled (Burgoyne-Allen and Castellanos 2022; US DOT 1972, 
2019). These shifts impact traffic congestion, road safety, and students’ opportunities for physical fitness and health. Safe 
Routes to School programming has been shown to simultaneously increase rates of walking and biking and decrease rates of 
injury and fatality (DiMaggio et al. 2016; Stewart et al. 2014). The Safe Streets and Roads for All program offers important 
opportunities to invest in sidewalks and other infrastructure, including around schools, and is not yet fully deployed (US 
DOT n.d.). Congress can ensure that these remaining funds are deployed as intended. 
Recommendation: Maintain support for the USDA Forest Service Urban and Community Forestry Program. 
The Urban and Community Forestry Program supports the cultivation of urban green spaces and trees and is a widely 
popular program, with applicants recently requesting over four times the amount of funding available. By maintaining 
support for this program, members of Congress can advance the ambitions of school districts looking to invest in schoolyard 
vegetation to combat extreme heat, particularly in communities most burdened by higher temperatures.

Governors
At the state level, governors can pursue a range of policy pathways to support school districts’ ability to make critical 
investments in infrastructure, resilience, and student health. 
Recommendations: Work with legislators and other state leaders to develop funding programs in support of school 
building efficiency and systems upgrades, solar energy, resilient and green schoolyards, vehicle electrification, and 
infrastructure that promotes walking and biking to school. 
Consider supportive policies such as requiring transitions to zero-emission vehicles for student transportation, 
offering technical assistance, and allowing power purchase agreements within the state. Prioritize underserved 
communities that are most affected by aging and unhealthy buildings and vehicles for funding and other support.
1. Building and energy efficiency. Investments in school building energy efficiency and HVAC systems provide 
meaningful opportunities for cost savings and indoor air quality improvements, which, in turn, can have a positive 
health impact for students and adults (US EPA 2015, 2011; Eitland et al. n.d.). To realize these benefits, governors 
can consider budget proposals that fund programs like Maryland’s Decarbonizing Public Schools Program, which 
includes funding for heat pumps, energy efficiency measures, as well as solar and other investments (Maryland Energy 
Administration n.d.). 
In addition, nearly 9,000 schools in the United States are powered in part by solar energy (Generation180 2024). States 
can reduce and stabilize their K–12 schools’ energy costs and promote energy independence by advancing policies and 
funding programs for solar deployments, such as Pennsylvania’s Solar for Schools grant program or Minnesota’s Solar 
for Schools program (PA Department of Community and Economic Development n.d.; Minnesota Department of 
Commerce n.d.; Generation180 2024). 
In addition to funding, governors can consider solar-supportive policies. Power purchase agreements (PPAs) are solar 
ownership models in which a third party owns a solar installation and sells the solar electricity produced by a project to 
a school district. The 29 states (plus the District of Columbia) that allow PPAs account for over 90 percent of the solar 
capacity installed at US schools (Generation180 2024). Governors can work with their legislatures to pass legislation that 
allows PPAs as a solar ownership model to expand options for school districts and others (Generation180 2024).
2. School grounds. Transforming asphalt school grounds into resilient and multipurpose spaces with tree coverage, 
playground equipment, and gardens can yield positive impacts on nearby ambient temperature, runoff and flooding, 
and student health (Trust for Public Land 2021). Governors can look to the Cal Fire Urban and Community Forestry 
Grants for an example of a state-level program that awarded nearly $7 million for green schoolyard investments in 2024 
(Cal Fire n.d.).
3. Student transportation. Governors can consider policies to ensure that students across their states benefit from electric 
school buses—which have no tailpipe emissions—by working with legislators to set a timeline for the transition of the 
state’s school bus fleet to zero-emission vehicles, as states such as Maryland, New York, and Washington have done. 
State leaders should seek out stakeholder input on timelines and other considerations to ensure ambitious, realistic, and 
equitable implementation and pair transition requirements with funding, such as New York’s $500 million allocated 
from its Environmental Bond Act, and technical assistance, such as the offerings available from the New York State 
Energy Research and Development  Authority. Both funding and technical assistance should prioritize underserved 
communities that stand to benefit the most from replacing the legacy diesel fleet. 
Governors can also work with their state departments of transportation to structure both federal and state funding for 
Safe Routes to School Programs to improve the feasibility and safety of walking and biking to school. States such as 
Kansas and Colorado are finding success with their Safe Routes to School programs by dedicating additional state or 
other funding for the program, offering technical assistance and dedicated staffing, and supporting equitable deployment 
(Johnson et al. 2024).

Recommendation: Encourage state agencies to engage state infrastructure banks to connect districts within their 
states to financial solutions. 
Governors can empower their state agencies to work with state infrastructure banks to mobilize capital from public and 
private sources for clean energy projects, including within school districts. State agencies can connect school districts with 
these banks to explore financing products for interventions that provide operational savings over time, such as renewable 
energy technologies, energy efficiency, building electrification, and zero-emission transportation. The Connecticut Green 
Bank (2024), for example, offers financing support for school bus electrification. Additionally, governors can encourage a 
review and restructuring of school district investment mechanisms overall to address barriers such as the length of contracts 
and the ability of school districts to apply operational savings to capital spending needs.
Recommendation: Empower state agencies to assume greater leadership in the procurement and deployment of health-
promoting school technologies.  
More broadly, governors can empower their state agencies to take on a stronger leadership role in centralizing procurement 
and deployment of the technologies described here. In South Carolina, for example, the Department of Education provides 
wide-ranging support to districts across the state interested in school bus electrification. This includes spearheading 
statewide applications for federal funding, procuring electric buses and charging infrastructure at the state level, offering 
standardized statewide staff training, and maintaining statewide data on school bus routes and maintenance. This 
centralized approach not only eases the administrative burden on individual school districts and promotes a unified vision 
for the state but also improves the state’s negotiating position. 
Discussion and other policy considerations 
Federal and state leaders can explore a range of policy and funding vehicles, as well as financing, to help school districts turn 
the tide on decades of deferred investments in facilities, grounds, and transportation. These long-overdue improvements will 
promote better physical health for students and school personnel, contribute to better academic outcomes, and save school 
districts critical funds. 
Taken together, the suite of investments recommended here can also boost resilience—not only for the students and staff 
attending each school but also for the entire community. By improving building efficiency; deploying technologies like solar, 
storage, and electric school buses equipped for vehicle-to-grid integration; and redesigning schoolyards to reduce flood risk 
and decrease temperatures, leaders are helping schools fulfill their vital roles as places of learning and as hubs for broader 
resilience and safety. This is critical for all communities and especially those experiencing the worst impacts of disasters. 
With millions of staff members and over 50 million students attending public schools in the United States—and their 
siblings, parents, and other caregivers who are frequent visitors—investments in school infrastructure are among the most 
highly visible (NCES n.d.a). The investments described here create broader opportunities for community engagement, 
education, and the normalization of efficient, resilient, and healthy technologies for all.
